The Talent and the Throne: Money and Power in Scripture, Catholic Tradition, the Catechism, and Canon Law

A concordance of the positive and negative witness on wealth and authority — what the Church praises, what it condemns, when seeking them is right, and the judgment Scripture attaches not only to grasping but to burying what was entrusted.

Abstract

Neither money nor power is evil; each is an entrusted good whose moral character depends on its end, its means, the attachment of the one who holds it, and its use. Scripture and the Church condemn two opposite failures, not one. The first is grasping: the love of money and the love of domination, which the Catechism names as idolatry. The second is burying: the sterile hoarding of resources and the refusal of responsibility out of fear, ease, or self-love — the failure for which the servant with one talent is judged. The page then treats the power the Holy Spirit gives, a practical rule for using money and authority (including retail trading and investment), the limits the Church sets against both abolishing and absolutizing property, authority, and nation, and eleven of the strongest objections, answered from Scripture first and from the Church's teaching in confirmation.

Core Thesis: Two Failures, One Stewardship

In brief

The Bible does not teach that money and power are evil, nor that wealth and authority prove God's favor. It teaches that both are entrusted and must be accounted for. It condemns loving them as ends, getting them unjustly, and using them to dominate — and it equally condemns hiding them away unused when they were given to serve others.

Popular readings tend to collapse the biblical witness into one of two slogans: "money is the root of all evil" or "prosperity is a sign of blessing." Neither survives the complete record. 1 Timothy 6:10 names the love of money, not money, as the root; the same chapter then instructs Christians who actually are rich how to hold their wealth (6:17–19). Romans 13 recognizes civil authority as a servant of God; Acts 5:29 places obedience to God above it. The Catechism names both money and power in its list of things a person can idolize (CCC 2113), yet also calls authority necessary to every society (CCC 1897) and calls profit necessary to business (CCC 2432).

What unifies the record is the category of stewardship: "the ownership of any property makes its holder a steward of Providence" (CCC 2404), and authority "should be exercised as a service" (CCC 2235). A steward can fail in two directions. He can treat the master's goods as his own and use them for himself — the fault of the rich fool, of Ahab, of the rulers who "lord it over" others. Or he can refuse to use them at all — the fault of the servant who buried the talent. This page sets out the evidence for both, together with the Spirit-given power that answers the servant's fear, a practical rule for using what has been entrusted, the boundaries the Church sets on either side, and the strongest objections to the whole argument.

\[\text{Grasping} \;\longleftarrow\; \textbf{Stewardship} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Burying}\]
The organizing verse
"Seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be given you besides" (Mt 6:33). Authority and resources do not vanish from the Christian life; they cease to occupy first place — and, once in second place, they are put to work.

Sources and Method

In brief

This page draws on four kinds of source, each marked with a colored label so the reader can see what kind of authority stands behind each claim. Scripture references follow the New American Bible, Revised Edition; quotations are kept short and most content is paraphrased.

Scripture
Old and New Testaments, NABRE numbering (Sirach, Wisdom, Tobit included as canonical).
Magisterium
Catechism of the Catholic Church, councils, papal encyclicals, the Compendium of the Social Doctrine.
Canon Law
1983 Code of Canon Law; Book VI as revised by Pascite gregem Dei (in force 8 Dec 2021).
Tradition
Fathers, Doctors, saints, and classical sources the tradition received (Aristotle, Dante).

Weight of sources. The sources do not carry equal authority. In descending order: Scripture and defined doctrine; ecumenical councils; the Catechism, a "sure norm" whose weight follows the sources it presents (Fidei Depositum 3); papal encyclicals, exhortations, and addresses; documents of the Roman Curia (such as Oeconomicae et pecuniariae quaestiones, 2018); teaching of episcopal conferences such as the USCCB; and the Fathers, Doctors, and theologians, whose weight depends on the Church's reception of them. The "Magisterium" label covers the second through fifth levels; the text names each document so the reader can weigh it.

Abbreviations. CCC — Catechism of the Catholic Church; can./cann. — canon(s) of the 1983 Code of Canon Law; GS — Gaudium et Spes; RN — Rerum Novarum; QA — Quadragesimo Anno; CA — Centesimus Annus; OPQ — Oeconomicae et pecuniariae quaestiones; ST — Aquinas, Summa Theologiae; SpEx — Ignatius, Spiritual Exercises. Numbers after a document name are its paragraph numbers; the sign § is reserved for this page's own sections and for canonical paragraphs (e.g., can. 1284 §2).

Structure. Part I (§§02–06) sets out the scriptural witness on power, on wealth, and on their fusion. Part II (§§07–10) moves from description to moral analysis: when seeking is right, what power the Spirit gives, what the burial of a talent incurs, and a practical rule of use. Part III (§§11–14) gathers the Church's teaching — the Fathers and Doctors, the Catechism, social doctrine, and canon law. Part IV (§§15–16) marks the boundaries the Church sets on either side and answers the strongest objections. §17 draws the synthesis.

Two interpretive rules govern the whole page. First, description is not prescription: Proverbs often reports how the world works ("the rich rule over the poor," Prov 22:7) without approving it. Second, regulation is not endorsement: a law that fences in a practice (interest, kingship, debt) may be pointing away from it. Both rules come from the Church's own reading of Scripture as a whole, in which each text is read in light of the others and of the living Tradition (CCC 111–114).[2]

03

Power — Disordered: Domination, Pride, and Absolute Claims

In brief

The same Scripture that honors good rulers condemns power used for self-exaltation, oppression, and the demand for total loyalty. The recurring progression runs from wanting to be first, to comparing oneself with others, to exalting oneself, to dominating, to injustice. Power is never absolute: God must be obeyed before any ruler.

Wealth — Legitimate: Work, Production, Prudence, Provision

In brief

Scripture approves of work, planning, saving, trade, and productive investment. Several of its heroes are wealthy, and wealth can be a gift from God. What makes wealth good is how it is earned and what it is used for: providing for one's family, helping the poor, and supporting good works.

In brief

Scripture is severe about wanting to become rich for its own sake, trusting in wealth, and earning it by cheating or exploiting others. Jesus says no one can serve both God and money. The Church calls the love of money a form of idolatry and treats profit as a necessary means, never the final goal.

Power from on High: What the Spirit Gives

In brief

Scripture speaks of another kind of power, different from office or money. The Holy Spirit gives it for witness, courage, skill, and service. It cannot be bought, earned, or seized, and it answers the fear that made the servant bury his talent.

In Acts 1:6 the disciples ask a political question: "Lord, are you at this time going to restore the kingdom to Israel?" Jesus does not grant them a kingdom. He promises power of another kind: "you will receive power when the holy Spirit comes upon you, and you will be my witnesses" (Acts 1:8). The Greek word is dynamis — capacity, strength to act — not exousia (authority, jurisdiction) or kratos (might, dominion). Christ does hold exousia, "all power in heaven and on earth" (Mt 28:18), and he had already shared exousia with the Twelve (Mt 10:1; Lk 9:1), while in Acts 1:7 exousia over times and seasons remains the Father's; what the risen Christ promises here is dynamis for witness. Among the first fruits of that power at Pentecost, after the preaching and the baptisms (Acts 2:41), is the voluntary sharing of goods (Acts 2:44–45); among its first tests is a lie about money (Acts 5:1–11); and among its first institutional uses is the choice of administrators "filled with the Spirit and wisdom" to run a food distribution (Acts 6:3).

The Buried Talent: Judgment on Not Using What Was Entrusted

In brief

Many readers remember only the warnings against greed and ambition. But Scripture and the Church also judge the opposite failure: hiding money or refusing responsibility out of fear, laziness, or self-protection, when those gifts were given to be used for others. The parable of the talents is the clearest case, and the Church's law and theology carry the same principle forward.

1. What the parable actually punishes — and a correction

In Matthew 25:14–30 a talent is an enormous sum — on the usual estimate about six thousand denarii, roughly twenty years of a laborer's wages. Two servants trade and double what they received. The third digs a hole and buries his master's money, explaining that he was afraid and thought the master a hard man (25:24–25). He is called "wicked, lazy"; his talent is taken and given to the one with ten; and he is cast into the darkness outside (25:26–30). In Luke's version (19:11–27) the servant keeps the coin wrapped in a cloth (19:20).

A common paraphrase treats "putting it in the bank" as the condemned act. The text says the opposite. In both Gospels the master names the bank as the minimum he would have accepted: the servant should at least have deposited the money with the bankers so it could come back with interest (Mt 25:27; Lk 19:23). What is condemned is sterile burial from fear — capital withdrawn from every use, bearing nothing for anyone. Saving and deposit, which Scripture elsewhere praises (Gen 41; Prov 6:6–8; 21:20), are not the fault. The fault is treating the entrusted thing as one's own to neutralize.

The minimum-return principle
The bank was the floor, not the sin. The sin was choosing no return at all — out of fear of the master, not love of him — when the gift was given to be put to work.

Two further details sharpen the reading. First, the servant's excuse is a false image of the master ("a demanding person"); fear of God misread as harshness produces paralysis rather than prudence. Second, Luke sets the parable inside a story of kingship: a nobleman goes away to receive a kingdom, and those who refused to have him reign are judged alongside the idle servant (Lk 19:12, 14, 27). Money and power are entrusted together, and both are accounted for at his return.

3. The theologians: pusillanimity, meanness, sloth

Aquinas names the buried talent as a sin of the soul that thinks too small. In ST II-II q.133 a.1, pusillanimity (faint-heartedness) is a sin because it refuses to tend to what is proportionate to one's power; his example is the servant who buried his master's money and did not trade with it out of faint-hearted fear, and was punished for it (Mt 25; Lk 19). Pusillanimity is the vice opposed by defect to magnanimity, as presumption and ambition are opposed by excess.[5]

Meanness (parvificentia) is likewise a vice (ST II-II q.135 a.1): the failure to undertake a great work proportionate to one's means because one prizes the money more than the work. Its virtue is magnificence (q.134), the virtue of great expenditure for great works. The opposite vice, waste (consumptio), spends beyond the work's worth. Both Aquinas and Aristotle before him (Nicomachean Ethics IV.2) place the virtue between these two failures.

Sloth (acedia) is a capital sin (CCC 1866), a refusal of the joy that comes from God and a slackness toward the good (CCC 2094, 2733) — the Matthean servant is called precisely "lazy."

Gregory the Great, who himself tried to flee the papacy, distinguishes two refusals of office in the Pastoral Rule. Those richly gifted to help others who refuse rule for the sake of their own quiet are culpable and risk losing the very gifts they received for others (I.5); those who decline out of humility are truly humble only if they do not resist when the divine will lays the burden on them (I.6).[7] John Chrysostom reads the talents as every power one has to help a neighbor — money, speech, protection, any ability — so that no one can plead having nothing to invest (Homily 78 on Matthew).[12]

6. The great refusal

Dante places at the entrance of Hell, outside it proper, the souls who lived "without infamy and without praise": neither heaven nor hell will have them, and they run forever after a banner. Among them is "the shade of him who made, through cowardice, the great refusal" (Inferno III.59–60), traditionally read as Celestine V, who resigned the papacy after five months. The Church's judgment differs from the poet's: the Church canonized Celestine in 1313, and canon law provides for a free and properly manifested papal resignation (can. 332 §2). The episode therefore illustrates exactly Gregory's distinction — refusal from humility and refusal from cowardice look alike from outside and are judged by their source.

7. The boundary: legitimate non-pursuit

Not every renunciation buries a talent. The Church honors voluntary poverty as an evangelical counsel (Mt 19:21; can. 600), religious renunciation of goods (can. 668), clerical simplicity of life (can. 282), clerical abstention from civil power and business without permission (cann. 285 §3, 286), and humble refusal of office (Gregory, I.6). The difference lies in the source and the destination: renunciation for God and neighbor hands the talent back to be used by others or offers it up; burial from fear or ease removes it from every use. The first is fruitful; the second is the servant's sin.

Neither Collectivism nor Anarchy — nor Their Mirror Images

In brief

If this page's warnings against greed and domination were pushed until they forbade people to earn, own, or govern, they would become something the Church condemns. The same holds for the opposite extreme. On each of three questions — property, authority, and nation — Catholic teaching rejects both the error of abolishing the good and the error of absolutizing it, and leaves a wide middle in which Catholics may disagree in good faith. The section closes with the question of the ordinary retail trader and investor, and with a reminder that none of this licenses scrutiny of a neighbor's motives.

The Church's answer
The Church permits neither extreme. A Catholic may not deny the right to own and to earn (communism), nor reject political authority or the legitimacy of nations (anarchism). The Church condemns with equal clarity the mirror images: money made absolute, the totalitarian state, and the idolatry of race or nation. The "no authority" position, moreover, is not only a position of the left; right-libertarian anarcho-capitalism denies the state as well, and receives the same answer.

G. The wide middle: where Catholics may legitimately disagree

The Council recognizes that "with equal sincerity some of the faithful will disagree with others on a given matter" (Gaudium et Spes 43), and the U.S. bishops state that "the Church is involved in the political process but is not partisan" (Faithful Citizenship 58). Within the boundaries A–F, the following are matters of prudential judgment on which faithful Catholics differ: tax rates and the design of welfare; the degree of regulation and trade policy; levels and systems of immigration; defense budgets and deterrence posture; and the balance among state, market, and civil society.

Logical note
"Warning against greed leads to communism" would be a slippery slope: this page condemns greed and communism alike. The sound form of the argument is narrower — denying that earning and owning are legitimate at all does lead to collectivism, and denying authority does lead to disorder. Pius XI adds the constructive half: the remedy for communism is justice, not denunciation alone. "There would be today neither Socialism nor Communism if the rulers of the nations had not scorned the teachings and maternal warnings of the Church" (Divini Redemptoris 38).

H. The retail trader and investorpermitted, under conditions

Trading for profit is lawful when the gain is ordered to a good end — the support of one's household, help to the needy, or the public good — or is received as the wage of one's labor (Aquinas, ST II-II q.77 a.4). The Holy See's principal document on finance teaches that "money in itself is a good instrument" (Oeconomicae et pecuniariae quaestiones 15), that the instruments markets employ "are morally permissible, provided they do not turn against the dignity of the person" (13), and that profit is "intrinsically necessary for every economic system" (23). Scripture agrees: the capable woman "considers a field and buys it" (Prov 31:16), and the master expects at least interest from the bankers (Mt 25:27).

I · A reminder

Examine yourself, not your neighbor

Nothing on this page authorizes Christians to inspect one another's motives about money or power. Every examen here is turned inward; what may be asked of others is limited to public acts done in an office, through lawful process.
What Scripture forbids
  • Judging hearts. "Do not make any judgment before the appointed time, until the Lord comes, for he will bring to light what is hidden" (1 Cor 4:5); "the LORD looks into the heart" (1 Sam 16:7); "Who are you to pass judgment on someone else's servant?" (Rom 14:4). See also Mt 7:1–5; Jas 4:11–12.
  • Prying and meddling. Paul tells Christians "to mind your own affairs" (1 Thess 4:11), rebukes those "acting like busybodies" (2 Thess 3:11), and warns against going "from house to house" as "gossips and busybodies" (1 Tim 5:13). Peter lists the "intriguer" alongside the thief (1 Pet 4:15).
  • Gossip and slander. "You shall not go about spreading slander among your people" (Lev 19:16); the talebearer betrays confidences (Prov 11:13; 20:19).
  • Uprooting too early. The servants want to pull up the weeds; the master refuses, lest they "uproot the wheat along with them" (Mt 13:29–30).
  • Inspecting others' giving. Give in secret, "and your Father who sees in secret will repay you" (Mt 6:4). What is meant to be hidden is not the congregation's to audit.
What Scripture asks, within limits
  • Fraternal correction, in order — for a known fault, not suspected motives: "go and tell him his fault between you and him alone" first (Mt 18:15–17); correct "in a gentle spirit, looking to yourself" (Gal 6:1; Lev 19:17).
  • Accountability for officeholders, through due process. An accusation against an elder requires two or three witnesses (1 Tim 5:19; Deut 19:15). Office brings scrutiny of public acts — accounts, audits, stewardship, as in Samuel's audit (1 Sam 12:3–5) and Paul's handling of the collection (2 Cor 8:20–21) — not of a person's private soul.
  • Pastoral care. Shepherds "keep watch over you" (Heb 13:17; Acts 20:28) as a shepherd tends sheep, not as an informant watches a suspect.
Confirmed byCan. 220 (no one may illegitimately harm another's good reputation or "injure the right of any person to protect his or her own privacy"); CCC 2477–2479 (rash judgment, detraction, calumny; the favorable interpretation, after Ignatius, Spiritual Exercises 22); CCC 2488–2492 (privacy and secrets); can. 1390 §2 (unlawful injury to a good name); the canonical maxim de internis non iudicat Ecclesia — the Church does not judge internal matters, which belong to the sacramental forum (can. 983).
The ruleJudge your own heart; judge officeholders' public conduct through lawful process; leave everyone else's motives to God — "remove the wooden beam from your eye first" (Mt 7:5).
16

The Strongest Objections, Answered

In brief

A position is only as strong as its answer to its best critics. Below are eleven serious objections to this page's thesis, each stated as its defenders would state it. Where an objection rests on Scripture, the reply is drawn first from Scripture read as a whole, with Church teaching as confirmation. Each reply names the logical flaw, credits what the objection gets right, and offers a constructive correction.

Format follows the scholastic articulus: objection, sed contra (on the contrary), respondeo (I answer that), then the flaw and a constructive path. The objections come from several directions — radical-Gospel, prosperity, realist, quietist, institutional-critical, market-absolutist, exegetical, consequentialist, doctrinal-development, Protestant, and both authoritarian and anarchist — and none is attributed to a named contemporary person.
Objection 1 · Radical Gospel

"The Gospel demands total renunciation; stewardship tames it."

ObjectionJesus told the rich man to sell what he had (Mk 10:21) and said, "everyone of you who does not renounce all his possessions cannot be my disciple" (Lk 14:33). The first community held all things in common (Acts 2:44–45), and Basil and Chrysostom called withheld surplus theft. "Stewardship" is a comfortable compromise.
On the contrary
  • The same call is conditional: "If you wish to be perfect" (Mt 19:21).
  • Calls differ by person: the healed man who begs to follow is told instead, "Go home to your family" (Mk 5:18–19); "each has a particular gift from God… everyone should live as the Lord has assigned" (1 Cor 7:7, 17).
  • Luke 14:33 is about priority: its parallel in Lk 14:26 ("hate father and mother") appears in Matthew as "loves father or mother more than me" (Mt 10:37); the same passage commends counting the cost (Lk 14:28–32).
  • Renunciation as inner posture: buy as though not possessing, and use the world without being absorbed in it (1 Cor 7:29–31).
  • Counter-examples inside the same canon: Zacchaeus keeps half and hears "Today salvation has come to this house" (Lk 19:8–9); women patrons give "out of their resources" (Lk 8:3); "was it not yours?" (Acts 5:4); the rich are instructed, not told to sell (1 Tim 6:17–19).
  • Confirmed by CCC 1973–1974 (precepts and counsels) and CCC 2544 (detachment required of all); Dilexi te 42 on the gravity of withholding from the poor.
I answer thatScripture distinguishes a universal precept — nothing may be preferred to Christ — from particular vocations to actual dispossession. The precept binds everyone; the counsel is given to some. Both are real, and neither may be dissolved into the other.
What it gets rightThe Fathers' charge of theft stands and is already on this page (CCC 2446): surplus withheld from real need is injustice, not stewardship.
Logical flawHasty generalization — a call to one person (the rich man) and one community's voluntary practice (Acts 2; cf. 5:4) are made into a universal law.
Constructive pathAn honest surplus audit, not a sentimental one; Ignatius's test of readiness to give up (SpEx 149–157); honor for the counsel of poverty as a real vocation — possibly one's own.
Objection 2 · Prosperity gospel

"God wants his faithful rich; prosperity is the sign of blessing."

ObjectionDeut 28:1–14 promises abundance for obedience; "it is the LORD's blessing that brings wealth" (Prov 10:22); test God with tithes and see the floodgates open (Mal 3:10); John wishes his reader to prosper (3 Jn 2). Therefore pursue wealth as evidence of faith.
On the contrary
  • Scripture names the error: those "who suppose religion to be a means of gain" (1 Tim 6:5).
  • Scripture's own protest: the wicked prosper (Ps 73:3–12; Jer 12:1; Mal 3:14–15; Hab 1:13), and the psalmist resolves it only "in the sanctuary," at the end of things (Ps 73:17).
  • Job exists to refute the equation; "time and chance" befall all (Eccl 9:11); disaster does not prove guilt (Lk 13:1–5; Jn 9:2–3).
  • Reversal: "you received what was good during your lifetime" (Lk 16:25); the faithful went destitute (Heb 11:37); Christ "became poor although he was rich" (2 Cor 8:9).
  • Wealth may not be credited to oneself: "it is he who gives you the power to acquire wealth" (Deut 8:17–18).
I answer thatDeuteronomy's blessings are covenant blessings on a nation whose law also commanded gleaning, debt release, and just wages; they cannot be detached from those duties. 3 John 2 is a standard letter greeting, not a promise.
What it gets rightWealth can truly be God's gift (§04); Scripture does not despise material blessing.
Logical flawAffirming the consequent — "if faithful, then blessed" does not yield "if prosperous, then faithful" — together with cherry-picking that omits Job, Ecclesiastes, and the Passion.
Constructive pathTreat blessing as a trust, not a scorecard: "enriched in every way for all generosity" (2 Cor 9:11). Measure faithfulness by fruit (Gal 5:22–23), not balance sheets.
Objection 3 · Political realism

"Power is domination by nature; servant leadership loses."

ObjectionThe realist tradition — Thucydides' Melian Dialogue, Machiavelli — holds that the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must; a ruler who restrains himself is replaced by one who does not. Nietzsche adds that "servant" ethics is the resentment of the weak dressed as virtue.
On the contrary
  • Scripture is not naive: it records what realists record (1 Sam 8:11–18; Eccl 4:1; Prov 22:7), then prescribes institutions — limits on the king (Deut 17), delegation (Ex 18), impartial courts (Deut 16:18–20), a state that bears the sword (Rom 13:4).
  • Service chosen from strength: "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down on my own. I have power to lay it down" (Jn 10:18); Jesus washes feet knowing "that the Father had put everything into his power" (Jn 13:3).
  • Christ is not passive: the whip in the Temple (Jn 2:15); the woes against leaders (Mt 23).
  • Realism joined to faith: "with one hand they worked and held a weapon with the other" (Neh 4:11); the king counts his troops before war (Lk 14:31).
  • Victory not by might alone: "not by sword or spear" (1 Sam 17:45–47); sovereignty passes away because of injustice (Sir 10:8).
  • Confirmed by CCC 2265, 2308–2310 (legitimate defense and deterrence); Augustine, City of God IV.4; Aquinas on magnanimity and fortitude (ST II-II qq.123, 129); Leo XIV, Message to the Pontifical Academy of Social Sciences (9 Apr 2026): legitimacy rests "on the wisdom and virtue with which it is exercised," not on accumulated strength.
I answer thatService is the mode in which strength is exercised, not its absence. Scripture's recurring judgment is that domination undermines itself (1 Kgs 12:16; Sir 10:8), because it consumes the legitimacy on which durable power rests.
What it gets rightGood intentions without power, institutions, or deterrence do fail — Scripture agrees (Prov 24:5–6; Lk 14:31–32).
Logical flawIs–ought fallacy (how power often behaves does not prove how it must behave); false dilemma (domination or helplessness); against Nietzsche, the genetic fallacy (a theory of an ethic's origin does not refute its content).
Constructive pathBuild durable power: legitimacy, rule of law, accountability, credible deterrence, and trained successors (§10). Strength ordered to justice.
Objection 4 · Quietism

"Christians should keep their hands clean of money and power."

Objection"My kingdom does not belong to this world" (Jn 18:36); "Do not love the world" (1 Jn 2:15). Politics and business corrupt; the faithful should withdraw.
On the contrary
  • Christ's own prayer: "I do not ask that you take them out of the world but that you keep them from the evil one… I sent them into the world" (Jn 17:15, 18).
  • Paul rejects withdrawal as impossible: otherwise "you would then have to leave the world" (1 Cor 5:9–10).
  • John himself uses "world" in two senses: "God so loved the world" (Jn 3:16) and "do not love the world" (1 Jn 2:15).
  • Salt and light, not a lamp under a basket (Mt 5:13–16); "seek the welfare of the city" (Jer 29:7).
  • Joseph, Daniel, Esther, Nehemiah, and Obadiah served inside pagan or corrupt governments.
  • Confirmed by Gaudium et Spes 43; Christifideles Laici 42; USCCB, Faithful Citizenship 13, 16 ("not a time for retreat"); Leo XIV, Address to the Inter-Parliamentary Union (21 Jun 2025): politics as "the highest form of charity."
I answer thatIn John, "world" (kosmos) can mean the order in rebellion against God, not creation or public life. John 18:36 speaks of the source of Christ's kingship ("not from here"), not of where Christians may act. Withdrawal from fear or ease is the buried talent (§09); renunciation for God is not (§09, subsection 7).
What it gets rightSome offices and some money are compromising; clerics are rightly restricted (cann. 285–287); Gregory honors refusal made from humility.
Logical flawEquivocation on "world"; nirvana fallacy — refusing imperfect institutions because they are imperfect, and so leaving them to worse hands (Jotham's fable, Judg 9:7–15).
Constructive pathDiscern the vocation; enter with safeguards — counsel, accountability, limits set in advance, and readiness to resign rather than sin (Thomas More).
Objection 5 · Institutional critique

"Stewardship talk is cover for religious institutions' own wealth and power."

ObjectionThe Church's record includes simony, the abuses surrounding indulgences, nepotism, and modern Vatican financial scandals. Stewardship language legitimates the rich and pacifies the poor — the Marxist "opium" critique.
On the contrary
  • The biblical root of "misuse does not abolish right use": "do and observe all things whatsoever they tell you, but do not follow their example" (Mt 23:3).
  • Scripture is the first critic of religious greed: "a den of thieves" (Jer 7:11, quoted in Mt 21:13); shepherds who feed themselves (Ezek 34); priests who teach for pay (Mic 3:11); Jason's purchase of the high priesthood (2 Macc 4:7–10).
  • A biblical model of transparency: Paul arranges the collection so no one can blame him, honorable "in the sight of others" (2 Cor 8:20–21); Samuel's public audit (1 Sam 12:3–5).
  • Confirmed by the Church's own penal law (cann. 1376–1377, 1380, 1383, 1393) and CCC 1868 (complicity through silence) and can. 1287 (public accounts).
I answer thatThe abuses happened, and reforms followed (Trent; the 1983 Code; the 2021 penal revision; curial financial reforms). But the misuse of a norm does not refute the norm (abusus non tollit usum). Nor does the critique supply a remedy: power left unexamined is not power abolished, and Pius XI warned that economic power concentrates from either direction (QA 105–112).
What it gets rightReligious language can launder power; vigilance is a duty (CCC 1868).
Logical flawTu quoque / ad hominem — hypocrisy discredits the hypocrite, not the principle; genetic fallacy — a claim about why a teaching arose does not show it false.
Constructive pathTransparency as remedy: published annual accounts (can. 1287), independent audit, penalties actually applied, lay expertise in administration (can. 1280). Rule 11 of §10: keep accounts open to scrutiny.
Objection 6 · Market absolutism

"A firm's only duty is profit; charity is private; universal destination is socialism."

ObjectionMarkets create wealth best when firms maximize profit within the law. State redistribution is coercion, and "universal destination of goods" is a euphemism for collectivism.
On the contrary
  • Obligations to the poor were law, not optional charity: gleaning (Lev 19:9–10; Deut 24:19–22), debt release (Deut 15), the tithe for the poor (Deut 14:28–29), Nehemiah's enforced restitution (Neh 5), taxes owed (Rom 13:6–7).
  • Scripture also limits coercion: the state that "will take" (1 Sam 8:11–18); "you shall not steal" (Ex 20:15); property that "was yours" (Acts 5:4); giving "not with sadness or compulsion" (2 Cor 9:7).
  • Confirmed by CCC 2402–2403 (both property and universal destination), 2424, 2432; Centesimus Annus 32–35, 42; USCCB, Economic Justice for All 24 and Faithful Citizenship 50 (wages together with property and initiative); Dilexi te 86.
I answer thatBoth halves of the tension are biblical before they are papal. The Church defends markets and condemns socialism, but a market is legitimate within a "strong juridical framework" serving the common good (CA 42). Subsidiarity limits the state; solidarity limits the individual.
What it gets rightPrivate initiative is a real source of wealth (CA 32), and an overbearing state violates subsidiarity (CCC 1883).
Logical flawFalse dilemma — absolute market or collectivism; slippery slope — any public duty leads to socialism.
Constructive pathFirms that serve the common good in fact: just wages (CCC 2434), honest dealing (CCC 2409), productive reinvestment (QA 51), voluntary generosity, and a limited, subsidiary state.
Objection 7 · Exegetical

"The talents are not about economics — or the third servant is the hero."

Objection(a) Reading economics into Matthew 25 imposes one's own ideas on the text; the parable concerns the kingdom and readiness. (b) Some scholars (W. R. Herzog, Parables as Subversive Speech, 1994) read the master as an exploitative elite who "harvests where he did not plant," making the servant who refuses to trade a resister of an unjust system.
On the contrary
  • The frame is stewardship: the faithful and the abusive steward immediately precede (Mt 24:45–51); the master "entrusted his possessions" (Mt 25:14).
  • The master himself names the bank and interest (Mt 25:27; Lk 19:23).
  • The master's identity: the faithful "share your master's joy" (Mt 25:21, 23); the Last Judgment follows directly (25:31–46); in Luke he is the returning king (19:12, 15).
  • The harsh portrait is the servant's own: "With your own words I shall condemn you" (Lk 19:22); the outcome confirms the master: "to everyone who has, more will be given" (Lk 19:26).
  • Confirmed by Chrysostom, Hom. 78 on Matthew; CCC 1936–1937; QA 51; Magnifica humanitas 9, which applies Mt 25:14–30 to scientific discoveries as "talents entrusted to humanity."
I answer thatThe primary meaning is eschatological; money is the vehicle of the image, not its point. But a commercial image presupposes that productive use is good, or it would fail as an image.
What it gets right(a) The primary reference is spiritual — see §08. (b) Real owners must not be "hard men" (Jas 5:4; Sir 34:27).
Logical flaw(a) False either/or — a parable's primary eschatological point does not exclude the moral logic of its image; (b) ignoring literary context.
Constructive pathRead at two levels: spiritual gifts first, material means by extension, always under the justice owed to workers.
Objection 8 · Consequentialism

"Get the money and power first, by whatever it takes; do good later."

ObjectionMore resources mean more good. If some corner-cutting now buys great philanthropy or reform later, the arithmetic favors it — "earn to give" or "capture the institution" logic.
On the contrary
  • A good thing done by forbidden means: Saul offers the sacrifice himself because he "felt compelled," and loses the kingdom (1 Sam 13:8–14); "obedience is better than sacrifice" (1 Sam 15:22).
  • Fast gain does not last: "wealth won quickly dwindles away" (Prov 13:11; cf. 20:21; 28:20); greed takes the life of its possessor (Prov 1:19).
  • Direct texts: "Let us do evil that good may come of it?" (Rom 3:8); "What profit is there for one to gain the whole world and forfeit his life?" (Mk 8:36).
  • Confirmed by CCC 1753–1756, 1789: "One may never do evil so that good may result from it."
I answer thatMeans form the agent: unjust acquisition corrupts the very person who is supposed to use the resources well later. The future good is uncertain; the present wrong is certain. Scripture's pattern is Saul's "I felt compelled" (1 Sam 13:12): a good end invoked to excuse a forbidden means.
What it gets rightEarning to give is biblical (Eph 4:28; Acts 20:35), and scale does multiply good.
Logical flawEnds justify the means, compounded by a calculation that ignores uncertainty — a speculative benefit weighed against a certain harm, with the agent's corruption left out.
Constructive pathEarn to give within the four arguments of §07 — end, means, attachment, use. Let integrity set the limit on growth, not become the price of it.
Objection 9 · Doctrinal development

"The Church condemned interest, then allowed it; her economic teaching follows the times."

ObjectionCouncils and popes condemned lending at interest (Lateran V; Vix Pervenit), yet Catholic institutions now hold interest-bearing assets. The teaching bends to circumstance and lacks authority.
On the contrary
  • What the law protects: the ban targets the poor kinsman in need — "the poor among you" (Ex 22:24); "your kinsman [who] becomes impoverished" (Lev 25:35).
  • Commercial lending was treated differently: interest permitted with the foreigner — commonly read as the foreign merchant (Deut 23:21); Nehemiah's crisis was a famine (Neh 5:3).
  • Supporting, though not decisive: in the parable the master names deposit with bankers as the minimum he expected (Mt 25:27; Lk 19:23) — an image that would fail if moderate interest were intrinsically unjust.
  • Confirmed by CCC 2269, 2409, and the scholastic doctrine of extrinsic titles.
I answer thatThe principle is Scripture's own: never exploit the needy; let capital be productive. When money became productive capital, lending carried real opportunity cost and risk, so the same principle yielded a different application. That is development, not contradiction.
What it gets rightThe shift was gradual and debated and should be reported completely (see the note in §05).
Logical flawEquivocation — "usury" meant any loan profit in one period and extortionate lending in another; the objection treats the change in the word as a change in doctrine.
Constructive pathApply the unchanged principle today: predatory lending, payday debt traps, and exploitative sovereign lending remain condemned; lend generously to those in genuine need (Lk 6:34–35).
Objection 10 · Grace and works

"Judgment for omission is works-righteousness; grace is free."

ObjectionWe are saved by grace through faith, "not from works" (Eph 2:8–9). Saying people are judged for failing to invest or give turns the Gospel into merit-earning.
On the contrary
  • Ephesians read to the end: "created in Christ Jesus for the good works that God has prepared" (Eph 2:10).
  • Grace and effort in one sentence: "work out your salvation… for God is the one who, for his good purpose, works in you" (Phil 2:12–13); "not I, however, but the grace of God that is with me" (1 Cor 15:10); "apart from me you can do nothing" (Jn 15:5).
  • Faith that works: "faith working through love" (Gal 5:6); "faith without works is dead" (Jas 2:14–26).
  • Judgment by deeds: Mt 7:21; 25:31–46; Rom 2:6–7; 2 Cor 5:10; Rev 20:12; the fruitless branch is taken away (Jn 15:2).
  • Confirmed by Council of Trent, Session VI (1547); CCC 2006–2011; the Catholic–Lutheran Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (1999).
I answer thatThe talents are themselves grace — given, not earned — and their fruit is the Spirit's work in us (§08). Merit in the Catholic sense is God crowning his own gifts (cf. Augustine, cited in CCC 2009). The Church rejects both works without grace and a faith that bears no fruit.
What it gets rightNo one earns salvation, and anxious activism is not the Gospel (1 Jn 4:18).
Logical flawFalse dichotomy — grace or works, when the biblical position is grace working through works; also proof-texting Eph 2:8–9 without verse 10.
Constructive pathAct from gratitude, not anxiety: the servant who trusts the master invests; the one who fears him buries.
Objection 11 · Authority

"Romans 13 demands total submission — or, conversely, all authority is illegitimate."

Objection(a) "Let every person be subordinate to the higher authorities" (Rom 13:1): to criticize or resist rulers is sin. (b) Authority is domination as such; Christians should reject hierarchy.
On the contrary
  • Romans 13 defines authority by function: a terror to bad conduct, "a servant of God for your good" (13:3–4); when power demands worship, Scripture calls it a beast (Rev 13).
  • Holy disobedience: the midwives "feared God" and did not obey Pharaoh (Ex 1:17); the three young men and Daniel refuse unjust decrees (Dan 3; 6:11); "we must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).
  • Freedom within submission: "as free people, yet without using freedom as a pretext for evil" (1 Pet 2:13–16); even the king is under the law (Deut 17:18–20).
  • Against anarchy: "everyone did what was right in their own eyes" (Judg 17:6; 21:25); prayer for rulers remains a duty (1 Tim 2:1–2).
  • Confirmed by CCC 1897, 1902–1903, 2242–2243; Leo XIV, Message to the Pontifical Academy of Social Sciences (9 Apr 2026): power "a means ordered toward the common good," with governors accountable and replaceable "through peaceful means."
I answer thatAuthority is legitimate by reason of its purpose — the common good. It binds in conscience when it serves that purpose and does not bind when it commands evil. Armed resistance is admitted only under the strict conditions of CCC 2243.
What it gets right(a) Order and respect for rulers are real goods (Rom 13:7). (b) Authority is often abused, and Scripture itself says so (§03).
Logical flaw(a) Quoting out of context against the rest of the canon; (b) composition — inferring from abuses by some authorities that authority as such is abuse.
Constructive pathObey what is lawful; refuse what is evil; reform through lawful channels — petition, vote, courts, the prophetic word (Nathan). Force only under CCC 2243.

Synthesis: The Virtue Between Grasping and Burying

In brief

Across Scripture and Catholic teaching, each good use of money or power sits between two opposite vices: one of too much, one of too little. The grasping vices get most of the attention, but the Church treats the burying vices as real sins too.

Possessions (ST II-II q.117–119)
AvariceHolding and acquiring beyond measure; the rich fool.
LiberalityUsing wealth well, especially in giving.
ProdigalityScattering goods without reason; the younger son (Lk 15:13).
Great works (ST II-II q.134–135)
MeannessDeclining the great work to spare the money; the buried talent.
MagnificenceGreat expenditure for great and useful works (QA 51).
WasteSpending beyond the work's worth for display.
Honor and office (ST II-II q.129–133)
PusillanimityRefusing what one is capable of, from fear; flight from rule for ease.
MagnanimityUndertaking great things worthy of honor, for God and neighbor.
Ambition · PresumptionSeeking honor or power beyond merit or for oneself.

Put together, the biblical and Catholic witness yields a compact rule set:

  1. Seek capability, not domination (Mk 10:42–45; CCC 2235).
  2. Seek responsibility as burden, not as appetite (1 Tim 3:1; Augustine XIX.19).
  3. Seek sufficient and productive resources, not riches as master (Eph 4:28; 1 Tim 6:9).
  4. Produce rather than exploit (Prov 31; Jas 5:4; CCC 2409).
  5. Possess without being possessed (SpEx 23; Clement).
  6. Put the entrusted to work — at the very least, never bury it (Mt 25:27; can. 1284 §2 6°; ST II-II q.133).
  7. Refuse only for God, never from fear (Gregory, Pastoral Rule I.5–6).
  8. Neither abolish nor absolutize property, authority, or nation (§15; CCC 2425; Mit brennender Sorge 8).
  9. Rely on the Spirit's power, not on might (Zech 4:6; Acts 1:8; 2 Tim 1:7).
  10. Advance in order to serve, rather than serve in order to advance (Mt 20:28).
\[\textbf{God} \;\rightarrow\; \text{wisdom, justice, love} \;\rightarrow\; \text{person} \;\rightarrow\; \text{authority + wealth} \;\rightarrow\; \text{stewardship} \;\rightarrow\; \text{work, provision, protection, generosity}\]

This page ends where Scripture ends. The twenty-four elders cast their crowns before the throne (Rev 4:10–11); the only one declared worthy "to receive power and riches, wisdom and strength, honor and glory and blessing" is the Lamb that was slain (Rev 5:12); and in the new Jerusalem "the kings of the earth will bring their treasure," and the nations their honor and glory (Rev 21:24–26). Power and wealth are not abolished at the end. They are handed back to their source, and taken up into the city.

The accounting
The master returns to settle accounts with all his servants (Mt 25:19). He asks the grasping servant about what he took, and the burying servant about what he left undone. "Well done, good and faithful servant" is spoken only to those who put what they received to work.
