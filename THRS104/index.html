Faith Before the Fork: Buber, Tillich, and Catholic Theology on the Definition of Faith

A comparative study of Martin Buber's I and Thou and Paul Tillich's Dynamics of Faith against Catholic theology on faith, revelation, mediation, symbol, doubt, and the Incarnation — with a case study on the claim, made in a university classroom, that these two texts state a faith "shared by Protestants and Catholics."

Core proposition

The differences between Catholic theology and the theologies of Buber and Tillich do not begin after the definition of faith; they begin in it. Both authors were read widely by Catholics and can be studied by Catholics without any injury to faith, but neither states a common Protestant–Catholic ground. Buber's refusal of every mediator and Tillich's redefinition of faith as ultimate concern were each written to displace the Catholic account of faith, mediation, and revelation, not to precede it.

Motivation

This page grew out of THRS 104, Mystery of God/Human Person, the core theology requirement at the University of San Francisco, which I took in a summer section in 2016 — the only one offered when I needed the credit early — and which turned out to be Protestant theology.[58] The two textbooks were Buber's I and Thou and Tillich's Dynamics of Faith: two works written to replace the Catholic account of faith and mediation, which the lecturer presented as the common ground Protestants and Catholics share. A Catholic at a Jesuit university, graded on Protestant theology: I have decided the B+ was the correct grade, and possibly a compliment. The rest I call miracles, and I do not use the word loosely.

What follows is the comparison I would have wanted on the first day.

00

Core Thesis

The order of differences
Presenting Buber and Tillich as "just the faith level, before we get to differences" reverses the actual order: the differences begin in the definition of faith itself.

Thesis question

Diagnostic question
Why can a core theology requirement at a Catholic university be satisfied by a course whose two textbooks reject the Catholic definition of faith, without any governing instrument being breached — and what does that gap do to the students routed through it?

Cue. Treat the "shared belief" claim not as a classroom aside but as a legitimacy narrative aimed at a specific audience, and test it against the instruments that actually govern the arena.

Why-questions. Which instruments define what a Catholic university owes its students in theology? Which of them reach a non-Catholic instructor? Where does the norm stop and the enforcement stop? Who benefits from the ambiguity? Is the claim sincere, legitimating, or delegitimating — and toward whom?

This page examines two books that are frequently assigned as an introduction to "faith" in a nondenominational register: Martin Buber's Ich und Du (1923), known in English as I and Thou,[1] and Paul Tillich's Dynamics of Faith (1957).[7] Each is set against Catholic theology as stated in Aquinas, the councils of Trent, Vatican I, and Vatican II, and the Catechism of the Catholic Church. The comparison is organized around the questions on which the three positions can be placed side by side: what God is, what faith is, whether revelation has content, whether anything mediates between God and the person, what a sacrament is, who Christ is, and what to make of doubt, law, and institution.

The occasion for the study is a pedagogical claim examined in Sections 09–11: that these two books state what Protestants and Catholics hold in common about faith, so that Catholic students could take a course built on them "without any problems." The analysis concludes that the claim was defensible only at the thinnest possible level and misleading at every level below it. Buber and Tillich are worth reading, and Catholic theology has engaged both with seriousness. But the shared ground the claim asserts does not exist: on the definition of faith, on mediation, and on the content of revelation, both authors take the position that Catholic theology was formulated to deny.

Scope note. Buber was a Jewish philosopher of religion and Tillich a Lutheran theologian; neither is treated here as representative of Judaism or of Protestantism as a whole. The comparison is between two named texts and the Catholic magisterial and Thomist tradition, not between confessions. Where a Catholic position is stated, it is cited to a magisterial or classical source rather than to a school opinion.
01

Buber: The Text and the Word "Thou"

The book in question is Martin Buber's I and Thou (Ich und Du, 1923).[1] Buber was a Jewish philosopher of religion, not a Christian theologian, and the "Thou" is the translator's rendering of the German intimate Du. Ronald Gregor Smith's 1937 translation chose the archaic English second-person singular to preserve the intimacy of Du as against Sie;[2] Walter Kaufmann's 1970 translation rejected "Thou" as pious and archaic and rendered the title I and You, explaining in his prologue that "Thou" had come to suggest exactly the distance Buber meant to abolish.[3] The point is the mode of address, not archaic pronouns.

His thesis: there are two primary word-pairs, I–It (the world as object, used and known) and I–Thou (the world as presence, met), and God is the Eternal Thou who can only be addressed, never possessed.[1] The primary words are not two vocabularies but two modes of the whole person's existence; the I of I–It and the I of I–Thou are not the same I.[1] The Eternal Thou is the one Thou that by its nature cannot become an It; every particular Thou is addressed through it, and it is addressed through every particular Thou.[1] Buber's account is shaped by his Hasidic sources[5] and is, at the same time, critical of any mysticism that dissolves the self into the divine, since dissolution abolishes the two terms that dialogue requires.[1]

Two rows deserve comment. On the nature of God, the Catholic tradition does not choose between the ontological and the personal. Aquinas argues that in God essence and existence are identical, so that God is subsistent being itself,[12] and that our names for God are neither univocal nor equivocal but analogical;[13] the same tradition confesses God as the one who speaks, hears, and is addressed in prayer.[21] Buber's Eternal Thou excludes the first half of this pairing on principle.[1]

On Christ, Buber's Two Types of Faith distinguishes the Hebrew emunah, trust in a person whom one already stands in relation with, from the Greek pistis, acknowledgment that something is true; he locates Jesus within the first and Paul and the Church within the second.[4] Catholic theology refuses the disjunction: Dei Verbum describes the obedience of faith as the free commitment of the whole self to God and the willing assent to the revelation he gives,[18] and the Catechism treats personal adherence and free assent to revealed truth as inseparable.[21]

03

Convergence: Catholic Personalism

Where the two converge is Catholic personalism, which was partly developed alongside or in response to Buber. Ferdinand Ebner, a Catholic, published Das Wort und die geistigen Realitäten in 1921, slightly before Buber, arguing that the human spirit is constituted in the I–Thou relation established by the word.[35] Romano Guardini's Welt und Person (1939) developed a Catholic account of the person as constituted by being addressed by God.[36] Gabriel Marcel's distinction between problem and mystery, and between having and being, runs parallel to Buber's I–It and I–Thou.[37] Above all, Karol Wojtyła's Love and Responsibility (1960) and, as John Paul II, his catecheses on the theology of the body ground sexual ethics in the personalistic norm that a person may never be treated as a mere object of use[38, 39] — a norm structurally identical to Buber's prohibition on reducing a Thou to an It.[1]

Gaudium et Spes 24, which teaches that man cannot find himself except through a sincere gift of himself, is the closest conciliar statement to Buber's dialogical anthropology.[20] Balthasar wrote the most direct Catholic engagement, Einsame Zwiesprache: Martin Buber und das Christentum (1958), which treats Buber as a serious interlocutor while arguing that the Incarnation and Trinity complete rather than contradict the dialogical principle.[41] Ratzinger's Introduction to Christianity (1968) makes the same move at the level of doctrine: the Trinitarian confession establishes relation as a basic category of being, so that the dialogical structure Buber finds between God and man already exists within God.[40]

04

The Fault Line: Mediation

The point to build any comparison around
For Buber, anything standing between I and Thou degrades the meeting; for Catholic theology, the mediator — Christ, the sacrament, the neighbor — is precisely where the meeting happens.

The sharpest fault line is mediation. Buber holds that whatever is interposed between the I and the Thou, whether image, concept, institution, or intermediary, converts the relation into an I–It relation with the intermediary.[1] Catholic theology holds the opposite as a matter of confession: there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus,[28] whose mediation the Council of Trent places at the center of justification;[27] the sacraments confer the grace they signify by the power of Christ acting in them;[22, 26] and the neighbor is the place where Christ is encountered, so that the meeting with the person is not a detour from the meeting with God but its ordinary form.[20] That is the point any comparison should be built around.

05

Tillich: The Text

The second book is Paul Tillich's Dynamics of Faith (1957).[7] Tillich was a German-American Lutheran theologian and philosopher, and this is his most accessible statement of what faith is. His thesis: faith is "the state of being ultimately concerned" — not belief in propositions with insufficient evidence, but the centered act of the whole person gripped by what concerns it unconditionally.[7] Everything else follows from that redefinition: the analysis of idolatry as misplaced ultimacy, the claim that faith speaks only in symbols, the insistence that doubt is internal to faith, and the treatment of the historical Jesus.[7] The book is a popular condensation of positions worked out systematically in the first two volumes of the Systematic Theology.[8, 9]

The first row governs the rest. For Aquinas the act of faith is "to think with assent": the intellect adheres to what God has revealed, moved to do so by the will under grace, and this assent is distinguished from opinion, which assents with fear of the opposite, and from doubt, which does not assent at all.[15] Vatican I defined faith as a supernatural virtue by which, with the help of grace, we believe that what God has revealed is true, not because its intrinsic truth is seen by natural reason but on the authority of God who reveals.[17] Vatican II added, without retracting this, that the obedience of faith is the free commitment of the whole self to God.[18] Tillich's "ultimate concern" is designed to retain the second element and discard the first.[7]

07

Convergence: Where Catholic Theology Can Meet Tillich

Where Catholic theology can meet Tillich: his critique of faith as "belief with weak evidence" is close to Aquinas's rejection of faith as mere opinion,[7, 15] and his account of idolatry as misplaced ultimate concern is nearly a commentary on Augustine's ordo amoris, the rightly ordered love that gives to each thing the measure of love its place in the order of being deserves and no more.[7, 33, 34] Rahner's "supernatural existential" — the thesis that every human being is already ordered by grace toward God as the horizon of all knowing and willing[46, 47] — and the Council's teaching on implicit faith among the unbaptized in Lumen Gentium 16[19] overlap with Tillich's claim that everyone has an ultimate concern.[7]

Catholic readers who engaged him seriously include Thomas O'Meara, Gustave Weigel, and later David Tracy.[48, 49, 50, 51] O'Meara and Weisser's Paul Tillich in Catholic Thought (1964) collected the Catholic responses of the period and included Tillich's own reply;[48] Weigel's 1950 essay in Theological Studies was among the first sustained Catholic readings;[49] Tracy adopted and revised the method of correlation in Blessed Rage for Order and The Analogical Imagination.[50, 51] Balthasar was sharply critical.[42]

08

The Fault Lines: Analogy of Being, and Doubt

The same fault line, running the other way
Buber refused mediation because it would objectify the Thou; Tillich dissolves mediation because every finite carrier of the ultimate is only a symbol. Catholic theology insists that finite things — a human nature, bread, water, a sentence in a creed — can really carry the infinite without being reduced to symbols or raised to idols.

The decisive divergence is the same one as with Buber but running the opposite direction.[1, 7] That insistence — that the finite can carry the infinite — is the analogy of being,[13, 44] and it is what Tillich, following Barth's suspicion of it, could not accept. Barth's preface to the first volume of the Church Dogmatics called the analogia entis the invention of Antichrist and the decisive reason not to become Catholic;[45] Balthasar's study of Barth is the classic Catholic reply.[43] Tillich's own ontology allows finite realities to "participate" in being-itself only symbolically, never to convey it efficaciously.[7, 8, 52]

The other point worth pressing is doubt. Tillich's most attractive claim for modern readers is that doubt belongs inside faith: because faith concerns the ultimate and the believer is finite, every act of faith carries a risk, and the acceptance of that risk is the courage of faith.[7, 11] The Catholic reply is not that believers never doubt but that doubt is the shadow of the act, not its structure: certainty rests on God who reveals, not on the believer's psychological state.[16, 17, 21] Aquinas holds that faith is more certain than science with respect to its cause, though less certain with respect to the subject's grasp;[16] Newman's Grammar of Assent argues that real assent can be unconditional even where the grounds cannot be exhaustively demonstrated;[32] and the Catechism treats voluntary doubt as a sin against faith and involuntary doubt as a difficulty to be overcome, not as faith's inner form.[21]

The Classroom Claim: A Case in Institutional Framing

The following account is the author's own recollection of a course taken at the University of San Francisco, a Jesuit Catholic university. It is a personal testimony, not a documentary record; the instructor is not named, and no claim is made about his motives beyond what the structure of the claim itself supports.

These two texts were assigned at the University of San Francisco by a Protestant instructor who claimed, at the beginning of the course, that they state the same thing as Catholic theology — that they express the common belief shared by Protestants and Catholics — so that Catholics could also take the course and should do so without any problems. The claim turned out to be a manipulation. The instructor's defense, when the point was pressed, was that the claim concerned only the level of faith and not yet "the next step." But even at this step the two theologies are already very different, as the tables in Sections 02 and 06 record line by line.

The claim was defensible only at the thinnest possible level and misleading at every level below it. Here is how the levels sort.

What the instructor could truthfully have said

Buber and Tillich are widely read by Catholics, cited in Catholic seminaries, and neither requires a denominational commitment to study.[41, 48] A Catholic can take the course without sinning against faith. That much is true and is probably what he told himself he meant.

10

Where It Becomes Manipulation

Where it becomes manipulation

"These are the same as Catholic theology" and "this is the shared belief of Protestants and Catholics" are false statements, not overstatements.

Tillich's definition of faith was written precisely to replace the Catholic one.[7, 15, 17] His "Protestant principle" is defined against Catholic sacramental and magisterial claims by name.[10] His treatment of the historical Jesus as theologically dispensable is not a neutral preliminary that Catholics happen to also hold — it is the thing the Catholic doctrine of the Incarnation exists to deny.[9, 24, 29]

Buber's rejection of any mediator between the I and the Eternal Thou is not a shared starting point either; it is the Jewish-philosophical form of exactly the objection Catholics have answered since the Council of Trent.[1, 26, 27]

Presenting these as "just the faith level, before we get to differences" reverses the actual order: the differences begin in the definition of faith itself.[7, 14, 21]

Each clause of that statement rests on a documented position. Tillich opens Dynamics of Faith by declaring that faith as "belief in something with a low degree of evidence" is a distortion to be removed, and he proposes ultimate concern as the replacement;[7] the definition he removes is, in substance, the definition Aquinas and Vatican I give.[15, 17] The essays collected in The Protestant Era define the Protestant principle as the prophetic protest against any absolute claim made for a relative reality, and name the Roman Catholic sacramental system and hierarchy as the paradigm case of such a claim.[10] The second volume of the Systematic Theology argues that faith cannot guarantee the historical existence or the name of the man Jesus, and that the certainty of faith attaches to the New Being manifest in the biblical picture rather than to the historical person;[9] the Catechism confesses that the Word became flesh in a particular man at a particular time,[24] and Paul holds that if Christ has not been raised, faith is empty.[29] Buber's refusal of mediation is stated in I and Thou itself;[1] the Council of Trent's canons on the sacraments and its decree on justification answer, under anathema, the claim that no created instrument conveys grace and that Christ's mediation is dispensable.[26, 27]

10b

The Foreclosing Formula

reconstructed This section rests on the author's recollection of the instructor's recurring classroom formula and, in one instance, of his affect, roughly ten years after the course. It is stated once, without adjectives, and no statement about the instructor's intent or inner state is made or implied.

What was observed

The instructor used a fixed sentence pattern — "…, I won't give it a thought" — to close questions from students. The author, who took the course wanting to learn what the two assigned authors meant and how their positions differed from Catholic theology, met the formula often enough that he no longer recalls the referent of "it" in any single instance; the pattern, not the occasion, is what remained. The formula was used with particular frequency in the second half of the term, after the date on which a student could no longer withdraw without a record and after the earlier date on which a student could withdraw freely. On at least one occasion, while reasoning with the author, it was delivered with visible anger.

Two cases

Whether the formula was appropriate depends on what "it" was, so two cases have to be distinguished.

If "it" was a genuinely marginal claim — a fringe reading, a conspiracy, a bad-faith objection — then "I won't give it a thought" is a legitimate, if blunt, way to say the position does not meet the threshold for class time. Teachers do this and should.

If "it" was a Catholic position — the sacraments as efficacious, the Magisterium, Christ's mediation, the content of faith — or a student's request to know what the assigned authors actually held, then the sentence is inappropriate on three separate grounds, and the emotion makes each worse rather than better.

Three grounds

Academically, it refuses the one thing a theology seminar exists to do. A position the teacher declines to engage cannot have been shown wrong; it has only been declared beneath consideration. In a core section where the syllabus has already excluded Catholic sources, dismissing the Catholic position by affect rather than argument means the students never hear the strongest form of the view the course is implicitly against. That is a failure of the discipline, not a matter of taste.

Institutionally, it runs against the norm inventoried in Section 09a. Ex corde Ecclesiae art. 4 §§3–4 asks non-Catholic teachers at a Catholic university to respect Catholic doctrine and the university's Catholic identity.[53] "I won't give it a thought," said with feeling, about a Catholic doctrine, in a required theology course, at a Jesuit university, is about as clean a counter-example to "respect" as one could construct without breaking any enforceable rule — which is exactly the gap Section 09b describes.

In the Jesuit tradition specifically, it contradicts the presupposition Ignatius places at the front of the Spiritual Exercises (§22): one should be more ready to save the other's proposition than to condemn it, and if it cannot be saved, to ask how he understands it before correcting him.[59] The Ratio Studiorum built the Jesuit classroom on disputatio, in which the opposing case is stated at its strongest before it is answered.[60] A lecturer who forecloses a position with emotion has, in that moment, abandoned both.

The deniability question

A reader may ask whether the formula was a deniable substitute for a coarser dismissal — a way of letting the audience know that the speaker did not care while keeping the words on record polite. As a reading of the rhetoric it is plausible: a stock phrase, used often, delivered with heat, that closes a topic while stating no reason, does the work of a dismissal while leaving the speaker able to say he merely declined to pursue it. That structural description — foreclosure without argument, plus deniability — is true of the formula whatever the speaker meant by it, and it is recorded here as such.

What is not recorded is any claim about what the instructor "really" wanted to say. That would be a statement about his inner state; it is not in evidence, and it would be the first sentence on this page that a hostile reader could fairly call unfair. The evidence discipline of Sections 09–10a marks motive as hypothesis and keeps it out of the findings, and this is the case it was written for. The stronger move is the one the page makes elsewhere: describe the mechanism, not the man. A reader who sees a recurring formula that ends discussion without stating a reason, delivered with visible irritation, will draw the inference without assistance, and the page stays clean.

Temper

Whether losing one's temper while arguing with a student is appropriate for a lecturer at a Jesuit university is a separate question, and it does not depend on intent. Visible anger at a student's reasoning is a professional failure in any classroom; in a theology classroom at a Jesuit university it also contradicts the two norms just cited — the Ignatian presupposition[59] and the tradition of disputation[60] — and the "respect" clause of Ex corde art. 4 §4 if the position provoking the anger was a Catholic one.[53] The caveat is the one stated at the head of this section: the affect is recollected, not documented, and is reported once.

Timing
A formula that ends questions is one thing in week two, when a student can still leave, and another after the withdrawal deadlines, when the students who remain are the audience that can no longer choose. The claim-diagnosis method in Section 10a asks about audience and effect; on the author's recollection, both changed at the point in the term when exit closed.
11

The Method and Its Name

The move described in Section 10 has a name. It is the lowest-common-denominator method: take a rich tradition, strip out what is distinctive, present the residue as "what everyone believes," and treat the stripped parts as optional add-ons. Anyone who protests looks sectarian.

The Catholic tradition has a specific word for treating the content of faith as secondary to the experience of faith. It was condemned as modernism in Pius X's encyclical Pascendi Dominici Gregis (1907), which describes the modernist account of faith as a religious sense arising from the subconscious and treats dogma as a secondary symbolic expression of that sense,[30] and in the Holy Office decree Lamentabili Sane of the same year, which condemns the propositions that dogmas are only interpretations of religious experience and that revelation is nothing but the consciousness acquired by man of his relation to God.[31] Tillich's account of faith, in which ultimate concern is primary and creed and sacrament are symbols that live and die with the concern they express, is close to a textbook instance of the position those documents describe.[7] The instructor may have been sincere, but the structure of the pitch is the same whether or not he knew it.

12

Engagement Is Not Endorsement

The sentence "Catholic readers who engaged him seriously include Thomas O'Meara, Gustave Weigel, and later David Tracy; Balthasar was sharply critical" may be read as if it were evidence that Catholic theology and Tillich's already agree at the level of faith. It is not, and the point matters because names of Catholic readers are exactly the kind of evidence a "shared ground" claim tends to lean on. O'Meara, Weigel, and Tracy engaged Tillich as an interlocutor, which is what serious Catholic theologians do with serious non-Catholic ones.[48, 49, 50] Engagement is not endorsement. The 1964 volume that O'Meara edited was organized precisely to register where Catholic thought could and could not follow Tillich, and Tillich's own afterword acknowledged the distance.[48]

Balthasar's verdict, that Tillich's system dissolves the particularity of Christ into a general ontology, is the mainstream Catholic assessment.[42] His critique of the "anthropological reduction" in Love Alone Is Credible is aimed at every method that makes human self-understanding the measure of revelation, of which Tillich's method of correlation is the most systematic modern form.[42, 8] Even Tracy, the most sympathetic, treats Tillich as a method to be corrected rather than a theology to be adopted: Blessed Rage for Order revises the method of correlation into a mutually critical one,[50] and The Analogical Imagination reinstates analogy — the very category Tillich subordinated to symbol — as the governing form of Catholic theological language.[51]

13

A Practical Test for "Shared Ground" Claims

Two questions
Ask what the author says about mediation (Christ, Church, sacraments) and about whether faith has content. If the answer is "no mediation" or "no content," the shared ground is a slogan.

This is the practical test for any future "shared ground" claim. Both criteria are drawn from the Catholic definition of faith rather than from any school within it: the one mediator is a scriptural and conciliar confession,[28, 27, 22] and the content of faith is defined at Vatican I and reaffirmed at Vatican II.[17, 18] Both Buber and Tillich fail that test on page one: Buber's opening pages establish that the Thou is met only without intermediary,[1] and Tillich's opening chapter establishes that faith is ultimate concern rather than assent to any content.[7]

Translate for action — syllabus-review checklist

For a Catholic student choosing a core theology section, five questions to put to the syllabus before enrolling:

  1. Is the confessional standpoint of the instructor and of the assigned texts disclosed?
  2. What do the assigned texts say about mediation — Christ, Church, sacraments?
  3. What do they say about whether faith has content?
  4. Are Catholic sources — Trent, Vatican I, Vatican II, the Catechism — on the syllabus at all?
  5. Which definition of faith does the grading rubric assume?

Translate for action — one institutional recommendation

Recommendation
Disclose the confessional standpoint of each core-theology section in the catalog, so that the choice the author could not make is available to others. This asks nothing of academic freedom and nothing of any instructor's conscience; it asks only that the student be told, before enrolling, what the instruments in Section 09a do not currently require anyone to tell him.
14

Triangulation: Buber, Tillich, and Catholic Theology

The two comparisons collapse into a single table on the three questions that decide whether shared ground exists: the definition of faith, mediation, and the status of the finite.

QuestionBuberTillichCatholic theology
Faith Emunah: trust in the Thou one already stands before; not assent to propositions.[4] Ultimate concern: the centered act of being grasped by the unconditional; not belief.[7] Trust and assent: commitment of the whole self to God who reveals, and adherence to what he reveals.[15, 17, 18, 21]
Mediation Refused: a mediator objectifies the Thou.[1] Dissolved: every finite carrier of the ultimate is only a symbol.[7, 52] Constitutive: Christ the one mediator; sacraments and neighbor as the place of meeting.[28, 22, 20]
The finite The It-world is necessary but cannot become sacred as It.[1] Participates in being-itself symbolically; raised to ultimacy it becomes idolatrous.[7, 8] Can really carry the infinite without becoming an idol: the analogy of being.[13, 44, 26]
Content of revelation None: Presence, not content.[1] None: ecstatic reception, expressed afterwards in symbols.[8] Deeds and words; a deposit that can be defined and guarded.[18, 17, 25]
Christ A great Jewish brother within emunah.[4] The New Being under the symbol "Jesus as the Christ"; the historical man dispensable.[9] The Word made flesh; the historical Incarnation and Resurrection constitutive.[24, 29]

Read across, the table shows why the classroom claim fails at the first level and not the second. On every row the Catholic column affirms a conjunction — trust and assent, presence and content, the finite carrying the infinite — where each of the other two columns affirms one conjunct and denies the other. A Catholic student can profitably read both books; a Catholic student told that both books state his own faith has been told something false about his own faith.
