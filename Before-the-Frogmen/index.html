Before the Frogmen: Hainan, the Taiwanese Naval Network, and the Origins of Taiwan's Navy Frogmen, 1939–1954

A reconstruction, from declassified Japanese records, ROC institutional histories, and Taiwanese oral and biographical sources, of how Japanese-trained Taiwanese naval men passed through Hainan, surrender, and the rebuilt ROC Marine Corps into the first years of Taiwan's naval frogman force — and of where the record still falls silent.

Abstract

Taiwan's naval frogmen are usually dated from 1 March 1954, when the ROC Navy's Underwater Demolition Team (海軍水中爆破隊) was formed at Zuoying with American training. This page argues that the unit had a second, older ancestry. Its first commander, 吳振武 (Wu Zhenwu), is recorded as a lieutenant (junior grade) and battalion commander (大隊長) in the Imperial Japanese Navy's 16th Guard Unit at Sanya on Hainan; after the war he recruited former Japanese naval volunteers as cadre for the rebuilt ROC Marine Corps, and he is reported to have led clandestine swimmer teams (水鬼隊, literally "water-ghost teams") on the Fujian coast before the UDT existed. At least one veteran of a formal Hainan Special Naval Landing Force, 黃圳島 (later 黃金島), followed him into the Marines. The evidence supports two continuities, personal and network, and suggests a third, in maritime and small-unit skill, that is not yet shown; it does not support a Japanese origin for UDT doctrine, which was American. Each claim is graded by the strength of its source. A disclosed section (§13) examines the author's own lineage — a grandfather who, by his own spoken account given about twenty years ago, rose from the lowest rank to platoon leader — in U.S. terms, a lieutenant's post — at the grade of a captain in a Navy SEAL platoon — in a Hainan landing force — and derives step by step that the chance the author works for the PRC is about 0.005–0.08 percent (at most roughly 1 in 1,250), and for his father 0.1 percent or less — against a baseline of 0.04–0.11 percent for an average Taiwanese adult — in round terms, about 5–80 in 100,000 for the author and 20–110 in 100,000 for his father, against 40–110 in 100,000 for an average Taiwanese. Even if either volunteered, the PRC would most likely reject him or treat the offer as a provocation (55 in 100), or use him only under tight control, tested as a possible planted double agent (40 in 100), rather than trust him (5 in 100). The page closes with the specific archival work that could convert the remaining hypotheses into named, person-level findings.

00

Core Finding: What Carried Over, and What Did Not

In brief

At least one of the people who built Taiwan's first naval frogman unit had learned soldiering and the sea in the Japanese Navy on Hainan: the man who commanded it in 1954. Others may have, but have not yet been identified. But the specialized techniques of underwater demolition came from the United States Navy. The Japanese inheritance was people and experience; the American inheritance was doctrine.

C1 · PERSONAL CONTINUITY

One man spans every stage

吳振武: IJN 16th Guard Unit, Sanya (1944–45) → leader of Taiwanese repatriation at Sanya → ROC Marine recruiter (1948) → reported frogman teams (水鬼隊) leader → first commander of the 1954 UDT.

Grade B–C B at the IJN and UDT ends; C for the links between
C2 · NETWORK CONTINUITY

Ex-IJN volunteers sought as cadre

吳 sought former first- and second-term Japanese naval volunteers as the "backbone" (骨幹) of the rebuilt Marines; 黃金島, a Hainan landing-force veteran whom 吳 is reported to have trained, joined as cadre.

Grade C 黃's cadre service: B
C3 · SKILL CONTINUITY

Sea confidence and small-unit combat

Japanese naval training and years of Hainan counter-guerrilla operations produced men fit for amphibious work. That this pool fed the early frogman (水鬼) teams person by person has not yet been shown.

Grade D (hypothesis)
D1 · DOCTRINAL DISCONTINUITY

UDT doctrine was American

The 1954 unit trained under a U.S. Navy Troop Training Team; its first instructor completed the U.S. UDT course; a U.S. UDT detachment from Yokosuka trained the first class.

Grade B
The defensible model
Japanese-trained Taiwanese maritime and landing-force human capital, plus the ROC Marine institutional framework, plus U.S. UDT technical doctrine — not "Japanese Marines became Taiwan's SEALs," and not "the Americans built Taiwan's frogmen from nothing."

Sources, Evidence Grades, and Terms

In brief

The record is thin and uneven, so every claim on this page carries a grade showing how strong its source is. Several common words — "Marines," "SEALs," "PLA" — have to be used carefully because they did not mean in 1940 what they mean now.

Grade A
Contemporary official record, or an archive's own finding aid summarizing one (JACAR, NIDS, Taiwan National Archives).
Grade B
Institutional or scholarly secondary work drawing on records (ROC Navy histories, the UDT veterans' association, peer-reviewed studies, state human-rights archives).
Grade C
Memoir, oral history, biography, or encyclopedia entry not yet matched to a document.
Grade D
Inference or hypothesis: consistent with the evidence, testable, not yet shown.

Method. An earlier exploratory survey produced a set of candidate claims. Each was then checked against the page or document it cited. Where the check failed or the source was unreachable, the claim is dropped, corrected, or marked as unverified. Three results of that checking deserve mention at the outset: 許昭榮 was recruited by 吳振武 into the Navy's technical ratings (台灣技術員兵), not the Marines; the oral-history sentence sometimes quoted placing 黃金島 and 吳振武 together at Mawei could not be found in the cited interview, whose speaker instead places 吳 at Zuoying; and the published sources disagree on whether 吳 was the UDT's first team commander (隊長) or first battalion commander (大隊長). The page follows the sources where they agree and reports the disagreement where they do not.

"Marines." Imperial Japan had no separate marine corps. The nearest equivalent was the Navy's Special Naval Landing Force (海軍特別陸戦隊, SNLF). On Hainan, the Navy's guard units (警備隊) also fought as naval infantry. A veteran who later said "I was in the Japanese 陸戰隊 (landing force)" may have meant either.

"SEALs." Taiwan's 1954 unit was a UDT — an underwater demolition team. The U.S. Navy's own UDTs were the ancestors of the SEALs, whose first two teams were established in January 1962.[18] "Taiwan's SEALs" is therefore an anachronism for 1954, though the lineage is real.

"PLA." On Hainan in 1939–45 the Communist opponent was the Qiongya (瓊崖) Communist force, later the Qiongya Column (瓊崖縱隊), which Japanese records call Communist forces (共産軍) or Communist "bandits" (共産匪). Veterans speaking decades later naturally called them "the Liberation Army." This page uses the period terms.

Names. 黃圳島 took the name 黃金島 after 1947; both are used, in period. Japanese unit abbreviations follow period usage: 横四特 (Yokosuka 4th SNLF), 佐八特 (Sasebo 8th SNLF), 舞一特 (Maizuru 1st SNLF).

Japanese Forces on Hainan: A Navy-Dominated Occupation

In brief

The 1939 invasion was mainly a Navy operation, with a small Army force in support that soon left. For the rest of the war Hainan was run mainly by the Japanese Navy, through five ground formations, each responsible for one part of the island. Knowing which part a veteran served in usually tells you which unit he belonged to.

The Taiwanese in Hainan's Naval System

In brief

Nearly 14,000 Taiwanese worked for the Japanese Navy on Hainan as civilian employees — mostly interpreters and police auxiliaries. About 800 were formal servicemen, mainly in the naval landing forces. Many started as civilians and later passed an exam to become naval volunteers on the island itself.

04

The War They Fought

In brief

For six years the Japanese naval units on Hainan fought guerrillas — Nationalist, Communist, and local. That war included the killing of villagers during sweeps. This account includes both the combat and the civilian deaths, and treats Japanese casualty numbers with caution.

The opponents. JACAR's entries for the 16th Guard Unit, Yokosuka 4th SNLF (横四特), and Sasebo 8th SNLF (佐八特) each describe the same three opponents: Chongqing's security units (重慶軍の保安団), guerrilla formations (遊撃隊), and Communist forces (共産軍).[1] A The Communist movement on Hainan, grew in the later war years: named the Qiongya Column (瓊崖縱隊) in autumn 1944, it is reported to have reached about 7,000 men and to hold nearly three-fifths of the island by 1945 — the years in which the Taiwanese volunteer cohorts served.[24] C

The operations. The island-wide sweeps were numbered Y1, Y2, and onward; landing-force platoons were regularly combined into temporary punitive columns (討伐隊) for them. A surviving series of Yokosuka 4th SNLF (横四特) battle reports (戦闘詳報) runs from December 1943 to 1945, with sections recording claimed results, Japanese losses, ammunition expended, and number of sweeps.[8] A (finding aid)

Civilian deaths. The Japanese record cannot be read as a count of combatants. The economist and historian 斉藤日出治 (Saitō Hideharu) has examined the Hainan Guard District's war diaries against local testimony and argues that massacres of residents were carried out alongside the Y operations.[9] In a later project he matched villagers' accounts of a 1945 killing at Longwei New Village (龍衛新村) in western Hainan against Yokosuka 4th SNLF (横四特)'s own battle report No. 5 of 15 April 1945, "The Battle of Longwei New Village, 2 March 1945."[10] B

On numbers
Japanese categories such as "abandoned corpses" mixed armed opponents of every allegiance with civilians killed in sweeps. Large island-wide totals reported in secondary literature cannot be divided among units, and no Japanese figure should be restated as "Communist soldiers killed" — let alone as any one man's tally — without event-by-event corroboration.
A commonly cited total of 24,387 "abandoned corpses" for February 1939–July 1942, with monthly peaks in 1941, is attributed to Saitō's analysis of the war diaries. The verification for this page could reach the paper's bibliographic record and abstract but not its tables, so the figure is reported here only as a lead to be checked. It ends in July 1942, before the first Taiwanese naval volunteer cohort existed; Taiwanese civilian employees, including interpreters and police auxiliaries, were present throughout that period.

How a Taiwanese held officer rank. Three discrepancies are preserved rather than resolved. (3) A claim that 吳 was the Marine battalion's "chief of staff" sent to Taiwan, and later commanded the 1st Regiment's 2nd Battalion, comes from the History of the ROC Marine Corps, which could not be reached for verification.

The Rebuilt Marines and the Ex-IJN Cadre

In brief

The ROC Marine Corps was rebuilt in 1947. 吳振武, who had joined the ROC Navy that March, was writing to former Japanese naval volunteers by July, and was recruiting at Zuoying by late 1948, seeking men the Japanese Navy had already trained. One of them was 黃金島, a Hainan landing-force veteran whom 吳 is said to have trained during the war. He served as a Marine cadre and was arrested in 1952, by then at the Armored Forces School.

Frogmen (水鬼, "Water Ghosts") Before the Name: The Undocumented Years

In brief

Before 1954 there were already swimmer teams raiding the mainland coast, and 吳振武 is reported to have led some of them. Almost nothing about who these men were has been published. This is where former Japanese naval men are most likely to be found — and where the evidence is weakest.

What is reported. The only published statement located for this page is the Wikipedia line that 吳振武 "repeatedly led frogman teams (水鬼隊) on important intelligence and demolition missions along the Fujian coast" during 胡宗南's command of the Southeast Anti-Communist National Salvation Army.[11] C The attribution conflicts with 胡宗南's own Wikipedia article, which gives his command (1951–53, under the alias 秦東昌) as the Jiangsu–Zhejiang Anti-Communist National Salvation Army (江浙反共救國軍) on the Dachen islands off Zhejiang; the Fujian-coast guerrilla forces were under 胡璉 at Kinmen, and the only "Southeast" body on record, 黃炳炎's Southeast People's Anti-Communist National Salvation Army (東南人民反共救國軍), was merged into those forces in 1950.[30] Either the commander or the coast is probably wrong. If the account is confirmed from operational records, it would place clandestine swimmer operations under an ex-IJN officer before the American UDT curriculum arrived.

The obvious question. Where would such a commander have found swimmers, boat handlers, and men used to small-unit fighting? His documented recruiting network — former Japanese naval volunteers sought as Marine cadre — is a plausible source. D No roster of these early teams has been located.

Why the record is thin. Clandestine coastal operations in the early 1950s were among the least documented activities of the period, and many records remain unpublished or unindexed in ROC military archives. There may also be a cultural reason. The Koyanagi interviews — some 4,000 pages in 44 volumes, recorded with 47 senior Imperial Navy officers between 1956 and 1961 — were kept confidential until 2010, one sign of how guarded that navy's record remained.[20] Taiwanese veterans of that navy, who after 1945 served a different state and lived through 228 and the White Terror, had further reasons to say little. 張子涇's Hainan memoir appeared only in 2017.

Silence explains why evidence may be missing; it does not supply the missing evidence. What can be said is that many stories remain unpublished, unstudied, or held only in private memory — not that classified files prove a particular lineage.

The Author's Case: Lineage and Counterintelligence

In brief

The author is the grandson of the Erlin veteran in §12. This section asks whether that ancestry — a Taiwanese who rose from the lowest rank to platoon leader (in U.S. terms, a lieutenant's post — at the grade of a captain in a Navy SEAL platoon) in a Japanese naval landing force and fought the Qiongya (瓊崖) Communist forces — bears on whether his son or grandson could be trusted or recruited by the PRC, and whether suspecting them on that basis would be reasonable. The estimates are the author's own analytic judgments; subsection D sets out how each was derived, and subsection E compares them with an average Taiwanese adult, so that readers can check them.

Disclosure. The author has a personal interest in this question. The method is therefore stated so that the conclusions do not rest on his word: base rates of espionage, how PRC recruitment works in practice, and how PRC political review (政審) treats family history.

C. Reasoning

  • Base rate. Taiwan prosecuted 64 people for PRC espionage in 2024, in a population of about 23 million — on the order of 0.0003 percent a year.[33] The lineage does not raise that rate; its likelihood ratio is about 0.5 to 1.
  • How recruitment works. Documented Taiwanese cases turn on money or debt, compromise, grievance, religious networks, business ties, and recruitment by already-compromised colleagues — not ancestry.[33] A hostile service seeks access and vulnerability. Descent from a man who fought the Communist forces signals the opposite of ideological sympathy, and would make a volunteer look like a planted double agent.
  • No grievance to exploit. The grandfather's line was not affected by the 228 Incident or the White Terror, so no grievance against the ROC state exists on that side.
  • Proper practice. Security vetting in Taiwan and the United States examines current foreign contacts, finances, travel, and conduct. A grandfather's service in the Second World War is not a foreign-influence factor.
  • Taiwan's own history. 吳振武 was detained for about six months on suspicion arising from his past and was found not guilty (§06). The early ROC state's distrust of Japanese-trained Taiwanese produced exactly this kind of false suspicion.

D. How the probabilities were derived

Each estimate (1) defines a reference class of comparable cases, (2) anchors on the best available data, (3) adjusts with explicit multipliers or likelihood ratios, each given as a range, and (4) reports the result as a range. Where no data exist, the input is marked as a judgment. Bayesian updating is used in its simplest form: evidence multiplies the prior odds by its likelihood ratio (LR), that is, by how much more likely the evidence is if a hypothesis is true than if it is false. Downward: a lineage of anti-Communist combat makes a person look like a planted double agent and signals no ideological fit (×0.8–0.9). No documented Taiwanese espionage case turns on ancestry; targeting turns on access. Net about ×1.

D8. If one of them volunteered, trusted as an asset (5, against 10). Walk-ins are used, with heavy testing, in perhaps half of cases and trusted with significant tasking in about 10 percent (judgment from intelligence practice). The lineage doubles the suspicion of a planted double agent (LR ≈ 2 against trust): 10 ÷ 2 = 5. The same shift moves probability from use toward rejection: for an identical volunteer without this history, used under tight control about 50 and rejected about 40; with it, used under tight control about 40 and rejected about 55 (the three outcomes sum to 100).

D9. Working for the PRC. Base rate: 64 prosecutions a year; if 10–30 percent of agents are caught, 210–640 new agents a year, or 8,500–25,600 over a 40-year adult life — about 0.04–0.11 percent of 23.4 million. Likelihood ratios: the lineage, 0.5–1 (no ideological fit; looks like a planted double agent); no 228 or White Terror grievance in the paternal line, about 1; for the author, a public research record critical of the CCP, a further 0.3–0.7. Father: 0.04–0.11 percent × 0.5–1 ≈ 0.02–0.11 percent. Author: × 0.3–0.7 ≈ 0.005–0.08 percent.

D10–D11. A counterintelligence case opened, or suspicion held reasonable, on lineage alone (< 5 each). If lineage were used as a flag, at most about 1 in 1,000 of those flagged would be agents (D9) — more than 999 in 1,000 would be false positives. Counterintelligence practice and security adjudication (in the United States, Security Executive Agent Directive 4) require specific, articulable indicators, and ancestry is not one. The residual below 5 covers analyst error or bias, not a justified inference.

Conclusion of this section
On the evidence, this ancestry makes PRC trust in the son or grandson less likely, not more, and it gives no ground for suspicion. What would legitimately raise concern about anyone — unexplained money, undisclosed contacts with PRC officials, pressure applied through relatives, reaching for information beyond one's role — is conduct, and conduct is not inherited.

Conclusion

Taiwan's naval frogmen did not begin from nothing in 1954. The officer who commanded them is recorded as a lieutenant (jg) and battalion commander (中尉大隊長) in the Japanese Navy's 16th Guard Unit at Sanya, is reported to have led the repatriation of Taiwanese servicemen there, and, as a Marine recruiter, deliberately sought former Japanese naval volunteers as cadre. At least one veteran of a formal Hainan landing force followed him into the Marines. That much is supported, at the grades shown.

What is not yet supported is the stronger claim that Japanese naval technique formed the basis of Taiwan's UDT, or that any other named Hainan veteran became a founding frogman. The specialized doctrine of 1954 was American, and the founding roster's earlier lives remain unwritten.

The record is thin, probably in part because the men said little, and certainly because the files have not yet been read. The second can change. The roster of 22, the early swimmer teams, and the Hainan name lists are finite and searchable, and they are where the open questions can be settled.

Summary
People and experience from Hainan; institution from the rebuilt Marines; doctrine from the United States. The first is documented for one man and plausible for more; the second and third are documented.
