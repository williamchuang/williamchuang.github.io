The Hidden Founding: Catholic Agency and Irony in the Making of the United States, 1048–2025

A revolution that denounced Catholic toleration was won with Catholic money, ships and soldiers. The first American bishop owed his appointment to a Protestant deist he had attended through illness. A religious order that Rome had abolished survived in the open in Maryland because the new republic disclaimed all authority over religion. This page documents that record in one strictly chronological sequence, built from public instruments — bulls, charters, statutes, treaties and letters.

Abstract

This page documents the Catholic contribution to the founding of the United States — decisive, largely absent from the national narrative, and repeatedly ironic. A revolution that denounced Catholic toleration was won with Catholic money, ships and soldiers. The first American bishop owed his appointment to a Protestant deist he had attended through illness. A religious order that Rome had abolished survived in the open in Maryland, and under Russian protection, because the new republic disclaimed all authority over religion. The analytic spine is a licensing model — piracy, privateering and chartered monopoly as three states of one armed commercial system, and papal bulls, royal charters and letters of marque as instruments of the same kind — which explains the whole sequence without appeal to hidden agency. The documented record is set out where it belongs: Franklin's dealings with the Company and with Rome in §15, the Carroll land cession in the timeline for 1791, and the causes of the Jesuit suppression and restoration in §13.

The Priest and the Deist

In the late spring of 1776 a man of seventy lay ill on the road south from Montreal. The mission had failed completely: the Canadians would not join the American union, and the reason was on paper in two documents Congress had issued weeks apart — one denouncing the Quebec Act for tolerating Catholicism in the harshest terms available, the other inviting Catholic Canadians to make common cause. They had read both.

Benjamin Franklin, the most celebrated American alive, fell ill on the retreat. The man who attended him through it and brought him back to Philadelphia was Father John Carroll — a Jesuit, brought on the mission to lend it Catholic credibility.

So in the spring of 1776 Carroll was a man who had spent two-thirds of his life in Europe — schooled in French Flanders, ordained at Liège in the Holy Roman Empire, a teacher at Bruges in the Austrian Netherlands, a traveller across the continent as tutor to an English peer's son, and finally a chaplain in Wiltshire — and who had been home about two years. Congress sent him to Canada with Franklin, Chase and his cousin Charles.

Carroll had left Maryland at thirteen for the Jesuit college at Saint-Omer in French Flanders, entered the Society at eighteen, studied at Liège — a prince-bishopric of the Holy Roman Empire — and was ordained there on 14 February 1761. He taught at the English college at Bruges in the Austrian Netherlands, then travelled Europe from 1771 as tutor to Charles-Philippe, son of Lord Stourton, and served as chaplain to Baron Arundell in Wiltshire before sailing home. Twenty-six years abroad, ended when the suppression sent him back to a colony where his religion was a crime, by way of a kingdom where the statute of 1585 remained unrepealed.[28]

He was a Jesuit of a particular kind: one with no Society left to belong to. Three years earlier Clement XIV had abolished the Society of Jesus worldwide, dispersing some 23,000 men and closing roughly 800 colleges, and the abolition had been extracted by the Catholic crowns themselves — Portugal expelled the Jesuits in 1759, France in 1764, Spain, Naples and Parma in 1767 and 1768. By 1776 there was no Catholic country in Europe where Carroll could have served openly as a Jesuit. The order survived as an order in two places, and neither was Catholic: Orthodox Russia, where Catherine II refused to publish the brief, and for a time Protestant Prussia. Elsewhere the men survived without it — in Maryland, and in Beijing, where Jesuits had served the throne since Ricci reached the capital in 1601 and simply went on doing so.

The scale on the American side was smaller than the word “mission” suggests. Reporting to Rome in February 1785, Carroll counted nineteen priests in Maryland — nearly all of them former Jesuits, the same men who had held the plantations since the 1630s.[27] That was the entire Catholic clergy of the colony which had just supplied the only Catholic signer of the Declaration, and would shortly supply two signers of the Constitution and the first bishop in the United States.

So the priest at Franklin's bedside in 1776 came from a colony where saying Mass in public was a crime and Catholics could not vote, hold office, practise law, or run a school — and from an order that the Catholic crowns of Europe had just procured the abolition of.

Eight years later, in Paris, Rome asked the American government how the Catholics of the new republic should be governed. Congress answered in May 1784 that it held no authority whatever over religion and could neither approve nor object. The reply was meant as constitutional principle. Its unintended effect was to leave Franklin's private opinion as the only American input Rome possessed — and he recommended the priest who had attended him. The care came first, and the appointment followed from it by eight years. On 6 June 1784 John Carroll was named Superior of the Mission. In 1789 he became the first Catholic bishop in the United States, with jurisdiction from the Atlantic to the Mississippi, and founded Georgetown.

The shape of the whole story
The first head of the Catholic Church in America was, in practice, chosen by a Protestant deist, because a legislature had declined to have a religious policy.

This page is an attempt to document that pattern at length. Four ironies recur.

IRONY I

The republic was rescued by the powers it had denounced

France supplied the navy, the subsidies and the loans; Spain took the Gulf; Havana raised the money for Yorktown in a matter of hours; de Grasse, trained in the galleys of the Order of Malta, closed the Chesapeake. A revolution whose founding grievances included Catholic toleration was decided by Catholic monarchies.

IRONY II

Each shaped the other's institutions

Franklin's recommendation governed the American church for a generation; Charles Carroll, barred from voting in his own colony, signed the Declaration with his address attached; Daniel Carroll and Thomas FitzSimons signed the Constitution.

IRONY III

A suppressed order survived here in the open

From 1773 to 1814 the Society of Jesus did not canonically exist. In Maryland its former members held roughly 12,000 acres under a state charter, ran a college, and from 1805 lived under the Russian province — because an Orthodox empress had refused to publish a papal brief, and because a republic that could not establish a religion could not abolish one either.

IRONY IV

The machinery of revolution was built to police piracy

Vice-admiralty jurisdiction without juries entered colonial life in 1698 as a remedy in the East India Company's interest. Seventy years later it was a grievance in the Declaration.

None of this requires a hidden hand. Every link is a public instrument — a bull, a charter, a statute, a treaty, a letter — and the documentary record is stranger than the conspiratorial one. The concealment is not in the events but in the telling: these facts were not suppressed, they were simply left out of the national story, and the point of the timeline that follows is to put them back in one sequence where they can be checked.

01

Core Finding: Licence, Not Conspiracy

In brief

Four institutions that look unrelated — the knightly orders, the Jesuits, the East India Company, and the American founding — are joined by a single mechanism. Each depended on a licence granted by a sovereign: a papal bull, a royal charter, a letter of marque. When the licensing power turned against them, the ones that survived did so by moving the licence somewhere else. The ones that could not move it disappeared.

M1 · TEMPLAR ASSETS

Relocated into a crown order

Suppressed in 1312, the Temple's Iberian property and personnel passed in 1317 and 1319 to Montesa and the Order of Christ — royal creations under crown administration from 1523. Prince Henry's voyages were funded by those revenues. No surviving fleet, no hidden succession.

Grade A
M2 · THE SOCIETY OF JESUS

Relocated into an Orthodox empire

Suppressed worldwide in 1773 by a brief extracted by Catholic crowns, the Society continued at Polotsk because Catherine II declined to promulgate it, with superiors and a novitiate, for forty-one years. Restoration in 1814 extended a living body rather than reviving a dead one.

Grade A
M3 · THE ORDER OF ST JOHN

Surrendered territory, retained sovereignty

Stripped of its French commanderies in 1792 and of Malta in 1798, rescued uncanonically by Paul I and left stateless in 1801, the Order settled in Rome in 1834 and had its Grand Mastership restored in 1879. It holds extraterritorial premises — Palazzo Malta and the Villa del Priorato on the Aventine, and Fort St Angelo since 1998 — but no sovereign territory, and it never recovered the island. Rome therefore contains two sovereign subjects of different legal species, neither with jurisdiction over the other.

Grade A
M4 · THE CHARTERED COMPANIES

None survived as itself

The East India Company lost its monopolies in 1813 and 1833, ceased trading in 1834, surrendered government to the Crown in 1858, and was dissolved by statute in 1874; its trade passed to private firms. The VOC's charter was not renewed after 1799 and its debts and possessions passed to the Batavian Republic, becoming the Netherlands East Indies. The WIC failed in 1674, was re-chartered in 1675, and saw that charter lapse in 1791–92. In each case a state absorbed the function; in none did the corporation continue.

Grade A
The defensible model
Piracy, privateering and chartered monopoly were three licensing states of one armed commercial system, and the boundary between them was drawn by whoever held the pen. Institutions outlive the states that suppress them by moving the licence; institutions with no principle beyond extraction do not outlive the charter.

Terms. "Suppression" means the canonical act of 1773 (Dominus ac Redemptor), not expulsion by a state, which is what Portugal did in 1759, France in 1764, and Spain in 1767. "Country traders" were private merchants licensed by the Company to carry cargoes it would not carry itself. "Privateer" denotes a commerce raider holding a commission; "pirate" denotes one who does not, and much of Part II concerns who held the power to make that distinction.

On the record's hard parts. Omitting them would misstate how these institutions actually worked.
03

The Licence Thesis

In brief

The East India Company's charter granted it the right to make war, sign treaties, coin money and take prizes. A pirate did the same things without a charter. The difference was legal, not behavioral — which is why the same act could be a crime, a public service, or an act of state depending on who was describing it.

The chartered raider. The Company chartered on 31 December 1600 was not a purely commercial body. James Lancaster, who commanded the first voyage, had been a privateer against Spain. Between 1686 and 1690, under Sir Josiah Child, the Company made open war on the Mughal Empire, seizing Mughal shipping and preying on Red Sea pilgrim traffic — corporately, under charter, what Henry Every's men would be hanged for doing privately five years later.

The boundary made legal. Every's capture of the Ganj-i-Sawai on 7 September 1695 brought Aurangzeb to close the Company's factories at Surat, Bombay, Broach, Agra and Ahmedabad and imprison its officers. The Company's losses were real and its disavowal sincere; what the episode shows is that the most informed commercial court in Asia treated English pirates and the English company as one body. The Company's answer was to make the distinction explicit and to police it: convoy escorts, a doubled bounty, and the piracy acts of 1698 and 1700 allowing vice-admiralty trials in the colonies without juries.

The boundary aimed. Once it existed it could be used. Before 1698 the Company prosecuted lawful English interlopers as pirates. Kanhoji Angre held a commission from his own state and was called a pirate for taxing Company shipping. The Qawasim of the Gulf were given a coast named for the accusation and campaigned against in 1809 and 1819, on a charge that scholarship — including al-Qasimi's — argues was manufactured to remove a commercial rival.

Absorption rather than extinction. Morgan became Lieutenant Governor of Jamaica; Woodes Rogers, a privateer, became Governor of the Bahamas and suppressed Nassau in 1718; the amnesties of 1698 and 1717 returned pirate crews to naval and Company service. Piracy did not end in the 1720s so much as it was licensed, and the licensing coincided with the consolidation of chartered trade.

UnlicensedPiracy. Hanged when caught; pardoned in general amnesties; fenced through colonial ports.
CommissionedPrivateering. The same acts under a letter of marque; the American republic's first instrument at sea.
CharteredMonopoly. War, treaty, coinage and prize-taking granted corporately, with an army and a revenue.

Bengal to Baltimore: The Fiscal Chain

In brief

The first Catholic bishop of the United States owed his appointment, through five documented links, to a land-revenue settlement made in Bengal in 1765 by men who had never heard of him. Every link is a public instrument. No hidden agency is required, and that is the point.

LINK 1 · REVENUE

Bengal, 1765–1770

The diwani makes the Company a revenue state; dividends are raised against an asset that the famine of 1769–70 destroys while collection continues.

LINK 2 · INSOLVENCY

Grade A
RESULT

Baltimore, 1789

A suppressed Jesuit becomes first bishop of a diocese running to the Mississippi, and founds Georgetown, on a Protestant deist's recommendation accepted by Rome.

Grade A

On causation. Every link is a documented, public, institutional act: a revenue grant, a bankruptcy, a statute, a treaty, a letter. The chain is astonishing in its span and unremarkable in its mechanics. When a story of this shape is available, the burden on any hidden-agency account rises sharply — it must explain what the ordinary account leaves unexplained, and here very little remains.

On religious liberty. The American separation of church and state was not, in 1784, primarily an idea that triumphed. It was a legislature's confession of incapacity, and that incapacity produced immediately an American church that Rome governed without a state intermediary. John Carroll grasped the implication: a government that cannot establish a religion cannot suppress one either.

What the argument does not claim. It does not claim that British India policy was designed to produce any American outcome; it claims the opposite. It does not treat Catholic contribution as a hidden hand — French and Spanish intervention was public, treaty-bound and self-interested. And it does not require the Bengal famine to be read as intentional; the culpability lies in an extraction structure that continued through it.

Where this is weakest. Link 5 is the joint to press. Franklin's recommendation is attested in his own journal and in the sequence of Roman appointments, but the internal deliberation at the nunciature is not in evidence here; if Rome had settled on Carroll independently, the chain's final step becomes correlation. The nunciature correspondence would decide it.

Suppression and Restoration: A Causal Decomposition

In brief

The Society was suppressed by Catholic kings, preserved by an Orthodox empress, given political room by a man who imprisoned the pope, and restored by that pope three months after his release. Each of those four statements is documented, and together they explain 1814 without appeal to any hidden actor.

Who suppressed it

The agent was a coalition of Catholic crowns, not a doctrinal judgment. Braganza Portugal initiated in 1759; the Bourbon courts of France (1764), Spain (1767), Naples and Sicily (1767) and Parma (1768) followed, bound by the Pacte de Famille of 1761 and acting jointly from 1767. They pressed the 1769 conclave; the Spanish ambassador Floridablanca extracted the brief in 1773, with France holding Avignon and Naples holding Benevento as leverage and the threat of national schism explicit. The motive was regalist rather than theological: these governments wanted appointments, revenues and education under royal control, and the Society's fourth vow, independent colleges and Paraguay reductions made it the one body that could not be nationalized.

What made restoration possible

The corrected formulation
Napoleon is the efficient cause — he cleared the obstacle and supplied the motive, by failing. Russia is the material cause — it preserved the body to be restored. Pius VII's captivity fixes the date. Carroll caused the American province, not the restoration of the Society.
A note on the Order of Malta, which followed a different mechanism. Its territorial restoration was provided for at Amiens in 1802 and never carried out; Britain refused to evacuate and the 1814 Treaty of Paris confirmed Malta as British. Its institutional recovery was papal and late: Rome in 1834, the Grand Mastership in 1879. The United States played no part in either, and its use of Valletta as a naval base after 1801 amounted to accepting the dispossession. The Society survived by relocating its canonical existence; the Order survived by giving up territory and keeping sovereignty.

Three modes of survival

The brief of 1773 abolished the Society. It did not remove the men, and what carried them forward differed by place.

Corporate, in Russia. Catherine II declined to promulgate the brief in the newly annexed Belarusian territories, and Polotsk kept superiors, a novitiate and continuous formation for forty-one years. Of the three modes this is the only one that could be extended rather than restarted: in 1814 the restoration enlarged a body that had continued without interruption.

Proprietary, in Maryland. No canonical order existed here from 1773 to 1805. The men remained, and in 1792 a Maryland charter — the Corporation of Roman Catholic Clergymen — took title to roughly 12,000 acres. Property law held what canon law had released. In 1805 the same men affiliated to the Russian province and were Jesuits again, and Georgetown, founded during the suppression by priests belonging to nothing, passed to the restored Society in 1820.

Personal, in Beijing. Neither an order nor a property vehicle. When the brief reached the capital in 1775 the men submitted to it and went on in their posts — running the Imperial Astronomical Bureau, surveying, painting, casting artillery — as individual priests serving the Qing throne. The work outlived the institution because the men were still there and still needed. The mission passed to the Lazarists in 1785, and Jesuits returned to China under a different licence, treaty-port protection, in 1842.

The Hidden Instrument: Catholic Care, 1776 and 1938

In brief

Unconditional medical care given to religious outsiders did more to dissolve American anti-Catholicism than any argument. Two cases bracket the whole arc: a deist attended by a Jesuit in 1776, when Maryland Catholics were barred from office, and a Protestant boy nursed by sisters in 1938, when their hospitals were part of the civic furniture of a midwestern city.

The most effective Catholic instrument in American history was not argument, money or political office. It was medical care given without condition to people who had every social reason to distrust the giver — and it worked on individuals decades before it worked on the public.

This is the Hospitaller charism, traceable to the Amalfitan hospital of the 1040s with which this timeline opens, and it is the one thing the American record shows working continuously from the Revolution to the present. Two cases bracket the arc, and the inversion between them is the finding.

Foreign Principals: The Catholic Powers and the Charge of Divided Loyalty

In brief

The intervention that decided the war was Catholic, foreign, and in part deliberately concealed. The same structural fact — that Catholics answered to authorities outside the United States — was the standing charge against American Catholics from 1678 to 1960. This section sets out what the Catholic powers supplied, how they supplied it, and what the arrangement looks like under the categories a later American statute would create.

What was supplied

Money, before the alliance. In 1776 Louis XVI authorized one million livres in secret aid, and Spain matched it. The funds were routed through Roderigue Hortalez et Cie, a trading house established by Pierre-Augustin Caron de Beaumarchais in June 1776 for the purpose. Foreign powers not yet at war with Britain supplied a belligerent through a commercial front, and the great majority of the gunpowder available to American forces in the first years of the war came from abroad, overwhelmingly from French sources.

Alliance, from February 1778. The Treaty of Amity and Commerce and the Treaty of Alliance brought France into the war openly. French loans and subsidies to Congress ran to tens of millions of livres, and French war expenditure on the American account ran to considerably more.

Armies and fleets. Rochambeau landed roughly 5,500 troops at Newport in July 1780. At Yorktown in 1781 French troops were approximately half the besieging force. De Grasse's fleet closed the Chesapeake on 5 September 1781, the condition that made the siege possible; de Grasse had trained in the galleys of the Order of Malta and was a Knight of the Order.

The Gulf. Spain declared war on Britain in June 1779 as France's ally rather than as an ally of the United States. Bernardo de Gálvez took Baton Rouge in September 1779, Mobile in March 1780 and Pensacola in May 1781, occupying British forces that would otherwise have been available elsewhere. In 1781 Havana raised roughly half a million pesos within hours to fund the Yorktown campaign.

Individuals. Casimir Pulaski, a Catholic Pole, died of wounds at Savannah in October 1779. Tadeusz Kościuszko, also Catholic, served as an engineer at Saratoga and at West Point.

The arrangement in later categories

The Foreign Agents Registration Act dates from 1938 and cannot be applied to 1776 as law. It can be applied as a description, and the description fits closely enough to be worth stating.

Hortalez et Cie was a commercial entity funded by two foreign crowns, established to conduct activity in support of a political outcome in America while preserving the deniability of its principals. Those are the elements the later statute was written to reach: a principal outside the country, activity directed at a political result inside it, and concealment of the relationship.

After 1784 a second relationship of the same shape existed. The head of the Catholic Church in the United States was appointed by, and answerable to, a foreign sovereign — a sovereign with which the United States had no diplomatic relations. Congress had declared in May 1784 that it held no authority over religion, which left the arrangement outside American regulation entirely.

Neither observation implies wrongdoing by any party. Both describe how the founding was in fact financed, armed and, afterwards, ecclesiastically governed.

The charge, 1678 to 1960

The objection raised against Catholics in the English-speaking world was, in the first instance, not theological. It was that they answered to a foreign prince.

That premise is explicit in the Test Act of 1678 and in Maryland's act of 1704. It is the reasoning of the Continental Congress in October 1774, denouncing the Quebec Act to Britain in the same season it invited Catholic Canadians to join the union. It recurs in the burning of the Ursuline convent at Charlestown in 1834, in the Philadelphia riots of 1844, and in the theft and destruction of the stone Pius IX had sent for the Washington Monument in 1854. It appears in the Blaine amendment proposed in 1875 and in the state provisions that followed it. It was raised against Alfred E. Smith in 1928, and John F. Kennedy answered it before the Greater Houston Ministerial Association on 12 September 1960.

The charge was not baseless in its premises, which is why it lasted. Catholics did belong to a communion governed from outside the United States, and the founding had in fact depended on foreign Catholic money and arms. What the record also shows is that those two facts did not produce the disloyalty predicted from them.

The charge was not abandoned so much as answered, and then dismantled in law.

The institutional answer. Alongside the speeches and the rulings, the charge was answered by accumulation. The Knights of Columbus was founded at New Haven in 1882, in the decade when state constitutions were being rewritten on the Blaine model to bar Catholics from public funds. During the First World War the Order ran welfare centres for mobilized troops, open regardless of creed — Catholic provision for Protestant soldiers, at national scale, in wartime. The charge also circulated in forged form. A fabricated “Knights of Columbus oath,” purporting to bind members against Protestants, spread widely from about 1912 and was placed in the Congressional Record during a contested election case. In 1914 the Order's California leadership submitted its actual ceremonies to a committee of four senior California Masons, who examined them and signed a report finding nothing anti-Protestant or anti-Masonic in them; the report was entered into the Congressional Record in January 1915.[31] The accused invited inspection by the fraternity the forgery claimed they were sworn against. In 1924 — the year of the Johnson–Reed Act, at the height of the second Klan — its Historical Commission commissioned W. E. B. Du Bois to write The Gift of Black Folk: The Negroes in the Making of America, one volume of a Racial Contribution Series on groups the national history had passed over.[30] And from about 1951 it campaigned to insert the words “under God” into the Pledge of Allegiance; the law was signed on 14 June 1954.[29] An organization founded because Catholics were suspected of allegiance to a foreign power had placed two words in the national oath of allegiance.

1960. Kennedy's election removed it as a national political force, though the Houston address did so partly by conceding the premise: he pledged that his religion would not govern his public acts.

1965. Dignitatis Humanae, the Second Vatican Council's declaration on religious freedom, was drafted principally by John Courtney Murray, an American Jesuit. The Council held that religious liberty is a right grounded in the dignity of the person and that the state holds no competence in religious matters. The American arrangement — no establishment, no state church — passed from being the ground for distrusting Catholics to being the position of the universal Church, argued largely by an American from American constitutional experience. It is Carroll's reading of 1784 ratified at Rome one hundred and eighty-one years later.

1984. Full diplomatic relations were established between the United States and the Holy See, with William Wilson as first ambassador. Congress had prohibited funding a mission to the Papal States in 1867, and the interval had been covered by personal presidential envoys, among them Myron Taylor under Roosevelt and Truman.

2020 and 2022. Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue and Carson v. Makin curtailed the operation of the Blaine amendments in state constitutions, the last working legal residue of the nineteenth-century exclusion.

What this section claims

It claims that the Catholic contribution to American independence was material, foreign and partly concealed, and that these are documented in treaties, accounts and dispatches.

It claims that the same structural feature — external authority — supplied both the means of the founding and the standing argument for excluding those associated with it.

It does not claim that the exclusionary argument was correct, nor that it was merely a pretext. The first would require showing a harm the record does not show; the second would require showing the premises were false, and they were not. Nor does it claim that the 1965 declaration was caused by the American experience; it records that an American drafted it and argued it from that experience.

Where this is weakest. The figures are stated as magnitudes rather than precise sums: the gunpowder share and the French subsidy totals vary between sources. The registration analogy is a description applied across a hundred and sixty years and carries no legal weight in either direction.

Piracy, Privateering, and the Silver Chain

In brief

Piracy and the opium trade are connected through silver — two sequential solutions to the same balance-of-payments problem, separated by fifty years.

The chain. Spanish American silver was the principal target of Caribbean piracy and privateering between roughly 1570 and 1700. The same silver flowed to Canton, because China would sell tea and take little else. The resulting bullion drain was Britain's structural problem after 1765, and Bengal opium under the 1773 monopoly was the commodity that reversed the flow, so that tea could be bought with Indian opium rather than European silver. The Ganj-i-Sawai plunder of 1695 included Spanish rials; the same metal ran through both eras.

Why the personnel claim fails. The golden age of Atlantic piracy ends around 1722–26; the Bengal opium monopoly begins in 1773. There is no overlap. The men who carried opium to China were licensed country traders and, after 1834, private firms — smugglers under Qing law and lawful merchants under British law. Calling them pirates is rhetorically satisfying and legally wrong, and the wrongness will be used against the argument.

Where the categories genuinely merge. On the Chinese coast. Opium moved inshore in the armed "fast crabs" and "scrambling dragons," crewed by men who ran contraband and raided by turn; Zheng Yi Sao's confederation levied protection over coastal shipping in the same waters, 1801–10, which is why the clippers were armed. That is a documented commercial relationship between the trade and organized maritime force — narrower than the popular claim, and much harder to dismiss.

The skull and crossbones. The documented lineage of the image is Christian: Adam's skull at Golgotha beneath the crucifix, and memento mori in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century funerary carving across Britain, the Low Countries and New England, which sailors saw in every churchyard. The earliest firm flag attestation, Emanuel Wynne off Santiago de Cuba in 1700, pairs skull and crossbones with an hourglass, making the design a legible sentence: your time is running out.

The American inheritance. What the colonies retained was capacity, not criminality: shipwrights, gunners, prize crews, admiralty lawyers, and merchant houses that knew how to arm a vessel and sell a cargo of uncertain provenance. In the imperial wars that capacity was licensed as privateering. In 1775 Congress licensed it again, and its navy's first operation was a raid on Nassau — the pirate republic Woodes Rogers had suppressed in 1718.

Framing safeguard
The Baghdadi strand is real history and belongs in the record — but it travels with the Scottish and New England houses at equal weight, which the evidence supports, and with the British-state monopoly that created the trade named first. Keeping all three in frame is both accurate and what protects the argument from being read as something it is not.
Where this is weakest. The silver chain is a structural argument, not a documented intention. No contemporary states that opium was adopted to reverse a bullion flow that piracy had once targeted; the two eras are linked by the same metal and the same balance-of-payments problem, which is a pattern a reader may find suggestive or may find merely sequential.

The Nest and the Port: Human Continuity from Piracy to Revolution

In brief

An earlier formulation on this page held that piracy suppression reached 1776 through law rather than through people. That was too narrow. Two major studies show the transmission also ran through the port communities themselves — the merchants, governors, prize-court officers and families who sheltered pirates in the 1690s and resisted imperial jurisdiction thereafter. Not the same individuals; the same social formation.

Piracy lived on land

Mark Hanna's Pirate Nests and the Rise of the British Empire turns the subject inside out. Rather than following colorful individuals at sea, Hanna concentrates on the nexus between land and sea, arguing that piracy persists for a reason and that when the reason disappears the pirates do too — so the supportive nests ashore, not the perpetrators afloat, are what suppression must actually target.[19] Piracy and unregulated privateering flourished in the Pacific, Caribbean and Indian Ocean because of the active support of merchant elites in the North American colonies; far from hindering trade, the marauders' enterprises contributed to commercial development and to the economic infrastructure of the port towns.[19]

The political content is explicit. Hanna argues that wherever piracy flourished, it was because landed communities had political reasons for hindering imperial attempts to define piracy as entirely criminal, and that tolerance for pirates served as a test of where colonists stood on imperial integration: one faction accepted integration as the price of prosperity, while others — notably in the non-royal colonies — preferred the immediate benefits that sheltering pirates brought to hard-pressed local societies.[19] Popular support rested on traditions of self-reliance and autonomy that rejected metropolitan attempts to control the situation, and reviewers have noted that the account helps locate early American history and the Revolution.[19]

The pirates held office

Douglas Burgess's The Politics of Piracy supplies the personnel directly. Against the unlettered stereotype, he shows that pirates and illicit traders moved easily in colonial society, attaining respectability and even political office, while the goods they supplied became a cornerstone of colonial trade and transformed port cities from barren outposts into rich capitals.[20] The transformation reached the political sphere: colonial governors furnished local mariners with privateering commissions, presided over prize courts that validated stolen wares, and defended their prerogatives as vice-admirals.[20]

His conclusion is the one this section exists to record. The war on piracy, remembered as a triumph for the English state and its colonies, in fact drove a wedge between them, imperiling the American enterprise and bringing the colonies to the verge of rebellion; by the end of the seventeenth century the social and political structures erected in the colonies to protect illicit trade amounted to an independent American legal system, and the resulting tensions presage, and may predestine, the dissolution of 1776.[20] His closing chapter is titled "Forgotten Revolutions."

The corrected formulation
Anti-piracy reached 1776 through two channels, not one. The jurisdiction persisted in statute — vice-admiralty trials without juries, enacted in 1698 and 1700 and still a grievance seventy years later. The constituency persisted in the ports — the merchant families, governors' offices, prize courts and legal structures built to shelter illicit trade, which remained in place to resist the same jurisdiction in 1765. Human continuity here means continuity of a social formation, not of individuals, and it requires no one to live eighty years.

Why this strengthens the licence thesis rather than complicating it. Hanna's reframing supplies the missing middle term. If piracy lives in the communities that service it, then suppressing piracy necessarily means asserting jurisdiction over colonial ports, colonial juries and colonial governors — which is exactly what the 1698 and 1700 acts did. The constitutional grievance of the 1760s is therefore not a coincidence of legal form but the predictable consequence of how the earlier suppression had to be carried out. The licence was policed by taking authority away from the places that issued the unofficial version of it.

The scope of the finding. What is documented here, and what this page uses, is the institutional and social transmission: the port communities that sheltered the trade built colonial legal structures, and those structures outlasted the trade. A separate argument about shipboard governance and republican thought is advanced by Rediker and Linebaugh; the two are distinct questions and are kept distinct.

Where this is weakest. Hanna and Burgess establish that pirate-harbouring communities built colonial legal structures, and that those structures resisted imperial jurisdiction. The step this page adds — that the same social formation persisted to 1765 — is continuity of interest and office rather than of documented persons,; the link is continuity of interest and office rather than of named persons.

Conclusion

Four observations survive the sorting.

The category was always legal, never moral. Every was hanged for what Child's Company had done under charter. The Qawasim were destroyed for taxing a route the Company taxed. When the colonists became a state, their first act at sea was to issue commissions of their own. Nothing in this requires a hidden hand; it requires only that one notice who holds the pen.

Anti-piracy built the machinery that provoked the Revolution, and left the constituency to resist it. Vice-admiralty jurisdiction without juries entered American life in 1698 as a Company remedy against pirate-harbouring ports. Seventy years later it was the grievance. The transmission ran on two channels: the jurisdiction persisted in statute, and the port communities that had sheltered the trade — their merchant families, governors' offices and prize courts — persisted alongside it. The empire's instrument for policing its own frontier of licence became the instrument that lost it the frontier, in the same places it had first been aimed.

Institutions are rarely restored by their friends. The Society was suppressed by Catholic kings, preserved by an Orthodox empress, given political room by a man who imprisoned the pope, and restored by that pope three months after his release. The Order of Malta was rescued uncanonically by a Russian emperor, lost its island to a British refusal to evacuate, and recovered as sovereignty exercised from two extraterritorial houses in Rome rather than over any territory of its own. The papacy spent fifty-nine years with no land at all. And the one body here that was never suppressed — the Company, which had no principle beyond extraction — was wound up by statute and left no successor.

Medical care recurs at the points where the record turns. Twice in this sequence the medical work of a Catholic institution preceded a decision that shaped an institution. In 1776 a Jesuit with no order behind him attended a sick deist on the journey back to Philadelphia, and eight years later that deist's private recommendation determined who would govern the Catholic Church in the United States. In 1938 the sisters of a hospital in Omaha carried a Protestant boy through a ruptured appendix, and eighty-seven years later he opened the last letter of his life with them. Neither episode carried a condition. The same activity opens the timeline: the Amalfitan merchants who built a hospital at Jerusalem around 1048. Between those endpoints lie the six or seven hundred sisters who nursed both armies from 1861 to 1865, commemorated in Washington in 1924, and the hospitals that served American cities through the cholera and yellow-fever epidemics. Whether the pattern amounts to a mechanism is a question this page raises rather than settles.

Two dates are worth noticing now that everything sits in one sequence. 21 July and 16 December 1773 fall five months apart: the Society suppressed under crown pressure, and the Company's surplus destroyed in Boston — the two institutions that had carried European expansion into Asia and America, both broken in the same year by the states that had chartered them. And 1784 does double duty: in February the Empress of China sails to breach the Company's monopoly; in May Congress disclaims authority over religion; in June a suppressed Jesuit is named to lead the American church on a Protestant deist's recommendation; and in August Parliament puts the Company under a Board of Control. The new republic took up one licensing power and set down another in the same six months.

Summary
Licence, not conspiracy. The institutions that survived suppression did so by moving their licence to a jurisdiction that would not enforce it; the institution that held the most licence and the least principle is the only one that ended. The sick-room recurs at the points where that pattern turns.
