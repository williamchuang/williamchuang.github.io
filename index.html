How the λ-secure “self-laundromat” works

The core idea is simple: the geometry and machinery come from Logarchéon; the secret coordinate system comes from you. A private geometric key — your own change of basis — defines the coordinates in which your model actually works.

Step 1

You generate a private geometric key

  • You run a setup routine (or the runtime) that embeds your documents or signals into the λ-geometry space.
  • You generate a private geometric key on your side: in practice, a secret change of coordinates for the model and your data.
  • The system produces a transformed model and transformed data that live entirely in this disguised coordinate system.
Step 2

Training happens in the disguised coordinates

  • Training runs either on:
    • your own GPU/on-prem server, or
    • GPUs you rent in your own cloud account, using the λ-secure runtime.
  • Only the transformed model and transformed data ever touch that compute environment.
  • Neither Logarchéon nor the cloud provider sees plaintext data or a canonical tuned model.
Step 3

Inference stays vendor-blind

  • You can keep everything in the disguised coordinates for operations, or reverse the geometric key locally if you need canonical outputs.
  • Even if someone copies the runtime or model, they obtain only a transformed version, not a directly usable twin.
  • Standard crypto (disk encryption, TLS, TEEs) sits underneath the λ-geometry layer as defense-in-depth.

Plain English You get a self-service “encrypted AI laundromat”: your data and model go in wearing your own geometric disguise; all training and inference happen in that disguise; only you can remove it—and only if your policy says you should.

Security posture: what is protected, what is required, what is not claimed

This section is written for buyers who care about real adversaries, not slogans. It is assertive on what Logarchéon is built to do, explicit about what is required for those claims to hold, and careful about what is not being promised.

Core claim

If an attacker steals only transformed artifacts from storage—numbers with no keys and no operational access—they do not obtain readable data, and they do not obtain a usable interface to replicate your model’s behavior. They can compute “structure,” but structure is not intelligence unless it can be anchored.

Anchor principle Intelligence is not intelligence if you cannot anchor it—to identities, timestamps, provenance, or a query path.

Define the scenario

What “stolen artifacts only” means here

  • Attacker has: read-only access to transformed weights and/or transformed embeddings sitting in storage.
  • Attacker does not have: your geometric key, plaintext inputs/outputs, IDs/timestamps/labels, or any way to submit queries and observe results.
  • Key implication: no oracle. No feedback. No reliable way to test hypotheses.
MECE view

Five ways attackers try to extract value

  • Interpretation: “What does this cluster mean?”
  • Attribution: “Which entity/time/system created this?”
  • Operationalization: “Can I use this to answer new questions?”
  • Linkage: “Can I join this to external identity/metadata?”
  • Blast-radius scaling: “Can I turn one leak into a full compromise?”

Brute-force context

Key search is an entropy story

In practice, “guessing the key” is governed by effective entropy. The table below is illustrative only (expected brute-force time at 1018 guesses/second).

Effective entropy Expected brute-force time Deployment posture
128-bit ~ 5.39 × 1012 years Baseline floor in many systems; not Logarchéon’s target for long-lived, high-assurance deployments.
192-bit ~ 9.95 × 1031 years Conservative option for long-lived deployments.
256-bit+ default target ~ 1.83 × 1051 years Budget for worst-case assumptions and multi-tenant lifetime.

Quantum note Generic quantum search (Grover) can reduce effective symmetric-key security by roughly half (k → k/2). For this reason, Logarchéon targets 256-bit+ effective entropy to retain ~128-bit-class security even in a conservative quantum model.

Defense-in-depth options

Two extra layers buyers actually use

  • Dimensional padding (optional): embeddings/models can be carried in a higher-dimensional working representation with removable redundancy. Customers keep the “true” subspace definition; redundancy can be stripped on retrieval under policy. This is sold as defense-in-depth, not as a sole security guarantee.
  • Bulkhead mode (compartmented twins): for extreme sensitivity, customers can train and move workloads in small, isolated increments (separate “compartments,” like a ship). Even under an insider key leak, the exposed portion is bounded to the compartment involved at that step.

Blast-radius control The goal is not perfection; it is bounded damage under realistic failure modes.

Q&A (operational, not marketing)

Security Q&A

Q: If someone steals the transformed index, can they still cluster it and learn “who is close to whom”?

They can compute numerical relationships inside what they stole. The missing step is what makes it useful: anchoring (IDs, labels, timestamps, filenames, provenance) and operational access (a compatible way to produce queries and validate hypotheses).

Without anchors and without operational access, they can produce patterns, but they cannot convert patterns into client-identifiable or action-driving intelligence.

Working rule: intelligence is not intelligence if you cannot anchor it.

Q: When you say “stolen storage, no interface,” what does “interface” mean?

An interface is any channel that lets an attacker test guesses or use the system the way your users do. Examples:

  • Query UI / API: “Send a question → get answers, scores, or nearest-neighbor results.”
  • Embedding / indexing pipeline access: “I can run the same internal steps your system uses to turn a query into a compatible vector.”
  • Verification oracle: any feedback loop that tells the attacker “your guess was correct” (e.g., outputs match known results).

In the stolen storage, no interface case, the attacker has a file dump of transformed artifacts, but no ability to run live queries, no ability to generate compatible query vectors, and no way to validate interpretations.

Q: If the attacker somehow gets the right key/transform, can’t they undo it and recover everything?

If the secret transform is truly recovered, then the disguise can be undone for whatever was transformed. The remaining question is operational: what else did the attacker steal besides transformed numbers?

Two common realities matter:

  • Transformed vectors are usually not the original documents. In typical systems, vectors are derived representations. Undoing the transform gives you vectors again—still not the PDFs/emails—unless the raw documents were also stolen.
  • “Actionable intelligence” still requires anchoring. If the attacker lacks the mapping from “vector row” to “document ID / owner / timestamp / location,” then even perfectly recovered vectors may not translate into named, time-bounded intelligence.

Working rule: if you cannot anchor it, it is not intelligence.

Q: Concrete example (Agency-style): when is “even with the key” still not automatically actionable?

Consider an Agency retrieval system that supports analysts searching a classified corpus. Operationally, such systems almost always separate:

  • The documents (stored in a protected repository with access controls),
  • The vector index (a nearest-neighbor search structure), and
  • A mapping table that links “row 1,787” in the index to “Doc-ID, compartment, storage path, timestamp, etc.”

Case 1: attacker steals only the vector index file

  • They can compute relationships like “row 1,787 is close to row 144,000.”
  • But without the mapping table, those rows have no identities, no compartments, no timestamps, and no storage paths.
  • They cannot fetch documents, and they cannot name what they are looking at.

Case 2: attacker also obtains the secret transform/key

  • They may be able to undo the disguise on the index.
  • But they still have “row 1,787,” not “Report AB-1776 / source X / date Y,” unless they also stole the mapping table.
  • They still cannot use the system like an analyst without a query interface and access paths.

What makes it truly dangerous

  • Anchors leak: the mapping table leaks (row ↔ doc-ID/path/timestamp), or any joinable metadata leaks.
  • Access leaks: the attacker gains repository access (storage paths, credentials, or backups).
  • Oracle leaks: transformed queries leak from endpoints/logs, or the attacker can query a live interface.

Bottom line: the blast radius is determined by what leaks together: artifacts alone are different from artifacts + anchors + access.

Q: Optional technical notation (only if you want symbols)—what do the terms mean?

If a reviewer wants notation, we define it explicitly here:

  • D = the raw corpus (PDFs, emails, logs, reports).
  • E(·) = the encoder that turns raw inputs into vectors.
  • X = the canonical vectors produced by the encoder (the “normal” representation).
  • T = the customer-held secret transform (the geometric key).
  • X′ = the transformed vectors stored/used by the runtime.

In shorthand: raw data D → encoder E gives vectors X; then secret transform T produces stored vectors X′.

Q: Why isn’t “structure leakage” automatically a breach?

Because “structure” is not a business outcome. It becomes a breach when an attacker can connect it to something real: names, cases, patients, assets, time, or a query interface that lets them validate guesses. This is why Logarchéon treats metadata containment and query-path control as first-class security requirements.

Q: What does Logarchéon explicitly NOT claim?
  • No claim of “semantic secrecy” if you leak metadata. If IDs, timestamps, labels, filenames, or join keys leak, risk increases by definition.
  • No claim of safety under endpoint compromise. If transformed query vectors leak from endpoints/logs, you have created an oracle-like channel.
  • No claim that standard cryptography becomes optional. Disk encryption, TLS, access control, and auditing remain mandatory.
Q: Can quantum computing “tunnel through” 256-bit keys?

Not in any generic, accepted model of symmetric-key search. The conservative generic quantum advantage is Grover-style speedup (roughly a square-root speedup), which is why the default posture targets 256-bit+ effective entropy. “Tunneling” is not a separate generic shortcut that collapses symmetric-key security.

Quantum note: Generic quantum search (Grover) can reduce effective symmetric-key security by roughly half (k → k/2). For this reason, Logarchéon targets 256-bit+ effective entropy to retain ~128-bit-class security even in a conservative quantum model.

Q: What operational controls are required for the claim to hold?
  • Tenant-unique keys + rotation (limit correlation and blast radius).
  • Keep anchors separate (mapping tables, provenance metadata, join keys).
  • No embedding/hidden-state export outside the customer trust boundary (treat transformed representations as sensitive).
  • Harden endpoints like credentials (least privilege, minimal telemetry, enclaves where appropriate).
  • Strict metadata containment (IDs/timestamps/labels are often the real leak).
Q: If an insider leaks a key, is everything over?

Key leakage is serious. The goal is to avoid “one mistake equals total compromise” via blast-radius control: tenant-unique keys, key rotation, and optional bulkhead mode (compartmentalized processing for extremely sensitive workloads).

Bulkhead mode is the operational analogue of watertight compartments: you can structure processing so that a single compromised key is not automatically a full historical compromise.

Bottom line: Logarchéon’s security posture is not “magic encryption.” It is a design that makes stolen artifacts materially less reusable by default—then backs that up with the operational controls that decide whether real-world attackers can convert patterns into anchored, actionable intelligence.

Under the hood: CEAS, finite lift, GRAIL, and MIA

The landing page is simple on purpose. Underneath, the stack draws on original work in geometry, symbolic dynamics, and secure computation. The emphasis is: interpretable dynamics, encrypted-in-use execution, and invariant-first hardware.

Core pillars
  • CEAS: critical-entropy attention scaling; treats β as a controlled parameter to cut training steps and improve stability, especially in long-sequence regimes.
  • Finite-machine lift: decomposes model behavior into cycles and transients (Dunford D+N / PDN) for traceability and safe edit-without-full-retrain.
  • GRAIL: geometry-native secure execution aimed at cryptomorphic (function-identical, weight-distinct) twins and encrypted-in-use computation.
  • MIA: metric-invariant architecture where inputs, machine state, and outputs move together under group action; suitable for FPGAs and mature-node silicon.
