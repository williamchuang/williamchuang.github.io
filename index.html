Security posture: what we claim, what we require

Logarchéon is designed for environments where “we encrypted the disk” is not the whole story. The goal is to keep stolen artifacts from turning into usable leverage. Structure may exist in any representation—but intelligence is not intelligence if you cannot anchor it.

Baseline attacker (explicitly defined)

Read-only theft of transformed artifacts

  • Attacker steals only transformed artifacts (e.g., transformed vectors / transformed weights) from storage.
  • No plaintext inputs or outputs.
  • No labels, IDs, timestamps, or provenance metadata.
  • No query interface; no ability to submit inputs and observe outputs.
  • No known before/after pairs; no way to “check a guess.”

Practical effect They can do offline math, but they cannot reliably name what they see or operationalize it without an anchor or an oracle.

What turns this into a different risk class

Anchors and oracles

  • Anchors: document IDs, timestamps, client names, case numbers, patient identifiers, cross-dataset join keys.
  • Oracles: any channel that validates hypotheses (query access, endpoint leakage of keyed queries, logging of keyed embeddings).
  • Key reuse: reuse across tenants or long-lived deployments increases correlation value.

Sales-safe truth “Unlabeled structure” is not the same thing as “actionable intelligence.” Actionability is created by anchors and operational access.

Key strength (policy target)

Brute-force economics, and a conservative quantum note

Quantum note Generic quantum search (Grover) can reduce effective symmetric-key security by roughly half (k → k/2). For this reason, Logarchéon targets 256-bit+ effective entropy to retain ~128-bit-class security even in a conservative quantum model.

Effective entropy Classical brute-force scale (intuition) Quantum-adjusted intuition (Grover) Deployment note
128-bit Beyond practical brute-force in most real systems. Behaves like ~64-bit against a strong quantum search model. Minimum floor in many systems; not our target.
192-bit Conservative long-lived security margin. Behaves like ~96-bit against a strong quantum search model. Option for long-lived deployments.
256-bit+ default target Budgeted for worst-case assumptions and multi-tenant lifetime. Behaves like ~128-bit against a strong quantum search model. Default target for high-assurance posture.

Important This table is posture guidance, not a promise of “time-to-break.” Real-world outcomes depend on implementation and whether an attacker has a validation channel (an oracle).

Blast-radius control

Bulkheads: compartmented twins

For the most sensitive workflows, treat the runtime like a submarine: isolate payloads into compartments. If one compartment is compromised, the ship stays afloat.

  • Compartmented ingestion: upload only a small slice of data per cycle (a “compartment”), train, and pull it back.
  • Compartmented state: maintain multiple cryptomorphic twins across cycles, rather than a single monolithic training run.
  • Keyed separation: use tenant-unique keys and rotate keys per cycle/compartment to limit correlation value.

Why this matters Guessing the key is unlikely. Insider leakage is the higher-probability problem. Bulkheads are designed to cap damage even in that world.

Operational pattern

Cycle: load → train → retrieve → re-key → repeat

  • Load: move one compartment to cloud compute (still under your tenancy and controls).
  • Train: run the tuning step in the disguised coordinates; keep transformed artifacts contained.
  • Retrieve: pull the updated artifacts back under your governance boundary.
  • Re-key: rotate keys (and optionally re-wrap state) before the next compartment.

Not a magic claim This is not “perfect secrecy.” It is disciplined segmentation: you are engineering the maximum plausible breach into a smaller, survivable event.

Bottom line: the security posture is a combination of representation hardening (GRAIL), key discipline (unique + rotated), oracle suppression (containment + endpoint hardening), and blast-radius engineering (bulkheads).
Q&A

Quick answers for buyers and reviewers

Does “similar stays similar” mean an attacker can steal usable intelligence?

Similarity can remain meaningful for authorized computation inside the keyed runtime. For an unauthorized party, similarity becomes intelligence only when it can be anchored to identities, timelines, or operational hooks. If you cannot anchor it, it is not intelligence.

Why isn’t clustering alone a break?

Clustering is only dangerous when clusters can be named and exploited. Under read-only theft with no IDs/timestamps/labels and no interface to test hypotheses, “a cluster exists” does not translate into “this is Client X / Matter Y / Incident Z.”

Why add “bulkheads” if keys are already strong?

Because the most realistic failure mode in high-assurance environments is not brute-force guessing; it is operational compromise (insider leakage, process failure, accidental export). Bulkheads are a second line of defense that reduces the maximum plausible damage even when a key leaks.

What should we assume about quantum?

For symmetric-key brute-force, the conservative model is Grover-style speedup, which motivates a 256-bit+ target. Quantum changes the margin; it does not replace key discipline, oracle suppression, or segmentation.
