Why this exists: the “in‑use” trust boundary problem

Most security stacks protect AI at rest (storage) and in transit (network). Training and inference are different: they are in‑use operations. This is the phase where models, prompts, and intermediate representations must exist in operational form inside a runtime. That is where leakage, misuse, and misplaced trust assumptions matter most.

Definition

What “in use” means

“In use” means active computation: weights instantiated, activations flowing, embeddings cached, and intermediate states manipulated in memory (CPU, GPU, accelerator, and their runtimes). Encryption at rest and TLS do not, by themselves, address this phase.

Why “trust the CPU” is not a neutral assumption

Modern platforms include vendor‑managed subsystems designed for management and security. These components operate below the OS and expand the trusted computing base. This is not an allegation of intent; it is a standard threat‑modeling reality for high‑assurance buyers.

Vendor firmware subsystems (what they are)

  • Intel Management Engine (ME / CSME) — a dedicated microcontroller present in Intel chipsets since ~2008, capable of running code independently of the main CPU, accessing memory, and operating while the system is powered.
  • Intel Active Management Technology (AMT) — an out‑of‑band remote management capability running on ME (e.g., remote power, provisioning, recovery) on supported platforms.
  • AMD Platform Security Processor (PSP / Secure Processor) — a dedicated security processor embedded in AMD systems (since ~2013) used for secure boot, key handling, and platform security services.

Descriptive references: Intel ME/CSME, Intel AMT, AMD PSP.

Threat relevance and evidence

This class of subsystem is explicitly designed to persist below OS visibility. Historically, attackers value such layers for persistence, invisibility, and control.

  • Below‑OS persistence: firmware‑level footholds can survive OS reinstallation.
  • Out‑of‑band control surface: management features bypass host‑based defenses.
  • Vulnerability history: critical bugs have been documented over time.

Example signals (non‑exhaustive): Intel advisories SA‑00075, SA‑00086, and CVE‑2017‑5689 (NVD).

Generalization Similar management/security subsystems may exist in other SoCs, including less‑documented or foreign chipsets. Lack of documentation does not imply trustworthiness.

Why existing cryptographic methods fall short “in use”

FHE, MPC, and related approaches

  • FHE: operates on encrypted scalars; impractical for real‑time, large neural workloads with complex activations.
  • MPC: requires multiple parties, high bandwidth, and small/static payloads.
  • Runtime reality: many approaches still require decryption or canonical representations during execution, exposing data to firmware‑level trust boundaries.
What Logarchéon (GRAIL / Λ‑Stack) changes
  • No reliance on trusted CPU state: sensitive representations are not decrypted inside the CPU core.
  • Encrypted‑in‑use semantics: models are algebraically transformed, including representational semantics.
  • Plaintext‑speed operation: real‑time training and inference without FHE/MPC overhead.
  • Buyer‑held keys: governance, instantiation, and blast‑radius control remain under customer custody.
Bottom line: GRAIL runs at normal speed without trusting CPU firmware. Compared to FHE/MPC, it is orders of magnitude faster. Compared to plaintext, performance is equal—even with frequent or per‑step key rotation.
Residual risk & buyer responsibility

What this does not eliminate

No software system eliminates physical, RF, or side‑channel risk. High‑assurance deployments may still require controlled environments (SCIF/EMSEC discipline), power isolation, and strict endpoint governance.

Responsible deployment reminder Logarchéon reduces “in‑use” exposure and artifact reuse. It does not replace physical security, emissions control, or operational discipline.

Why CPUs Cannot Be Fully Trusted — and What This Reveals About GRAIL's Capabilities

Publicly Known Surveillance Units in CPUs

Modern CPUs from Intel and AMD embed persistent coprocessors with deep and privileged access to the system. These components include:

  • Intel Management Engine (ME) — a coprocessor embedded in Intel chipsets since 2008, now evolved into the Converged Security & Management Engine (CSME). It remains active as long as power is available and can execute code, access memory, modify storage, and transmit data independent of the OS.
  • Intel Active Management Technology (AMT) — runs atop ME/CSME, enabling out-of-band remote control: install software, reboot, or wipe disks—all without OS-level visibility.
  • AMD Platform Security Processor (PSP) — a firmware-level embedded control unit in AMD chips since ~2013, used for secure boot, fTPM, and other security services. Also known as the AMD Secure Processor.

These components can remain active even when the CPU appears powered off, and may communicate over network interfaces without host knowledge. Multiple vulnerabilities have been disclosed: INTEL-SA-00075, INTEL-SA-00086. Their mere presence in the trust base raises serious concerns.

Bottom line: You cannot fully trust CPU firmware—not Intel, not AMD, and not undocumented foreign chipsets. GRAIL avoids relying on these units entirely.

Why GRAIL Avoids the CPU Trust Trap—But Was Not Built For It

GRAIL’s ability to bypass CPU trust is not its goal—it is a by-product of its core design: a geometry-native, algebraic execution model that redefines how neural networks represent and operate on information. Because all computation happens over encrypted operator representations, and no decryption occurs inside the CPU, GRAIL naturally avoids firmware-scale threats.

GRAIL was originally designed for scientific AI—especially for universal-scale workloads like Mission Genesis, the U.S. Department of Energy’s national initiative to use AI to accelerate fundamental discovery. Its architecture is fully compatible with both general relativity (GR) and quantum field theory (QFT), enabling models to operate across the smallest and largest known physical regimes.

It supports coordinate transformations across observer frames and physical locations, enabling distributed agents to read and interpret signals without relying on fixed Euclidean representations. Euclidean-space neural models are often mismatched with the curved, relativistic, or quantum nature of the domains they are applied to. GRAIL remedies that by building its framework on geometric categories that are observer-invariant and covariant.

Note: GRAIL is not merely a CPU-avoidance strategy. It is a superset framework—reconciling symbolic entropy, post-quantum cryptography, curved geometry, and algebraic invariance within a unified execution architecture.

Security posture: what is protected, what is required, what is not claimed

This section is written for buyers who care about real adversaries, not slogans. It is assertive on what Logarchéon is built to do, explicit about what is required for those claims to hold, and careful about what is not being promised.

Core claim

If an attacker steals only transformed artifacts from storage—numbers with no keys and no operational access—they do not obtain readable data, and they do not obtain a ready-made way to turn those artifacts into mission-relevant “intelligence.” They can compute “structure,” but structure is not intelligence unless it can be anchored.

Anchor principle Intelligence is not intelligence if you cannot anchor it—to identities, timestamps, provenance, or a query path.

Define the scenario

What “stolen artifacts only” means here

  • Attacker has: read-only access to transformed weights and/or transformed embeddings sitting in storage.
  • Attacker does not have: your keys, plaintext inputs/outputs, IDs/timestamps/labels, or any way to submit queries and observe results.
  • Key implication: no oracle. No feedback. No reliable way to test hypotheses.
MECE view

Five ways attackers try to extract value

  • Interpretation: “What does this cluster mean?”
  • Attribution: “Which entity/time/system created this?”
  • Operationalization: “Can I use this to answer new questions?”
  • Linkage: “Can I join this to external identity/metadata?”
  • Blast-radius scaling: “Can I turn one leak into a full compromise?”

Q&A (operational, not marketing)

Security Q&A

Q: If someone steals the transformed index, can they still cluster it and learn “who is close to whom”?

They can compute numerical relationships inside what they stole. The missing step is what makes it useful: anchoring (IDs, labels, timestamps, filenames, provenance) and operational access (a compatible way to produce queries and validate hypotheses).

Without anchors and without operational access, they can produce patterns, but they cannot convert patterns into client-identifiable or action-driving intelligence.

Working rule: intelligence is not intelligence if you cannot anchor it.

Q: When you say “stolen storage, no interface,” what does “interface” mean?

An interface is any channel that lets an attacker test guesses or use the system the way your users do. Examples:

  • Query UI / API: “Send a question → get answers, scores, or nearest-neighbor results.”
  • Embedding / indexing pipeline access: “I can run the same internal steps your system uses to turn a query into a compatible vector.”
  • Verification oracle: any feedback loop that tells the attacker “your guess was correct” (e.g., outputs match known results).

In the stolen storage, no interface case, the attacker has a file dump of transformed artifacts, but no ability to run live queries, no ability to generate compatible query vectors, and no way to validate interpretations.

Q: If the attacker somehow gets the right key/transform, can’t they undo it and recover everything?

If customer-held secrets are truly recovered, then whatever was protected by those secrets is at risk. The remaining question is operational: what else did the attacker steal besides transformed numbers?

Two common realities matter:

  • Transformed vectors are usually not the original documents. In typical systems, vectors are derived representations. Undoing a transform gives you vectors again—still not the PDFs/emails—unless the raw documents were also stolen.
  • “Actionable intelligence” still requires anchoring. If the attacker lacks the mapping from “vector row” to “document ID / owner / timestamp / location,” then even perfectly recovered vectors may not translate into named, time-bounded intelligence.

Working rule: if you cannot anchor it, it is not intelligence.

Q: Concrete example (Agency-style): when is “even with the key” still not automatically actionable?

Consider an Agency retrieval system that supports analysts searching a classified corpus. Operationally, such systems almost always separate:

  • The documents (stored in a protected repository with access controls),
  • The vector index (a nearest-neighbor search structure), and
  • A mapping table that links “row 1,787” in the index to “Doc-ID, compartment, storage path, timestamp, etc.”

Case 1: attacker steals only the vector index file

  • They can compute relationships like “row 1,787 is close to row 144,000.”
  • But without the mapping table, those rows have no identities, no compartments, no timestamps, and no storage paths.
  • They cannot fetch documents, and they cannot name what they are looking at.

Case 2: attacker also obtains customer-held secrets

  • They may be able to undo some protections on the index.
  • But they still have “row 1,787,” not “Report AB-1776 / source X / date Y,” unless they also stole the mapping table.
  • They still cannot use the system like an analyst without a query interface and access paths.

What makes it truly dangerous

  • Anchors leak: the mapping table leaks (row ↔ doc-ID/path/timestamp), or any joinable metadata leaks.
  • Access leaks: the attacker gains repository access (storage paths, credentials, or backups).
  • Oracle leaks: transformed queries leak from endpoints/logs, or the attacker can query a live interface.

Bottom line: the blast radius is determined by what leaks together: artifacts alone are different from artifacts + anchors + access.

Q: Why isn’t “structure leakage” automatically a breach?

Because “structure” is not a business outcome. It becomes a breach when an attacker can connect it to something real: names, cases, patients, assets, time, or a query interface that lets them validate guesses. This is why Logarchéon treats metadata containment and query-path control as first-class security requirements.

Q: What does Logarchéon explicitly NOT claim?
  • No claim of “semantic secrecy” if you leak metadata. If IDs, timestamps, labels, filenames, or join keys leak, risk increases by definition.
  • No claim of safety under endpoint compromise. If transformed query vectors leak from endpoints/logs, you have created an oracle-like channel.
  • No claim that standard cryptography becomes optional. Disk encryption, TLS, access control, and auditing remain mandatory.
Q: Can quantum computing “tunnel through” 256-bit keys?

Not in any generic, accepted model of symmetric-key search. The conservative generic quantum advantage is Grover-style speedup (roughly a square-root speedup), which is why the default posture targets 256-bit+ effective entropy. “Tunneling” is not a separate generic shortcut that collapses symmetric-key security.

Quantum note: Generic quantum search (Grover) can reduce effective symmetric-key security by roughly half (k → k/2). For this reason, Logarchéon targets 256-bit+ effective entropy to retain ~128-bit-class security even in a conservative quantum model.

Q: What operational controls are required for the claim to hold?
  • Tenant-unique keys + rotation (limit correlation and blast radius).
  • Keep anchors separate (mapping tables, provenance metadata, join keys).
  • No embedding/hidden-state export outside the customer trust boundary (treat transformed representations as sensitive).
  • Harden endpoints like credentials (least privilege, minimal telemetry, enclaves where appropriate).
  • Strict metadata containment (IDs/timestamps/labels are often the real leak).
Q: If an insider leaks a key, is everything over?

Key leakage is serious. The goal is to avoid “one mistake equals total compromise” via blast-radius control: tenant-unique keys, key rotation, and optional bulkhead mode (compartmentalized processing for extremely sensitive workloads).

Bulkhead mode is the operational analogue of watertight compartments: you can structure processing so that a single compromised key is not automatically a full historical compromise.

Bottom line: Logarchéon’s security posture is not “magic encryption.” It is a design that makes stolen artifacts materially less reusable by default—then backs that up with the operational controls that decide whether real-world attackers can convert patterns into anchored, actionable intelligence.
