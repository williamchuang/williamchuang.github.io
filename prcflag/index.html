The Long Arm of the CCP: Tracing Patterns of Influence in U.S. Higher Education, Municipal Politics, and Corporate Finance

Migration selection, diaspora alignment, cross-system brokerage rents, and foreign-state influence in the United States—with San Francisco’s Chinese-American institutional ecology as a longitudinal case, 1990–2026.

Core proposition

Migration from an authoritarian state does not identify a migrant’s political preference toward that state’s regime. Exit may follow education, family, professional opportunity, income, lifestyle, or political pressure. After migration, homeland identity, cross-border economic incentives, institutional networks, foreign-state cultivation, and coercive connectivity may continue to shape preferences and behavior.

Core Thesis & Causal Architecture

Migration–Preference Identification Error
Moving to the United States is not evidence that an immigrant opposed the government of the country left behind.

This paper argues that the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) has established a pervasive and deeply entrenched network of influence within key American institutions, particularly in the realms of higher education, municipal politics, and corporate finance. Using the case of the University of San Francisco (USF) and its ties to San Francisco city government as a focal point, it traces the mechanisms by which the CCP has leveraged economic, political, and cultural capital to shape institutional incentives and individual behaviors in ways that serve its strategic interests, often at the expense of American values and national security. By mapping these patterns of influence and the costs they impose on those who resist them, this study aims to sound an urgent alarm about the nature and extent of the CCP's 'sharp power' within the United States, and to rally a vigorous, whole-of-society response to protect the integrity of our most vital institutions.

\[P(\text{opposes CCP}\mid \text{emigrated from China}) \neq 1\]

More generally, migration status cannot identify homeland-regime preference because the migration event is generated by heterogeneous causes:

\[E = E_{education}+E_{family}+E_{economic}+E_{professional}+E_{lifestyle}+E_{political}+\cdots\]

Causal architecture

The argument proceeds across four levels that must remain analytically distinct. At the individual level, migration selection explains why exit does not identify regime opposition. At the network level, cross-system brokerage, family ties, status, and coercive connectivity can preserve incentives for homeland engagement. At the institutional level, Chinatown associations and universities can develop durable PRC-facing relationships for reasons that include constituency representation, internationalization, revenue exposure, and organizational path dependence. At the symbolic level, flags and public events reveal institutional choices and mobilization but do not identify hidden ideology.

The 2018–20 rupture then supplies the temporal test: if mass opinion turns downward faster than established organizational relationships, the resulting divergence is evidence of institutional persistence, not proof that every participant shares the same political motive. The legal analysis enters only after this separation, asking when protected advocacy becomes foreign-directed activity and when ordinary scrutiny becomes unsupported attribution or discriminatory suspicion.

\[\text{Exit}\rightarrow\text{Networks}\rightarrow\text{Institutional incentives}\rightarrow\text{Symbolic environment}\rightarrow\text{2019 shock}\rightarrow\text{Persistence / divergence}\rightarrow\text{legal boundary}\]
Scope: who this framework applies to
The mechanisms formalized in Section 06 — migration selection, civilizational and national identity, economic and network pragmatism, institutional path dependence, foreign-state cultivation, and coercive connectivity — are actor-agnostic. They operate on any individual or institution with the relevant exposure or vulnerability. Chinese Americans and immigrants from the PRC are the paper's principal case; Taiwanese Americans and immigrants from Taiwan, Americans of any background, and persons from any country of origin sit under the same framework whenever they share the relevant exposure. The vectors that weight the mechanisms differ per individual and combine in different proportions: threats to the safety or livelihood of relatives inside PRC reach; financial rewards and career advancement contingent on PRC-market access; coercion, tasking, or surveillance by PRC state agencies including the Ministry of State Security (MSS), People's Liberation Army (PLA), United Front Work Department, or provincial and municipal security organs; and personal ego, ideological affinity, or nationalist attachment. A U.S. citizen with a PRC-market consulting practice, a Taiwanese American whose family in Taiwan runs a business dependent on Mainland supply chains, an American academic whose research center accepts CCP-linked funding, a Hong Kong or Uyghur activist whose relatives remain reachable by PRC security services, and a mainland Chinese graduate student whose parents are subject to hukou-linked pressure all sit under this framework — each with a different vector or mixture. The San Francisco Chinatown case (Sections 03, 05) and the USF case (Section 04) are specific institutional instantiations chosen for their longitudinal record; the framework itself governs conduct, exposure, and coercion, not ethnicity or country of birth.

Naming and answering the folk inference this paper exists to correct

A common intuition, particularly prevalent among U.S. observers skeptical of the PRC, runs as follows: if a Chinese immigrant favored the CCP or the PRC government, that person would have stayed; the fact of settling permanently in the United States is therefore itself evidence of opposition to the regime left behind. This inference is a selection-effect fallacy — it treats a single binary fact (departure) as though it screens the population on the one variable it is being used to prove, when Pew's own data show education and family reunification, not political opposition, as the dominant stated reasons for emigration.

This paper's contribution is not merely to note that the inference is invalid, but to supply the positive mechanism that explains the pattern the fallacy fails to predict: a durable propensity toward PRC-facing institutional and economic engagement despite physical relocation to the United States. The cross-system brokerage rent hypothesis developed in Section 07 does this work. Actors positioned between the United States and a partially integrated but institutionally divergent PRC can earn rents unavailable to actors on either side alone — from information asymmetry, bilingual and bicultural network access, regulatory arbitrage, and status portability. For a large fraction of the population whose institutional visibility this paper documents in San Francisco, the economically rational strategy was never "oppose the regime and cut ties" or "support the regime out of belief" — it was to remain positioned to extract value from the asymmetry between the two systems, which requires maintaining PRC-facing relationships, fluency, and standing regardless of private political conviction. Section 06's M3 (economic pragmatism) and M4 (institutional path dependence) mechanisms, and Section 08's account of why institutional relationships outlive shifts in mass opinion, describe the same underlying logic from different angles. None of this requires, or claims, ideological CCP support; it requires only that PRC-facing access remained more valuable than Taiwan-facing access for many occupations during the high-integration era, which is a testable economic proposition, not a claim about anyone's private loyalty.

Section 02 established the individual-opinion baseline. This section moves to the institutional-incentives link in the causal chain, asking whether San Francisco's Chinatown institutions tracked that national trend or diverged from it — the divergence that Sections 06–08 later explain mechanistically.

San Francisco provides an unusually useful longitudinal case because the traditional Chinatown associations left visible, dated indicators of political alignment. Historical pedigree and iconography of the two flags

The two national flags at issue are not peer contemporary symbols. The flag commonly called the "Taiwan flag" is the national flag of the Republic of China (ROC), adopted by law in 1928 from a design by Lu Hao-tung (陸皓東, 1895) that Sun Yat-sen carried as the revolutionary emblem. From 1928 until the United States switched diplomatic recognition to the People's Republic in 1979, this was the flag of the U.S.-recognized government of China. The PRC flag was adopted in 1949. A San Francisco Chinatown association displaying the ROC flag before 1979 was displaying the flag of the diplomatically recognized Chinese state and of the republican tradition continuous from the 1911 revolution. A post-1949 shift to the PRC flag is therefore the substitution of a specific communist-state symbol for the older republican-Chinese one, not the exchange of two peer contemporary options. (The earlier 1912–1928 Republic used the Five-Coloured Flag [五色旗]; the current ROC design was a 1928 national-flag adoption, not a continuous 1912 pedigree.)

The ROC flag's iconography repays close reading. Its central emblem is a sun in which a central disk is separated from twelve radiating triangular rays by an intervening blue ring — an eclipse of the sun, in which the corona of rays still shines around a ringed core. The twelve rays carry a double resonance: twelve tribes — recalling the twelve tribes of Israel as the constitutive scriptural polity — and twelve knights around the round table, a chivalric fellowship united in service to a common sovereign — the Arthurian round-table figure. Read together, the emblem carries republican, scriptural, and chivalric layers; a reading as "just the Taiwan flag" flattens all three.

Chosen alignment, not passive following
Freedom of choice makes the observed pattern meaningful. The ROC flag remains institutionally available inside the CCBA member system itself — Sue Hing and Hop Wo continue to display it, some associations display neither national flag, and no U.S. law, CCBA rule, or municipal restriction forbids ROC display. An association that adopted the PRC flag did so in an environment where the older republican-Chinese flag was and is a real alternative that other associations still exercise. The PRC-flag adoption is therefore a chosen ideological alignment with a specific communist-state government, not passive following of whichever state Washington happens to recognize — a passive-alignment reading would predict uniform PRC display once U.S. recognition shifted in 1979, and the persistence of ROC display among associations that prefer the older republican tradition falsifies that reading directly.

A resident's-eye view, 2014–2022

The following paragraph is the author's own recollection of living in San Francisco from 2014 to 2022. It is a personal impression, not a survey finding, and should not be read as an estimate of individual political belief among Chinese Americans as a group.

My own sense during those eight years was of an environment saturated with Beijing-aligned institutional visibility — flags, consular events, association leadership, chamber-of-commerce ties — concentrated in a traditional Chinatown establishment that the measured record above independently shows was genuinely PRC-majority in exactly that period. That is an observation about institutional density, a different quantity from individual belief. The separately sourced measure of individual sentiment is the national survey trend above: Chinese-American favorability toward China declining from roughly 68% in 2016 to 41% in 2022–23, with no survey in the record supporting an estimate near 75%. The two measurements answer different questions — how saturated an institutional environment felt to a resident, and what individuals told pollsters they believed — and this paper reports them as two separate data points rather than treating either as a corrected version of the other.

I was, during this period, publicly and openly anti-CCP, and my own experience navigating that institutional environment on those terms — particularly across 2019–2021 — was that it felt unwelcoming rather than neutral. I want to be careful about what that supports: I am not claiming personal targeting rising to the level of the individually documented transnational-repression cases cited elsewhere in this paper (Section 08, Section 09), and I have no independent record comparable to those cases. What I can say is that my subjective experience aligns with the independently documented pattern: independent reporting shows organized pro-Beijing counter-mobilization confronting Hong Kong-solidarity demonstrators across multiple U.S. cities that same August,[95] and the Washington Post's and Hong Kong Democracy Council's independently documented pattern of intimidation against anti-CCP demonstrators recurred as late as the November 2023 APEC summit in this same city.[24, 25] Taken together, that record makes an unfriendly climate for an openly anti-CCP resident of this specific institutional environment during this specific window a documented, not merely felt, possibility — my own account is one data point consistent with a pattern independently reported by others, not a freestanding claim resting on personal impression alone.

The ratio as an institutional vote, and what it means to be on the minority side of it

An association's flag choice is not a private opinion collected by a pollster; it is a discrete, standing decision made by that association's leadership and displayed continuously in shared civic space. Treated that way, the historical sequence in the table below reads less like a survey trend and more like a recorded institutional vote: 3–3–1 (Taiwan–PRC–neither) among the seven CCBA constituent associations in 2004, moving to roughly 70%–30% PRC–Taiwan by the mid-2010s, and to 2 of 7 associations still displaying a Taiwan flag by 2024. Each of those numbers is a count of governing bodies, not people, and it says nothing about how any individual member of those associations privately votes in an actual election — that distinction is the same institution/population separation this section already establishes elsewhere.

What the "vote" framing adds is a different — and, for anyone navigating this specific civic infrastructure, a more consequential — quantity: not what the Chinese-American population as a whole believes, but what an individual actually encounters when working through that infrastructure. A Taiwan-aligned or anti-CCP-aligned resident dealing with Chinatown's traditional association system in 2004 was engaging a body split down the middle. By the late 2010s, the same resident was engaging a system where PRC-oriented leadership, programming, and symbolism had become the numerically dominant environment across the institutions historically organized to represent Chinese San Franciscans collectively. That shift is a real structural fact about which doors are open, which associations offer support or standing, and which public events, resources, and leadership networks a person aligned against Beijing can expect to access inside that particular institutional space — independent of, and not resolved by, the separate national survey finding that individual Chinese-American opinion moved in the opposite direction over the same years. The resident's-eye account earlier in this section describes the lived texture of exactly that asymmetry; the association-level vote count is the structural fact behind it.

04

Campus Symbols, Revenue Dependence & the Institutional Incentive Not to Offend Beijing

Section 03 tested the institutional-incentives link using Chinatown associations. This section applies the same causal-chain logic to a structurally different institution — a university — but with a different mechanism at its center: not brokerage rents earned by individuals, but financial dependence on Chinese-origin tuition creating an institutional incentive for the university itself to accommodate PRC-government sensitivities, including by declining to sponsor, protect, or platform programming and speech that PRC authorities or PRC-government-aligned students would find objectionable.

1. The financial-dependence mechanism

A separate mechanism concerns the university rather than the student. Private universities operate with substantial fixed and quasi-fixed costs: buildings, debt service, tenure and faculty lines, administrative infrastructure, libraries, laboratories, information systems, and student services cannot be reduced in proportion to enrollment on short notice. A country-specific enrollment source can therefore matter far more than its raw percentage of total headcount suggests. The relevant quantity is not simply share of students; it is the contribution of that market to net tuition and contribution margin, its substitutability, and the speed with which costs can adjust if the market disappears.

USF provides unusually strong evidence of material China-market exposure. In September 2016, President Paul J. Fitzgerald stated at a USF China Business Studies Initiative forum that 10% of USF students came from different provinces throughout China. That is a direct contemporary university statement about students from China, not an estimate of Chinese ancestry. Earlier reporting in 2012 counted 781 Chinese nationals among 10,017 students and reported then-full tuition of $36,000, implying a gross sticker-price scale of about $28.1 million before aid and program differences. By Fall 2020, USF reported 525 students from China among 1,272 degree-seeking international students, or about 41.3% of that international population.

The most probative school-level evidence comes from USF's FY2027 Three-Year Financial Plan. The School of Management enrolled 640 Chinese undergraduates and 107 Chinese graduate students in Fall 2015, or 747 students. By Fall 2025, those figures had fallen to 31 and 12, respectively: a decline of 704 students, or approximately 94%. Over the same decade, total School of Management undergraduate enrollment fell from 2,291 to 994 and graduate enrollment from 732 to 411. USF states that the school's enrollment contraction was driven overwhelmingly by the collapse in international enrollment, alongside decline in the legacy College of Professional Studies.

The institutional response was not cosmetic. The same plan reports that four graduate programs were sunset, the remaining graduate portfolio was redesigned, full-time faculty fell from 86 to 47, and professional staff fell from 72 to 37. The loss of Chinese students alone numerically equals about 43.5% of the School of Management's total headcount decline over the period. That ratio is descriptive, not a causal allocation: it does not mean China caused 43.5% of every cut. It does show that the exposure was large enough to be structurally consequential.

This fixed-cost structure explains why an exposure of “only 10%” can be strategically large. If a national cohort supplies roughly one-tenth of enrollment or tuition while most costs cannot fall by one-tenth immediately, the revenue shock is absorbed first by the university's much smaller flexible margin. The result can require disproportionately large adjustments in hiring, sections, program portfolios, services, capital projects, or tuition discounting.

\[ \lambda^{*}=\min\left\{\lambda:\;(1-\lambda)R_0-C_{\mathrm{post\text{-}adjustment}}<0\right\} \]

A useful concept from the companion financial analysis is a revenue-shock tipping threshold, \(\lambda^{*}\): the smallest revenue loss that pushes recurring operations below break-even before management can fully resize costs. The concept is analytically valuable because it explains why a 5–10% source-market exposure can matter disproportionately in a high-fixed-cost university. The companion manuscript's specific numerical calibration of a roughly 5.4% tipping point, however, depends on assumed fixed/variable cost classifications, expense elasticity, spillovers, donor responses, and timing. It should be treated as a stress-test scenario, not an audited USF threshold, until those inputs are independently reconstructed.

\[ \text{Country-market shock} \rightarrow \text{net-tuition / auxiliary pressure} \rightarrow \text{fixed-cost squeeze} \rightarrow \text{program and staffing adjustment} \rightarrow \text{possible effects on breadth, services, outcomes, reputation and yield} \rightarrow \text{feedback} \]

This is a feedback mechanism, not an inevitable death spiral. Universities can offset a country shock through domestic recruitment, replacement international markets, philanthropy, price changes, new programs, reserves, endowment support, or cost restructuring. Rankings are likewise an indirect channel: closing a program does not mechanically lower a U.S. News score, but sustained resource, faculty, retention, outcome, or reputation changes can affect the admissions market and ranking inputs over time.[66]

For the symbolic-accommodation hypothesis, the key consequence is an incentive rather than a demonstrated motive. A university with hundreds of students tied to one foreign market has rational reasons to invest in country-specific recruitment, alumni networks, orientation, language-specific communications, student organizations, cultural programming, and inexpensive symbols of belonging. USF also built institutional China-facing infrastructure during the high-dependence period, including the China Business Studies Initiative and high-level U.S.–China programming. Whether the PRC flag itself was adopted or retained for recruitment, diplomacy, generic internationalism, or some combination cannot be inferred without the flag-policy decision record.[54, 58, 59]

\[ P(A_j)=f\bigl(\text{TuitionExposure}_j,\text{EnrollmentConcentration}_j,\text{RecruitmentPriority}_j,\text{PartnershipValue}_j,\text{Mission},\text{PoliticalCost}\bigr) \]

Here Aj denotes accommodation toward national market j. The hypothesis predicts more country-specific recruitment, services, networks, and symbolic inclusion as material exposure rises, controlling for a university's general level of internationalization. It does not predict that administrators consciously trade political principles for each tuition dollar, nor that accommodation is inherently improper.

The previously modeled estimate of approximately $40.9 million in “Chinese-heritage” tuition is not used as an observed revenue measure because it combines a 2016 statement about students from China, a 2020 international headcount, a later total-enrollment denominator, and 2025–26 tuition. Those years and populations are not commensurable, and “students from China” cannot be redefined as all domestic students of Chinese heritage. The directly supported evidence is narrower: China-origin enrollment reached roughly 10% of the university in 2016; the School of Management contained an unusually large Chinese cohort in 2015; and the later contraction coincided with substantial program and staffing restructuring. The separate $394,000 FY2024 operating-deficit figure is likewise omitted from the quantitative baseline because the cited audited statements report different accounting measures—a $15.873 million decrease in net assets without donor restrictions and a $22.929 million total increase in net assets—without identifying the $394,000 figure as the same accounting concept.

Observed financial exposure, recruitment infrastructure, enrollment concentration, and organizational contraction establish an incentive environment but not the motive for a particular symbolic decision. Administrative friction avoidance, revenue-motivated symbolic accommodation, and reduced comparative political instruction remain causal hypotheses unless contemporaneous policy records, communications, minutes, or testimony connect the incentive to the decision.

2. National evidence: financial dependence is a documented driver of institutional self-censorship on China

This subsection establishes a general, nationally documented mechanism. It does not claim that any specific incident of suppression occurred at USF; the USF-specific evidentiary question is addressed separately in subsection 6 below.

The financial mechanism just quantified for USF is not a theoretical possibility invented for this paper. Human Rights Watch has documented the pattern at the level of the U.S. higher-education sector as a whole: declining state funding for public universities and growing reliance on international tuition and Chinese-government-linked partnerships has made universities “susceptible to Chinese government influence,” producing censorship and self-censorship by administrators and academics who did not want to “irk Chinese authorities.” A GAO review found Chinese government entities provided individual U.S. universities between $1.5 million and over $15 million through Confucius Institute partnerships and related arrangements — funding relationships that came with documented content and hiring conditions.[96, 97]

HRW’s interviews document the mechanism operating exactly as the friction-avoidance hypothesis below predicts: one scholar reported that a senior university administrator asked him “as a personal favor” to decline media interviews critical of Xi Jinping during a presidential visit, citing concern that criticism could damage the university’s standing with Chinese partners and recruitment pipeline. HRW’s research also documents Chinese-government complaints to university officials specifically about hosting speakers PRC diplomats considered “sensitive,” and instances of course material being altered or removed after objections framed in terms of protecting the institution’s China-facing relationships. Separately, in March 2019 — within the same Bay Area region as USF, though at a different institution (UC Berkeley) — mainland-student attendees attempted to shout down speakers at a public event addressing the Xinjiang human-rights crisis, an example of the peer-pressure channel operating alongside the administrative one.[98, 99]

The mechanism HRW documents nationally is structurally identical to the one this section quantifies for USF: an institution financially exposed to a national market has a rational incentive to avoid programming, speech, or symbolism that the government of that market’s students finds objectionable, independent of any individual administrator’s personal views on China. That incentive does not require a conspiracy or explicit instruction from Beijing — it operates through ordinary institutional risk-aversion once the revenue exposure quantified above exists.

4. Institutional-role asymmetry: Chinatown associations and an American university do not carry the same symbolic burden

The meaning of a national flag depends partly on who is displaying it and what that institution exists to do. Some Chinatown organizations are clan, district, hometown, benevolent, business, cultural, or explicitly cross-Strait political organizations whose histories are directly bound up with Chinese communal identity. For such organizations, a PRC or Taiwan flag can have a comparatively direct representational, historical, or constituency function. Even there, the flag does not prove CCP ideology or foreign direction; it may express nationality, ancestral identification, diplomacy, association politics, or a particular cross-Strait position.

The University of San Francisco occupies a different institutional category. USF is a private American university whose published mission emphasizes education, diversity, critical engagement, and learning. It is not a PRC diplomatic mission, a PRC state institution, or a Chinese community association whose constitutive purpose requires representation of the PRC as a sovereign. A standing PRC flag on USF property is therefore more accurately treated as a discretionary internationalization and inclusion choice, not an institutionally necessary expression of USF's own national identity.

That discretionary-inclusion rationale is testable against USF's own conduct: USF recognizes a Taiwanese Student Association alongside its Chinese Students and Scholars Association (Section 04.5), so a generic policy of representing the university's actual student-nationality mix predicts a Taiwan flag at the same installation. The reported PRC-without-Taiwan asymmetry at Lone Mountain (Section 04.3 row; Section 11.C census pending) is evidence against the neutral-internationalization account and toward the institutional-accommodation account this section develops: a truly nationality-representative policy has no reason to include one Chinese-speaking polity's flag while excluding the other's.

This creates a useful Institutional-Role Necessity Gradient. A PRC embassy or consulate has an obvious mission-derived reason to display the PRC flag. A community organization explicitly organized around PRC national representation may also have a strong internal rationale. A broadly Chinese hometown or benevolent association has a contextual but contestable rationale. An American university has the weakest intrinsic nationality-based necessity: its rationale must instead come from a policy of international representation, student inclusion, diplomacy, recruitment, or another university-specific purpose.

5. Peer-network amplification: the same national symbol can appear in student and administrative channels

The institutional-cue hypothesis is sharpened when a national symbol is not encountered only once. USF currently recognizes a Chinese Students and Scholars Association within its culturally focused student-organization structure, alongside a Taiwanese Student Association. If dated photographs, video, CSSA posts, or university records are recovered, the event can be upgraded to a directly observed mobilization variable.

The causal proposition is an interaction rather than a direct flag effect. 6. What is established for USF, and how the specific claim (H10) is testable

This subsection states plainly what this paper's research has and has not found for USF specifically, distinct from the national pattern documented above, and identifies H10 as the specific testable claim that the design in Section 11 is built to decide.

Established: USF carried material, quantifiable financial exposure to China-origin enrollment during the 2012–2018 period (subsection 1), built dedicated China-facing recruitment infrastructure including a China Business Studies Initiative and a China-specific admissions office and WeChat recruitment channel, and displays a PRC flag under administrative authority in a high-salience location (subsection 3). USF also experienced a severe, well-documented contraction in Chinese enrollment and associated program and staffing cuts after 2018 (subsection 1), consistent with the national pattern of universities suffering real financial harm from reduced Chinese enrollment — which is itself further evidence that the underlying incentive to avoid China-market friction was, and for any remaining exposure still is, financially real rather than speculative.

H10 as the specific testable claim: the USF-specific causal question — whether USF administration has cancelled, discouraged, or disadvantaged specific anti-CCP or pro-Taiwan speakers, events, courses, or student organizations because of China-market concerns — is registered as H10 and addressed by the design in Section 11, not asserted here as a discovered incident. This is a deliberate posture, not a research failure. HRW's national research finds that self-censorship of this kind typically leaves no documentary trace, since it takes the form of an event never being proposed, a course topic never being assigned, or an invitation quietly not extended. A search for a specific public cancellation is therefore the wrong instrument: the design instrument in Section 11 — organization-year records, faculty and program-level testimony, comparative programming density across high- and low-China-exposure institutions — is the right one. 7. Observer attribution, public visibility, and the Lone Mountain photography problem

The account below is the author's own first-person testimony, not an independently adjudicated finding. It is presented as evidence of lived experience and institutional pattern, not as proof of any individual's intent or of a coordinated policy.

A separate reported episode concerns motive attribution during a Lone Mountain visit undertaken to check the university's PRC-flag display. According to the firsthand account, ordinary campus photography was interpreted as possible interest in a distant Bay Area security or military facility. The author reports that a vehicle — a blue Tesla consistent with a campus public-safety fleet — sounded its horn and stopped suddenly and closely near him while he was photographing the flag installation, and that the vehicle's occupant subsequently proceeded to the campus public-safety (CASA) building. No one approached the author directly or stated a reason for the stop at the time. The author did not learn of any "hostile reconnaissance" characterization through a direct conversation; his understanding of how the incident was interpreted is inferential, based on the sequence of events rather than on anything communicated to him.

The evidentiary standard here is symmetrical: just as PRC flag ≠ CCP agent, the inference visitor + camera near a flag ≠ evidence of intelligence activity requires its own independent evidentiary bridge, which is absent on the reported facts. The episode is not independently adjudicated and cannot establish institutional policy, discriminatory motive, retaliation, or coordination. Its value for the paper's argument is precisely that it is an ordinary, undramatic sequence — a horn, a close stop, a drive to a security office, no conversation — which is the same low-specificity evidentiary basis this section argues is insufficient to support a hostile-intent inference in either direction. If the university's own PRC flag is displayed as an ordinary inclusion symbol, an interested visitor's photography of that same flag should not, on this evidentiary record, independently trigger security scrutiny.

7.1 The public-information novelty test

The mere visibility of distant Bay Area infrastructure carries little diagnostic value. Lone Mountain is an elevated urban campus, and large portions of San Francisco Bay, bridges, islands, waterfronts, and former military properties are observable from numerous ordinary public locations. Public web maps, aerial imagery, photographs, and other lawful public imagery also make the existence and general exterior geography of such facilities widely accessible. A normal phone photograph whose intended subject is a campus flag therefore adds little or no novel information merely because distant infrastructure happens to fall within the same broad field of view.

Public availability does not make hostile reconnaissance impossible. Intelligence services can aggregate open-source material, use high-resolution optics, study patterns over time, or combine public and non-public information. But those possibilities establish only that public observation can sometimes be dual-use. They do not transform every publicly taken photograph into evidence of espionage. The evidentiary weight depends on what new information was being sought and how specifically the collection behavior targeted it.

\[ \text{Diagnostic value of reconnaissance evidence} \propto \text{Information novelty}\times \text{Target specificity}\times \text{Collection persistence}\times \text{Access circumvention}\times \text{Tasking evidence} \]

When information novelty and target specificity are near zero—for example, an ordinary wide-angle campus photograph taken from a lawful public location—the standalone evidentiary value for an espionage hypothesis should also be low. This is an analytic heuristic, not a rule of criminal law.

7.2 Identify the alleged facility before reasoning from it

The phrase “naval base across the Bay” is itself too imprecise for serious analysis. If the intended reference was former Naval Air Station Alameda, the Navy records that operational activity ended on 30 April 1997. If the reference was former Naval Station Treasure Island, the Navy records closure on 30 September 1997; the site is now a redevelopment and environmental-remediation area, although federal property and Coast Guard functions remain elsewhere in the Bay. If the intended object was an active site such as Coast Guard Island, that is a different factual question, but a distant line of sight still does not establish that it was the photographic target.

The proper sequence is therefore: identify the facility; determine whether it was actually framed or magnified; distinguish ordinary background visibility from deliberate repeated collection; and only then ask whether the behavior is more probable under benign documentation or hostile reconnaissance.

7.3 Recognition–suspicion consistency

A second issue is institutional consistency. USF's display of a PRC national flag does not mean the university believes the PRC government is benign, democratic, or strategically aligned with the United States. Universities can display the flags of states with which the United States has serious security disputes because the display may represent students, nationality, diplomatic recognition, or international inclusion. It would therefore be a false dichotomy to argue that a university cannot both display the PRC flag and take PRC state-security risks seriously.

But the reverse proposition matters equally. If the institution itself uses the PRC flag as an ordinary symbol of national inclusion, then PRC-related identity, language, or interest in that same publicly displayed symbol cannot logically function as sufficient evidence of hostile intent. A security inference must be supplied by conduct-specific evidence. Otherwise the institution risks a recognition–suspicion asymmetry: PRC national identity is normalized when useful for international inclusion, recruitment, and ceremony, but treated as intrinsically suspicious when an individual examines the institution's own PRC symbolism.

\[ \text{Institutional inclusion of PRC nationality} \not\Rightarrow \text{PRC regime endorsement} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \text{PRC-related identity or inquiry} \not\Rightarrow \text{hostile state intent} \]

The proper inferential sequence is Bayesian rather than accusatory. A routine visitor/flag-documentation explanation and a hostile-reconnaissance explanation are competing hypotheses. Evidence should update between them according to how specifically it is predicted by each. A camera aimed at the campus flag is strongly expected under the first hypothesis. The hostile-reconnaissance hypothesis becomes materially stronger only with behavior such as persistent targeting of a sensitive facility, unusual magnification or pattern collection, access circumvention, concealed tasking, or a foreign-principal relationship.

Security personnel are not barred from asking neutral questions when conduct creates a genuine site-specific concern. The methodological and civil-liberties boundary is crossed when a low-specificity fact—appearance, ancestry, language, or ordinary public photography—is converted into a high-specificity accusation without the missing evidentiary bridge. The same evidentiary principle applies throughout: observable symbols and identities are not substitutes for evidence of hidden motive.

7. The lived experience of being a visible dissenter in a pro-PRC institutional environment

Consider the predicament of an individual who finds themselves publicly at odds with the PRC in a community where pro-PRC sentiment predominates among those who hold the levers of advancement and approbation. In such an environment, where the majority of gatekeepers have aligned themselves, whether out of sincere conviction or pragmatic calculation, with a pro-Beijing orientation, the costs of dissent can be severe — and often imposed through mechanisms that leave little documentary trace.

The dissenting individual may find that, no matter how estimable their accomplishments or how unimpeachable their conduct, the ordinary tokens of recognition that mark a upward career trajectory are conspicuously withheld. Stellar academic performance goes unheralded; professional attainments and community contributions pass without the customary acknowledgement. While others are lauded and advanced, the dissenter languishes.

Worse, any misstep or error on the dissenter's part, no matter how trivial, becomes occasion for censure and opposition far out of proportion to the offense. The aim is not to address the lapse constructively, but to inflict maximum reputational damage, to paint the dissenter as congenitally unfit and undeserving of a place in the community. Faced with such unremitting hostility, the dissenter soon learns that even the most minor slip may be weaponized to catastrophic effect.

Most insidious of all are the efforts to entrap and ensnare the dissenter in compromising situations, to manufacture "mistakes" for which they may be endlessly upbraided and stigmatized. No stratagem is too underhanded if it lends itself to portraying the dissenter as blameworthy and beyond the pale. The apparatus of shunning and exclusion masquerades as the community's natural response to an erring member's misbehavior, concealing its fundamental purpose as an instrument of political coercion.

The parallels to the Cultural Revolution are impossible to miss: the zealous ideological gatekeeping, the capricious withholding of advancement, the outsized punishments for thought-crime, the ceaseless demands for political capitulation as the price of enfranchisement. Indeed, for the dissenter, the supposed protections and privileges of the institutional context — the university, the workplace, the civic association — are inverted, weaponized to enforce conformity and to inflict devastating personal and professional harm should they persist in heterodoxy.

Yet we must resist the urge to reduce this predicament to a simple morality tale or an abstract struggle of conscience. The stakes for the dissenter are not merely philosophical but acutely material, determining in the most concrete terms whether they will be able to learn, to work, to advance, and to thrive in their chosen field and community. It is this cold calculus, as much as any idealistic motive, that must inform the dissenter's every word and deed.

In this unsparing light, we are confronted with the true gravity of the choice facing those who would speak truth to power in an environment where power has so distorted the truth. It is not simply a decision to dissent, but a commitment to place at risk the fundamental building blocks of one's life and livelihood. And yet, even in the midst of this moral quagmire, the words of Saint Teresa of Calcutta ring out with startling immediacy: "Do it anyway." Those who take up this burden do not do so naively, imagining themselves immune to consequence. Rather, they are propelled by the unassailable belief that there is no other course of action that their conscience can abide. Like St. Teresa, they hold fast to the principle that one must do what is right, whatever price may be exacted, in the conviction that the ultimate victory will be a moral one, even if it comes at great personal cost.

That there are individuals prepared to make such a choice — who consider bearing witness a duty worth any sacrifice, even knowing the steep toll it may incur — stands as an enduring testament to the indomitable power of moral conviction. But it must also spur us to examine, with relentless honesty, the structures of incentive and intimidation that make such profound acts of moral courage necessary to begin with. We have to ask ourselves: what does it reveal about our institutions, about our communities, when to live with integrity is to paint a target on one's own back? If we fail to grapple with the full weight of that question, we risk becoming complicit in the very systems of coercion and control that we claim to reject. In a situation of such moral gravity, neutrality is not an option. As a university, and as a society, we must decide whether we are willing to offer more than mere words of support to those who dissent, and to commit ourselves to the difficult task of dismantling the apparatus of enforced conformity, wherever we may find it. To do any less is to fail not only the brave few who speak out, but the very principles we claim to stand for. The true test of our convictions lies not in the lofty ideals we profess, but in our willingness to act in their defense, even at cost to ourselves. This is the summons that the dissenter's courage places before us — the call to moral action that we ignore at our own peril.

11. Institution-level remedies (Section 09), not student-level suspicion

The friction-avoidance hypothesis targets institutional incentives facing university administration. The remedy that follows is administration-level: the transparency and symmetric-enforcement toolkit specified in Section 09 — disclosed funding relationships, viewpoint-neutral event and speaker policy, and published flag-and-symbol criteria. Individual Chinese or Chinese-American students are outside its scope; their presence on campus is not, per Section 00, evidence of any political position. The experimental record supports this allocation directly: Fan, Pan, Shao, and Xu find that anti-Chinese discrimination among Chinese undergraduates in the United States reduces support for political reform and increases support for authoritarian rule, while non-racist criticism of the Chinese government produces no such backlash. Student-level suspicion would therefore be both wrong in principle and counterproductive in effect; institutional-level accountability is the mechanism that actually works.

Extending the window by inference: why the elevated period plausibly runs from May 2019 through 2020–21

No survey instrument directly measures monthly institutional mobilization confidence, so no dataset can hand us an exact peak date. The argument below is a first-principles inference from documented, dated events — not a claim to have found a hidden measurement.

Treating May 2019 as the plausible onset, rather than October as the plausible close, follows from ordinary causal reasoning about how organizational confidence is built and sustained rather than switched on for a single autumn. Three chained facts support this: (1) the 3–14 May rhetorical escalation supplied the ideological vocabulary — "people's war" — that reframed routine trade friction as civilizational confrontation, which is the kind of framing that mobilizes organizations rather than individuals reconsidering a survey answer; (2) the 17–18 August cross-city counter-mobilization shows that vocabulary had already been operationalized into coordinated street presence within three months, well before the September/October San Francisco events this section documents; and (3) Hong Kong's National Security Law was not enacted until June 2020, meaning the underlying grievance driving PRC-aligned institutional mobilization did not resolve or de-escalate at the end of 2019 — it continued, and the pandemic-era rise in U.S. anti-Chinese sentiment supplied a second, independent motive for PRC-aligned institutions to close ranks defensively through 2020–21. Deductively, an institutional-confidence variable driven by an unresolved ideological trigger, a demonstrated capacity for rapid mobilization, and a compounding defensive motive should be modeled as elevated across a multi-year window, not as a single-season spike that resets by November 2019. The October 2019 close date in the table above is a documentary boundary — it is where this paper's discrete, dated SF events happen to end — not a claim that institutional confidence itself declined immediately afterward.

This inference is falsifiable in the same way the rest of this paper's claims are: it predicts that an organization-year panel of the kind specified in Section 10 should show San Francisco PRC-oriented institutional mobilization remaining elevated, not declining, across 2020–21 relative to the pre-2019 baseline, and declining only where an independent shock (such as pandemic-era program contraction) reduces institutional capacity rather than institutional will. That prediction has not yet been tested against a reproducible panel; it is presented here as the paper's best current inference, not as an established finding.

Six Causal Mechanisms

Sections 02–05 documented that a divergence exists between national opinion and San Francisco institutional behavior. This section formalizes the network-level mechanisms in the causal chain capable of producing and sustaining that divergence.

The same observable act—a PRC flag, a consular banquet, a lobbying position, a delegation, or a hometown-association membership—can arise from distinct causal mechanisms. Six pathways are analytically separable and may operate alone or in combination. Each pathway is defined by exposure, vulnerability, or opportunity structure, not by ethnicity or country of birth: the mechanisms apply to Chinese Americans and PRC-origin actors as the paper's case study documents them, and equally to Taiwanese Americans, Americans of any background, and persons from any country of origin who share the relevant exposure or vulnerability. Section 00's scope callout gives the actor-agnostic frame; the six mechanisms below formalize it.

M1 · MIGRATION SELECTION

Exit for reasons unrelated to politics

Education, family, professional opportunity, and income can generate migration without anti-regime selection. Emigration is therefore weak evidence of homeland-regime preference.

M2 · CIVILIZATIONAL / NATIONAL IDENTITY

Ancestral attachment without communist ideology

Cultural pride, national identity, and support for a homeland's prosperity may coexist with criticism of authoritarian rule. Host-state citizenship and ancestral-country attachment are not mutually exclusive. This mechanism covers multiple distinct ancestral-attachment traditions — including the republican-Chinese tradition continuous from the 1911 revolution and carried by the 1928 ROC national flag (Section 03), the modern Taiwanese-nativist tradition, and mainland-PRC national identity — each of which coexists differently with regime evaluation.

M3 · ECONOMIC / NETWORK PRAGMATISM

Opportunity asymmetry and cross-border utility

For many sectors during 1990–2019, the scale of PRC markets and institutions created larger potential opportunity sets than Taiwan. Alignment can therefore be instrumental rather than ideological.[14, 15, 34, 35]

M4 · INSTITUTIONAL PATH DEPENDENCE

Relationships outlive shifts in mass opinion

Consular contacts, delegations, business introductions, donors, media relations, and leadership interlocks create durable organizational capital that does not reset when public opinion changes.

M5 · FOREIGN-STATE CULTIVATION

Top-down influence and co-option

U.S. government and independent research documents PRC party-state efforts to cultivate selected diaspora leaders and organizations. The existence of this mechanism does not justify treating the diaspora collectively as suspect.[27, 28]

M6 · COERCIVE CONNECTIVITY

Family, asset, business, and professional leverage

Threats against relatives or other interests in China can create self-censorship or compliance in particular cases. FBI and transnational-repression research document the mechanism; population prevalence remains unknown.

\[\text{Diaspora alignment}=\text{bottom-up preference}+\text{institutional incentives}+\text{top-down cultivation}+\text{coercive constraints}\]

China has a large genuine private sector. Official 2025 data reported more than 57 million registered private enterprises, more than 92% of businesses, with official estimates above 60% of GDP and 80% of urban employment. The relevant mechanism is therefore not the absence of private enterprise, but the possibility that party-state regulatory, political, and financial power increases the value of governmental relationships in particular commercial settings.

Cross-System Brokerage Rents

Of the six mechanisms in Section 06, M3 (economic pragmatism) and M4 (institutional path dependence) admit the most direct empirical test. This section develops that test as the paper's central network-level economic mechanism.

The central economic mechanism is cross-system brokerage: actors positioned between economically connected but institutionally divergent systems can earn rents from information asymmetry, linguistic advantage, network access, regulatory differences, and status portability.

\[\text{Value of intermediary}\;\propto\;\text{institutional difference}\times\text{ability to bridge the systems}\]
Complete separationFew transactions cross the boundary; brokerage opportunities collapse.
Partial integration + divergenceTransactions remain possible while information and institutional differences stay large; brokerage rents can be maximized.
Complete integrationInformation asymmetry and boundary-specific advantages shrink; brokerage rents erode.

\[Opportunity_{PRC}\gg Opportunity_{Taiwan}\quad\text{for many sectors during the high-integration era}\]

This inequality is a scale hypothesis, not a moral claim. During the high-integration era, PRC market size, state capacity, commercial networks, and cross-border capital flows could make PRC-facing access more economically valuable than Taiwan-facing access for particular occupations. H1 · Cross-Border Rent Hypothesis

Economic and social exposure predicts engagement preference

Chinese Americans whose income, professional status, social capital, family interests, or business opportunities depend more heavily on access to China should, other things equal, exhibit more favorable attitudes toward China and stronger preferences for continued U.S.–China engagement.

H2 · Exposure Gradient

Higher China-linked exposure predicts greater aversion to confrontation

The greater the China-dependent economic or network exposure, the stronger the predicted preference for avoiding policies that threaten cross-border access.

H3 · Decoupling Shock

Instrumental alignment should be more elastic than ideological alignment

As U.S.–China relations deteriorate after 2018–20 and expected brokerage rents fall, China favorability should decline more among actors whose earlier alignment was primarily opportunity-driven than among actors whose preferences are ideological or nationalist.

\[U_i=Income_i^{China}+NetworkPrestige_i+FamilyAccess_i+CareerOpportunity_i+\cdots\]
\[E_i=w_1IncomeChina_i+w_2BusinessChina_i+w_3TravelChina_i+w_4FamilyChina_i+w_5AssetsChina_i+w_6InstitutionalTies_i\]
\[P(FavorableChina_i)=f(E_i,Generation_i,Age_i,Education_i,Income_i,Politics_i,MigrationReason_i,\ldots)\]

Self-selection and rent-induced alignment are competing causal directions in cross-section, and cross-section alone cannot separate them. The identification does not depend on cross-section. H3 (Decoupling Shock) makes a specific prediction about differential dynamics: if alignment is instrumentally driven by expected brokerage rents, then a large exogenous reduction in those rents should be followed by faster attitudinal decline among the previously most-exposed actors than among actors whose preferences are ideological or nationalist. The 2018–2020 rupture — the trade confrontation, the collapse of the Chinese-investment peak from the 2016 high, the Hong Kong crisis, the pandemic — supplies precisely that exogenous shock and is not endogenous to any individual's prior preference. Under selection-only, prior preferences should be little affected by the shock and any decline should be uniform across exposure levels; under the rent mechanism, the decline should be steeper where prior exposure was greater. Section 02 documents the aggregate decline (from 68% in 2016 to 41% in 2022–23); Section 08 documents that institutional alignment, with its accumulated relationship capital, decays more slowly than individual opinion. The predicted differential — instrumental faster than ideological, opinion faster than institution — is precisely the pattern the record shows. Section 11.E specifies the event-study design that quantifies this differential across the shocks named above; Section 11.F states the conditions under which the mechanism would be falsified. The endogeneity concern therefore names the reason cross-sectional evidence would be insufficient — a reason the paper's identification strategy already accepts and works around by design.

Legal Implications: Foreign Direction, Coercion, Transparency & Neutrality

The causal chain's final link is legal: having documented institutional persistence and divergence in Section 08, this section asks when those patterns could ever cross into a legally cognizable foreign-agency or coercion claim, and — just as importantly — when they cannot.

Conduct, not identity
Political agreement ≠ foreign agency. Nationality ≠ foreign agency. A flag ≠ foreign direction. The legally relevant variables are relationship, direction or control, covered conduct, disclosure duties, discriminatory treatment, misuse of protected records, threats, coercion, stalking, and other independently provable acts.[31, 67, 81]

1. Existing U.S. law: what each framework actually reaches

Foreign-influence law does not create a general offense of being sympathetic to a foreign country. FARA is principally a disclosure regime for specified activities performed at the order, request, direction, or control of a foreign principal, subject to exemptions. Section 951 is a distinct criminal notification statute for persons acting in the United States as agents of foreign governments. Neither statute is established by favorable views of China, a PRC flag, Chinese-language activity, consular attendance at a public event, or ordinary academic/business engagement standing alone.[31, 67, 81]

2. Transnational repression: conduct-specific legal analysis

The FBI defines transnational repression as foreign-government conduct reaching into the United States to intimidate, silence, coerce, harass, or harm diaspora and exile communities. Reported tactics include stalking, threats, family pressure abroad, cyber activity, coercive return efforts, assault, and attempted kidnapping. The category identifies a threat pattern; it does not create a presumption that ordinary diaspora organizations, students, or political advocates act for a foreign government.[29, 71]

Where a concrete incident involves repeated following, a credible threat, threatened injury, coerced transfer of property, interference with a legally protected right, or disclosure of protected institutional records, the appropriate legal analysis begins with the elements of the relevant statute. The nationality of the alleged actor is not a substitute for those elements.[74, 75, 76, 77, 78, 73, 69]

Legal distinction
The legally durable line is not “China-friendly versus China-critical.” It is independent versus foreign-directed, transparent versus undisclosed, voluntary versus coerced, viewpoint-neutral versus discriminatory, and public information versus protected or unlawfully obtained information.

The Stakes of Inaction: Envisioning an Unchallenged CCP

The preceding sections document the extent and mechanisms of CCP-aligned influence within key U.S. institutions, and the real costs borne by those who resist it. This section asks what an unchallenged continuation of that influence would mean. It draws on two bodies of evidence that carry different evidentiary weight, and the two are kept typographically distinct below: independently documented PLA basing expansion and BRI dual-use infrastructure, asserted directly and cited to primary and institutional sources; and a reading of the disputed Chi Haotian document, presented strictly as a contested-authorship hypothesis whose claims this paper has not been able to authenticate.

A. Documented: PLA overseas basing and BRI dual-use infrastructure

China operates two confirmed overseas military bases. The PLA Support Base in Djibouti opened in 2017. The Ream Naval Base joint logistics and training center in Cambodia was inaugurated in April 2025 and includes a 650-meter pier able to berth aircraft-carrier-sized vessels — capacity Cambodia's own navy does not use.[100] The Department of Defense's 2025 report to Congress states that Beijing is weighing additional overseas logistics sites across some combination of 21 countries in the Indian Ocean, Africa, and the Western Pacific, and that this basing effort advances alongside a six-carrier expansion target by 2035.[100] RAND's site-selection research, which evaluated 108 candidate host nations, finds the PLA's basing criteria driven primarily by economic and logistical utility rather than by preparation to strike U.S. forces; a companion RAND study of Chinese military writings concludes the PLA currently has neither the intent nor the capability to conduct offensive operations from its overseas bases through at least 2030, though it recommends the U.S. government continue monitoring for a shift.[101] This distinction matters: the basing network is a documented and expanding fact; its wartime offensive utility is a separately assessed and currently negative finding, not an open question this paper resolves.

BRI-financed ports show a documented dual-use pattern independent of any Chinese base agreement. C4ADS's analysis of fifteen BRI-funded port projects across the Indo-Pacific and CSIS's research on Chinese military access in the Indian Ocean both find that Chinese-financed port infrastructure is regularly built to specifications exceeding ordinary commercial need — deepwater berths capable of accommodating naval vessels, reinforced access roads, and fuel and cold-storage capacity — that match PLA operational requirements and support Chinese naval refueling and resupply without requiring a return to Chinese territory.[102, 103] Beijing's public framing of the Belt and Road Initiative as a purely economic program does not account for this pattern, which multiple independent research organizations have documented using open-source and satellite-imagery methods.

Evidentiary status: contested authorship, conditional reasoning
The subsection below reasons from the disputed "Chi Haotian speech." This paper's own provenance and stylometric investigation treats that document's authorship and authenticity as unresolved. Nothing in Subsection B is this paper's assertion of CCP intent — it is what the document would imply if it were established as genuine, offered because a document of contested origin still helps a reader see the shape of the risk the documented facts above would carry under a worst-case reading.

B. Conditional: if the Chi Haotian document's claims are credited

If the Chi Haotian document is genuine, its claims describe CCP ambitions reaching past regional hegemony toward the long-term displacement of the American population and the opening of North America to Chinese settlement and resource extraction. On that reading — and only on that reading — the documented PLA basing network and BRI dual-use ports described in Subsection A would function not as ordinary power projection but as forward staging for that outcome, and the institutional footholds traced in Sections 03–08 would represent the erosion of the target population's capacity to recognize or resist it. A CCP biological attack, in this same conditional scenario, would be harder to attribute and to counter if the party's influence reached into American epidemiology, law enforcement, telecommunications, and government. And a public exposed long enough to United Front elite cooptation and public-opinion operations might, on this reading, come to treat a gradual takeover as unavoidable, or even preferable to a fractious domestic politics.

Every sentence in this subsection depends on crediting a document this paper has not been able to authenticate. It is included as the clearest illustration of what would be at stake if the document is genuine, not as a finding this paper makes.

That these scenarios remain, for now, hypothetical rather than realized owes something to the researchers, officials, and journalists who have pressed the documented facts in Subsection A into public view despite real professional risk. The CCP's institutional leverage remains substantial, and the people working against it still face real headwinds. What this section adds to the paper's argument is narrow: the documented expansion of PLA basing and BRI dual-use infrastructure is real and growing regardless of how the contested document is resolved, and it is that documented reality — not the conditional scenario in Subsection B — that should drive the policy response this paper proposes in Sections 09 and 13.

Empirical Strategy, Identification & Falsification

Every mechanism and hypothesis introduced in Sections 01–09 requires a research design capable of falsifying it. This section specifies those designs so that the causal chain remains testable rather than merely narrated.

A. Evidence provenance and source weighting

Primary statutes, official institutional records, audited financial statements, and original survey reports receive the greatest evidentiary weight. Government and advocacy sources are used for attributed claims or event occurrence unless independently corroborated; state-media crowd estimates and causal characterizations are not treated as neutral measurements. Survey findings retain their original question wording, sampling frame, and methodological limits. This weighting rule separates evidence of occurrence from evidence of motive, prevalence, or causation.

B. San Francisco organization-year panel, 1990–2026

The primary unit of observation is the organization-year. A 100–300-organization panel can separate durable institutional alignment from one-off events and from individual public opinion.

D. U.S. public survey experiment: testing the Exit = Opposition intuition

A nationally representative experiment can randomize why a hypothetical China-born adult migrated to the United States: graduate education, economic opportunity, family reunification, political persecution, or an unspecified reason. Respondents then estimate the person’s likely views of the PRC government, CCP, Taiwan, U.S.–China restrictions, and continuing China-linked professional relationships. Result
Every objection above fails against the claim as actually stated. What each objection defeats is a stronger claim the paper explicitly refuses to make. Two recent flag studies confirm the specification the paper already applies. Dinas, Martínez, and Valentim (2024) show that longitudinal changes in public flag display reflect social-norm change and expressive behavior rather than a one-for-one change in private preferences. Jonathan Hassid's cross-national flag-density work reports null relationships between flag exposure and several presumed measures of political power. Both findings validate the paper's specification: flags are coded as symbolic display and paired with surveys, events, organizational data, and network structure whenever preference is the object of inference. The results defeat any attempt to read the paper as inferring private belief from public symbolism, and equally defeat any attempt to dismiss flag data as uninformative about institutional alignment — the estimand for which they are the correct measurement.

Strategic Framework: Countering CCP Influence, 2026–2036

The preceding analysis has laid bare the nature, extent, and mechanisms of CCP influence within critical American institutions, and sounded a stark warning about the potential consequences of allowing this influence to metastasize unchecked. If the United States is to safeguard its sovereignty, its values, and indeed the integrity of its very political system, it is imperative that these findings be translated with utmost urgency into a concrete, whole-of-society strategy for identifying, exposing, and excising CCP encroachment wherever it is found.

A. Quantifying the demographic and fiscal window

China's population peaked at 1.4126 billion in 2021 and has fallen for four consecutive years, to roughly 1.405 billion at the end of 2025.[104] The UN's 2024 World Population Prospects projects China will record the largest absolute population loss of any country in the 2024–2054 window (−204 million) and, if current fertility persists, the largest through 2100 (−786 million, returning to a population size last seen in the late 1950s).[105] Births fell to a record low of 7.92 million in 2025, down 17 percent year-on-year, against a total fertility rate near 1.0 — well below the 2.1 replacement level.[104]

The aging half of that trend is what converts a population problem into a fiscal one. China's 65-and-over population stood at 209.78 million (14.9 percent of the total) in 2022; official projections put it above 300 million by 2033 and above 400 million by 2050, peaking near 436 million around 2057 — an increase of roughly 100 million elderly people within about a decade.[106] The working-age population (15–64), by contrast, peaked in 2015 and has been declining since.[107] Two standard demographic ratios quantify the resulting strain:

For comparison, the United States is projected to retain more than 2.5 working-age adults per person 65 and older in 2050 — a materially larger support base for the same-aged pension and health-care obligations.[107]

A simple growth-accounting identity connects this demographic arithmetic to the GDP projections below. Using the standard Solow decomposition, output growth is approximately the sum of labor-force growth, capital's contribution, and productivity growth:

Growth-accounting identity
gY ≈ gL + α·gK + gA, where gY = GDP growth, gL = labor-force growth, gK = capital-stock growth, gA = total-factor-productivity growth, and α ≈ capital's share of output. With gL now negative and China's investment-to-GDP ratio already near 42 percent — leaving limited room for further capital deepening — sustaining historical growth rates requires gA to do proportionally more work than it has in any prior decade of China's development.

The realized numbers track that arithmetic. China's National Bureau of Statistics reported 5.0 percent GDP growth in 2025 (independent estimates put the figure closer to 4.0 percent); the IMF projects 4.4 percent for 2026 and 4.2–4.3 percent for 2027, with its longer-range review flagging a slide toward roughly 3.3 percent by 2029 on aging and slowing productivity growth.[109, 110] The fiscal side compounds the same pressure. A standard debt-dynamics identity gives the mechanism:

Debt-dynamics identity
Δd ≈ (r − g)·d − pb, where d = public debt as a share of GDP, r = the effective interest rate on that debt, g = nominal GDP growth, and pb = the primary budget balance (surplus positive). As g falls toward the low single digits while r holds roughly constant, (r − g) turns from a favorable to an unfavorable term, so the debt ratio rises automatically unless pb improves to offset it.

China's official 2026 budget deficit target is 4.0 percent of GDP; the IMF's augmented measure, which folds in local-government financing-vehicle borrowing, puts the true public-sector deficit closer to 14 percent of GDP, a level the Fund has flagged as unsustainable absent local-government debt restructuring and reduced industrial subsidies.[111] The IMF's 2026 review names a "deeper-than-expected" property-sector downturn as the primary domestic risk to the entire trajectory.[111]

Scope condition: what the arithmetic does and does not establish
This subsection's numbers are drawn from the UN Population Division, RAND, IMF, and China's own National Bureau of Statistics, and are not in serious dispute. What is in serious dispute is what follows from them strategically. Predictions of Chinese state collapse have a poor track record — Gordon Chang's The Coming Collapse of China (2001) is the canonical example of a forecast the subsequent quarter-century did not bear out — and demographic aging alone has not produced state collapse in Japan or South Korea, both further along the same curve. A separate and better-evidenced strand of IR scholarship (Beckley and Brands' "peak power" or "danger zone" thesis) argues the opposite mechanism from the one this paper's title analogy implies: a power that senses its relative window closing may become more risk-acceptant in the near term, not more passive, particularly on Taiwan. Any strategy built on these numbers should therefore treat demographic and fiscal exhaustion as a slow-moving structural constraint worth exploiting through sustained, lawful transparency and accountability measures — not as a basis for assuming Beijing will grow more cautious while that window is still open.

To be effective, this strategy must operate simultaneously on multiple fronts. At the level of national policy, it will require a comprehensive review of existing laws and regulations pertaining to foreign influence, electoral integrity, educational autonomy, and transparency in media and technology platforms. Where necessary, new legislative and executive measures must be crafted to close loopholes, strengthen disclosure requirements, and provide law enforcement and counterintelligence agencies with the tools they need to identify and disrupt CCP influence operations.

Crucially, any such measures must be designed and implemented with scrupulous adherence to constitutional principles and civil liberties. The goal must be to target foreign state actors and their witting proxies, not to stigmatize or harass individuals on the basis of ethnicity, national origin, or lawful political expression. Proper oversight, due process safeguards, and provisions for public transparency and redress will be essential to maintaining the moral high ground and preventing the fight against CCP influence from devolving into witch hunts or racial profiling.

At the institutional level, a top-to-bottom audit of universities, think tanks, media organizations, and corporations will be necessary to map the complete scope of CCP penetration and to develop tailored countermeasures. The goal should be to establish, as a condition of continued public funding and accreditation, robust internal safeguards against foreign influence, including mandatory disclosure of all financial ties to CCP-linked entities, strict firewalls between academic and political decision-making, and ironclad protections for freedom of inquiry and expression.

In tandem, a major investment in public education and awareness will be crucial to inoculating the broader American society against CCP "United Front" tactics of elite cooptation, disinformation, and psychological manipulation. This should include the development of K-12 and university curricula on CCP influence strategies, media literacy programs to help citizens identify and resist foreign propaganda, and public information campaigns to puncture the CCP's carefully cultivated veneer of benign "soft power." Only a well-informed and vigilant citizenry can serve as a durable bulwark against subversion.

Above all, the United States must approach this challenge in a spirit of national cohesion and resolve. While vigorous democratic debate over the specific means is both healthy and necessary, there can be no equivocation or partisan rancor over the fundamental ends. To confront so sophisticated and determined an adversary, Americans of all political persuasions must find common cause in the defense of our bedrock institutions and values. This is not a struggle that any faction can afford to sit out for temporary tactical advantage.

The road ahead will be long and arduous, and the costs of inaction are steep indeed. But if the revelations of this study teach us anything, it is that there can be no substitute for moral courage in the face of existential threat. Just as it has fallen to a intrepid few to sound the initial alarm, so will it now depend on the exertions of the many to see this struggle through to a successful conclusion. The task is daunting, but the stakes could not be higher. With clarity of purpose, unity of effort, and an unwavering commitment to our democratic ideals, it is one that the United States and its allies can — and must — win.
