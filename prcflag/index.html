Exit Without Opposition

Migration selection, diaspora alignment, cross-system brokerage rents, and foreign-state influence in the United States—with San Francisco’s Chinese-American institutional ecology as a longitudinal case, 1990–2026.

Core proposition

Migration from an authoritarian state does not identify a migrant’s political preference toward that state’s regime. Exit may follow education, family, professional opportunity, income, lifestyle, or political pressure. After migration, homeland identity, cross-border economic incentives, institutional networks, foreign-state cultivation, and coercive connectivity may continue to shape preferences and behavior.

Core Thesis: Exit ≠ Opposition

Migration–Preference Identification Error
Moving to the United States is not evidence that an immigrant opposed the government of the country left behind.

A common intuition treats emigration from an authoritarian state as a revealed preference against the regime. That inference is not identified by migration itself. For Chinese immigrants surveyed by Pew Research Center, the two leading stated reasons for migration were education (38%) and family reunification (31%). Political escape is therefore not a valid default assumption for the population as a whole.

The analytical correction is symmetrical. Favorable opinion of China, Chinese-language use, cultural pride, a PRC flag, or support for U.S.–China engagement is not evidence of foreign agency. Regime preference, national identity, commercial incentives, legal agency, and coercion are separate variables and require separate evidence.

\[P(\text{opposes CCP}\mid \text{emigrated from China}) \neq 1\]

More generally, migration status cannot identify homeland-regime preference because the migration event is generated by heterogeneous causes:

\[E = E_{education}+E_{family}+E_{economic}+E_{professional}+E_{lifestyle}+E_{political}+\cdots\]

Causal architecture

The argument proceeds across four levels that must remain analytically distinct. At the individual level, migration selection explains why exit does not identify regime opposition. At the network level, cross-system brokerage, family ties, status, and coercive connectivity can preserve incentives for homeland engagement. At the institutional level, Chinatown associations and universities can develop durable PRC-facing relationships for reasons that include constituency representation, internationalization, revenue exposure, and organizational path dependence. At the symbolic level, flags and public events reveal institutional choices and mobilization but do not identify hidden ideology.

The 2018–20 rupture then supplies the temporal test: if mass opinion turns downward faster than established organizational relationships, the resulting divergence is evidence of institutional persistence, not proof that every participant shares the same political motive. The legal analysis enters only after this separation, asking when protected advocacy becomes foreign-directed activity and when ordinary scrutiny becomes unsupported attribution or discriminatory suspicion.

\[\text{Exit}\rightarrow\text{Networks}\rightarrow\text{Institutional incentives}\rightarrow\text{Symbolic environment}\rightarrow\text{2019 shock}\rightarrow\text{Persistence / divergence}\rightarrow\text{legal boundary}\]

Campus Symbols, Regime Legitimacy & the Missed Democratic-Socialization Opportunity

Institutional-equivalence cue hypothesis
When a U.S. university places a PRC national flag in a standing international-flag installation—especially at an administrative core—the display can communicate legitimate membership in the campus community. The unresolved question is whether some students also generalize that legitimate national representation into normative equivalence between political regimes.

No reviewed study directly estimates a causal effect of PRC-flag exposure on CCP support among students at U.S. universities. The narrower proposition is that administratively authorized national symbols can function as legitimacy cues, while democratic political learning among PRC-origin students is not automatic. The empirical question is whether an uncontextualized equal-footing display changes perceived regime equivalence or reduces the salience of institutional differences. Individual political preference cannot be inferred from exposure to the symbol.

1. Why the university setting matters

International education is not merely geographic relocation. A university is an unusually dense environment of political resocialization: students encounter different rules governing speech, academic inquiry, association, elections, due process, institutional accountability, public criticism, and access to information. For students arriving from an authoritarian system, those differences can become objects of direct comparison—but only if they are noticed, understood, and interpreted rather than treated as background scenery.

Research on Chinese students and scholars in the United States shows why passive exposure should not be assumed to produce democratic learning. Han and Chen found that social-science education and consumption of foreign media were associated with more supportive attitudes toward liberal democracy, while the effect of simply living longer in the United States was mixed. More recent qualitative work finds that Chinese international students can continue to avoid political expression after migration through pragmatic disengagement, veiled allegiance, or closeted activism. The implication is not that universities should prescribe a political conclusion; it is that comparative civic learning requires an intellectual pathway.[40, 41]

\[\text{Democratic learning}\neq f(\text{physical presence in the United States alone})\]

3. Institutional-role asymmetry: Chinatown associations and an American university do not carry the same symbolic burden

The meaning of a national flag depends partly on who is displaying it and what that institution exists to do. Some Chinatown organizations are clan, district, hometown, benevolent, business, cultural, or explicitly cross-Strait political organizations whose histories are directly bound up with Chinese communal identity. For such organizations, a PRC or Taiwan flag can have a comparatively direct representational, historical, or constituency function. Even there, the flag does not prove CCP ideology or foreign direction; it may express nationality, ancestral identification, diplomacy, association politics, or a particular cross-Strait position.

The University of San Francisco occupies a different institutional category. USF is a private American university whose published mission emphasizes education, diversity, critical engagement, and learning. It is not a PRC diplomatic mission, a PRC state institution, or a Chinese community association whose constitutive purpose requires representation of the PRC as a sovereign. A standing PRC flag on USF property is therefore more accurately treated as a discretionary internationalization and inclusion choice, not an institutionally necessary expression of USF's own national identity.

This creates a useful Institutional-Role Necessity Gradient. A PRC embassy or consulate has an obvious mission-derived reason to display the PRC flag. A community organization explicitly organized around PRC national representation may also have a strong internal rationale. A broadly Chinese hometown or benevolent association has a contextual but contestable rationale. An American university has the weakest intrinsic nationality-based necessity: its rationale must instead come from a policy of international representation, student inclusion, diplomacy, recruitment, or another university-specific purpose.

\[Necessity(Flag_j\mid Institution_i)\neq Constant;\quad MissionFit_i\text{ matters}\]

Institutional-role asymmetry is not evidence that USF displayed the PRC flag for revenue, politics, or foreign influence. A universal policy granting equivalent representation to every nationality would instead weaken any claim of China-specific favoritism. The relevant explanatory variables are USF's own flag-selection rule, administrative location, stated purpose, recruitment environment, and decision process.

4. Peer-network amplification: the same national symbol can appear in student and administrative channels

The institutional-cue hypothesis becomes more plausible when a national symbol is not encountered only once. USF currently recognizes a Chinese Students and Scholars Association within its culturally focused student-organization structure, alongside a Taiwanese Student Association. USF also describes CSSA leadership as organizing orientation sessions tailored to Chinese international students, managing a dedicated WeChat group, and serving as a bridge between the university and Chinese international students. These functions are ordinary student-support activities, but they make the organization a consequential peer-network node.

A separate firsthand account from the 2019–2021 period describes CSSA participants marching on campus with an approximately thirty-foot-wide PRC flag. No independent archived university or news source has yet been located that establishes the dimensions or dates of those marches. The observation is therefore coded as a firsthand archival lead, not as a verified historical finding. If dated photographs, video, CSSA posts, or university records are recovered, the event can be upgraded to a directly observed mobilization variable.

The causal proposition is an interaction rather than a direct flag effect. A student who arrives with pre-existing political priors may receive repeated cues from co-national peers and from the university as an authority environment. The prediction is not that either cue creates CCP support. It is that the combination can reduce the likelihood that inherited assumptions are independently re-examined when comparative civic education and competing Chinese-language political narratives are weak.

\[\text{Prior socialization}\times\text{peer-network salience}\times\text{administrative symbolism}\times\text{low comparative instruction}\rightarrow\text{greater persistence of prior assumptions}\]
[60, 61]

5. From inclusion cue to possible regime-equivalence slippage

Universities generally display international flags to represent students, internationalism, or national diversity. That function is legitimate and does not by itself endorse a foreign government. The analytical problem begins one step later: national flags are also state symbols, and official placement by an authoritative institution can carry symbolic weight beyond the policy that generated the display.

Experimental research outside this specific campus context shows that political symbols can affect perceived legitimacy and political cognition. A 2023 experiment found that an otherwise non-instrumental symbol of authority increased subjects’ tendency to support the designated authority. Separate experimental work has found that national-flag exposure can influence political attitudes and behavior. These studies do not establish a PRC-campus-flag effect; they establish that treating symbols as psychologically inert would also be unwarranted.

\[\text{University-authorized display}\rightarrow\text{national legitimacy cue}\;\not\Rightarrow\;\text{regime legitimacy}\]

The hypothesized error is a Sovereign-Equality-to-Regime-Equivalence Slippage. The United States recognizes the PRC as a state and universities may represent Chinese students on equal terms with students from other countries. But equal membership in an international community does not logically imply that different constitutional orders are normatively equivalent on democracy, competitive elections, separation of powers, freedom of expression, academic freedom, due process, judicial independence, or constraints on executive power.

A student can therefore make a valid inference—“the university recognizes China and Chinese students as legitimate members of its international community”—and then make an invalid additional inference—“there is consequently no important political-institutional difference to examine between the PRC and the United States.” The analytically relevant issue is that second inference.

6. Revenue exposure, fixed-cost amplification, and the institutional-accommodation incentive

A separate mechanism concerns the university rather than the student. Private universities operate with substantial fixed and quasi-fixed costs: buildings, debt service, tenure and faculty lines, administrative infrastructure, libraries, laboratories, information systems, and student services cannot be reduced in proportion to enrollment on short notice. A country-specific enrollment source can therefore matter far more than its raw percentage of total headcount suggests. The relevant quantity is not simply share of students; it is the contribution of that market to net tuition and contribution margin, its substitutability, and the speed with which costs can adjust if the market disappears.

USF provides unusually strong evidence of material China-market exposure. In September 2016, President Paul J. Fitzgerald stated at a USF China Business Studies Initiative forum that 10% of USF students came from different provinces throughout China. That is a direct contemporary university statement about students from China, not an estimate of Chinese ancestry. Earlier reporting in 2012 counted 781 Chinese nationals among 10,017 students and reported then-full tuition of $36,000, implying a gross sticker-price scale of about $28.1 million before aid and program differences. By Fall 2020, USF reported 525 students from China among 1,272 degree-seeking international students, or about 41.3% of that international population.

The most probative school-level evidence comes from USF's FY2027 Three-Year Financial Plan. The School of Management enrolled 640 Chinese undergraduates and 107 Chinese graduate students in Fall 2015, or 747 students. By Fall 2025, those figures had fallen to 31 and 12, respectively: a decline of 704 students, or approximately 94%. Over the same decade, total School of Management undergraduate enrollment fell from 2,291 to 994 and graduate enrollment from 732 to 411. USF states that the school's enrollment contraction was driven overwhelmingly by the collapse in international enrollment, alongside decline in the legacy College of Professional Studies.

The institutional response was not cosmetic. The same plan reports that four graduate programs were sunset, the remaining graduate portfolio was redesigned, full-time faculty fell from 86 to 47, and professional staff fell from 72 to 37. The loss of Chinese students alone numerically equals about 43.5% of the School of Management's total headcount decline over the period. That ratio is descriptive, not a causal allocation: it does not mean China caused 43.5% of every cut. It does show that the exposure was large enough to be structurally consequential.

This fixed-cost structure explains why an exposure of “only 10%” can be strategically large. If a national cohort supplies roughly one-tenth of enrollment or tuition while most costs cannot fall by one-tenth immediately, the revenue shock is absorbed first by the university's much smaller flexible margin. The result can require disproportionately large adjustments in hiring, sections, program portfolios, services, capital projects, or tuition discounting.

\[ \lambda^{*}=\min\left\{\lambda:\;(1-\lambda)R_0-C_{\mathrm{post\text{-}adjustment}}<0\right\} \]

A useful concept from the companion financial analysis is a revenue-shock tipping threshold, \(\lambda^{*}\): the smallest revenue loss that pushes recurring operations below break-even before management can fully resize costs. The concept is analytically valuable because it explains why a 5–10% source-market exposure can matter disproportionately in a high-fixed-cost university. The companion manuscript's specific numerical calibration of a roughly 5.4% tipping point, however, depends on assumed fixed/variable cost classifications, expense elasticity, spillovers, donor responses, and timing. It should be treated as a stress-test scenario, not an audited USF threshold, until those inputs are independently reconstructed.

\[ \text{Country-market shock} \rightarrow \text{net-tuition / auxiliary pressure} \rightarrow \text{fixed-cost squeeze} \rightarrow \text{program and staffing adjustment} \rightarrow \text{possible effects on breadth, services, outcomes, reputation and yield} \rightarrow \text{feedback} \]

This is a feedback mechanism, not an inevitable death spiral. Universities can offset a country shock through domestic recruitment, replacement international markets, philanthropy, price changes, new programs, reserves, endowment support, or cost restructuring. Rankings are likewise an indirect channel: closing a program does not mechanically lower a U.S. News score, but sustained resource, faculty, retention, outcome, or reputation changes can affect the admissions market and ranking inputs over time.[66]

For the symbolic-accommodation hypothesis, the key consequence is an incentive rather than a demonstrated motive. A university with hundreds of students tied to one foreign market has rational reasons to invest in country-specific recruitment, alumni networks, orientation, language-specific communications, student organizations, cultural programming, and inexpensive symbols of belonging. USF also built institutional China-facing infrastructure during the high-dependence period, including the China Business Studies Initiative and high-level U.S.–China programming. Whether the PRC flag itself was adopted or retained for recruitment, diplomacy, generic internationalism, or some combination cannot be inferred without the flag-policy decision record.[54, 58, 59]

\[ P(A_j)=f\bigl(\text{TuitionExposure}_j,\text{EnrollmentConcentration}_j,\text{RecruitmentPriority}_j,\text{PartnershipValue}_j,\text{Mission},\text{PoliticalCost}\bigr) \]

Here Aj denotes accommodation toward national market j. The hypothesis predicts more country-specific recruitment, services, networks, and symbolic inclusion as material exposure rises, controlling for a university's general level of internationalization. It does not predict that administrators consciously trade political principles for each tuition dollar, nor that accommodation is inherently improper.

The previously modeled estimate of approximately $40.9 million in “Chinese-heritage” tuition is not used as an observed revenue measure because it combines a 2016 statement about students from China, a 2020 international headcount, a later total-enrollment denominator, and 2025–26 tuition. Those years and populations are not commensurable, and “students from China” cannot be redefined as all domestic students of Chinese heritage. The directly supported evidence is narrower: China-origin enrollment reached roughly 10% of the university in 2016; the School of Management contained an unusually large Chinese cohort in 2015; and the later contraction coincided with substantial program and staffing restructuring. The separate $394,000 FY2024 operating-deficit figure is likewise omitted from the quantitative baseline because the cited audited statements report different accounting measures—a $15.873 million decrease in net assets without donor restrictions and a $22.929 million total increase in net assets—without identifying the $394,000 figure as the same accounting concept.

Observed financial exposure, recruitment infrastructure, enrollment concentration, and organizational contraction establish an incentive environment but not the motive for a particular symbolic decision. Administrative friction avoidance, revenue-motivated symbolic accommodation, and reduced comparative political instruction remain causal hypotheses unless contemporaneous policy records, communications, minutes, or testimony connect the incentive to the decision.

7. Weijian Shan: political learning, financial-elite formation, and critical-infrastructure brokerage

USF's own history supplies an unusually revealing counterpoint to the missed-learning hypothesis. Weijian Shan (单伟建), later executive chairman and co-founder of PAG, was part of the first post-Cultural-Revolution cohort of mainland Chinese students to study in the United States. USF's 2023 honorary-degree citation states that Shan became one of the first students from China to attend an American university as a visiting scholar, earned an MBA from USF in 1981, and later completed graduate study at UC Berkeley under advisers Janet Yellen and George Akerlof. USF awarded Shan the degree of Doctor of Humane Letters, honoris causa, in May 2023.[51]

The same citation records Shan's retrospective description of arriving in the United States after the Cultural Revolution: the experience of having freedom of choice made him realize that he could choose what to study and determine his own future through his own effort. The example is analytically important because it shows the political-learning mechanism at the individual level: migration can expose a student to institutional freedoms that were previously absent, while the durability of that learning depends on whether those institutional differences are noticed, compared, and incorporated into later judgment.

Shan's subsequent career also illustrates the paper's cross-system brokerage mechanism. In Hong Kong, the Hong Kong Academy of Finance (AoF)—created with the participation of the Hong Kong Monetary Authority, Securities and Futures Commission, Insurance Authority, and Mandatory Provident Fund Schemes Authority—runs leadership-development programmes for senior banking, investment, regulatory, and other financial talent. Its Financial Leaders Programme is designed for executives typically one or two levels below chief executive rank. In 2025, AoF's Leadership Development Programme featured Shan in its Distinguished Speakers Series; the same series later featured Pablo Hernández de Cos, General Manager of the Bank for International Settlements (BIS). AoF and its research arm also operate directly in a BIS-connected institutional environment, including conferences co-organized with the BIS. Shan therefore participates in the formation of Hong Kong financial elites inside an ecosystem that interfaces directly with the BIS and the wider central-banking network.[83, 84, 85]

The same brokerage pattern extends into the Taiwan Cement / TCC network. Contemporary PRC corporate-information databases continue to list Shan as a non-executive director of TCC International Holdings Limited (台泥国际集团有限公司), while TCC's broader corporate structure spans Taiwan, Hong Kong-centered holding and commercial functions, and mainland operating companies. These records place Shan inside a corporate network connecting finance, Hong Kong, mainland China, and one of Taiwan's most strategically consequential basic-material industries.[86, 87]

TCC itself describes its Taiwan business as the country's largest and most historic cement and ready-mix concrete producer and seller, with a 2024 domestic cement-and-clinker market share of approximately 34.6% and a dense network of cement plants, ready-mix concrete plants, product facilities, distribution stations, and transport links across Taiwan. TCC's own plant directory shows concrete and distribution nodes extending from northern Taiwan through central and southern Taiwan to Hualien, together with mainland operating centers and plants. Its logistics operations include bulk-cement tractors and heavy trucks, making physical distribution an integral part of the group's industrial architecture.[88, 89, 90]

This industrial position has a direct national-security dimension. Taiwan's Ministry of Economic Affairs identifies cement as a basic material of both national defense and civilian industry and states that civil engineering, roads, bridges, ports, and national-defense works all depend on cement. Taiwan defense research likewise discusses high-strength concrete as a principal material for hardened military facilities, including protected command sites, tunnel-type installations, aircraft shelters, ammunition and fuel storage, and other reinforced structures.[91, 92]

For that reason, the nationwide concrete-order and delivery system can be understood as a military logistics and surveillance network in a passive material-flow sense. Large concrete orders, unusual surges in demand, atypical destinations, recurring delivery routes, shipment timing, customer identity, and concentrations of mixer-truck or bulk-cement traffic can reveal where major hardened construction is occurring and how rapidly it is progressing. The network need not contain a dedicated intelligence sensor to produce intelligence value: the logistics pattern itself can expose the location, scale, tempo, and sequencing of underground facilities, bunkers, tunnels, ports, runways, base upgrades, or other defense works. The uploaded source study identifies precisely these observables—order flows, routing, delivery schedules, client identity, geographic location, shipment timing, and anomalous demand—as the infrastructure-exposure mechanism.[88, 89, 90, 91, 92]

Board leadership adds another transnational node. TCC chairman 張安平 (Nelson Chang) received the Italian state's Grande Ufficiale dell'Ordine della Stella d'Italia (Grand Officer of the Order of the Star of Italy), a high-ranking Italian state decoration. The award is relevant here not as proof of any military role, but as another indicator of the breadth of elite cross-border recognition and access surrounding the same industrial network.[93]

The relevance of these links is structural rather than accusatory. They do not establish that Shan, Chang, or any TCC director transmitted defense information or acted for a foreign government. They demonstrate something narrower and more important for the paper's thesis: a single elite broker can simultaneously occupy or influence educational, financial, regulatory-adjacent, industrial, and transnational networks whose informational environments are highly asymmetric. In the paper's notation, Shan is therefore a concrete example of high Bi—cross-system bridging capacity—interacting with high institutional difference Dt and high connectivity Ct.

Two years after Shan's honorary degree, in May 2025, USF awarded an honorary degree to Anthony S. Fauci, M.D.. In his commencement address, Fauci urged graduates to employ critical thinking, respect evidence-based information, seek differing opinions, discern falsehoods, and challenge untruths. The connection is thematic rather than sequential: together, the Shan citation and Fauci address show that freedom of choice, critical inquiry, evidence, and intellectual challenge are values USF itself publicly celebrates.[52]

Cross-system brokerage
The Shan case links the paper's political-learning mechanism to a broader brokerage structure spanning USF, Hong Kong financial-leadership formation, BIS-connected institutions, TCC corporate governance, and a defense-critical material supply chain. The analytic question is therefore not whether one affiliation proves foreign agency, but how cross-system brokers acquire value from bridging institutions whose political, financial, and informational rules differ.

C1 · PRIOR SOCIALIZATION

Political priors arrive before the student

Students do not enter the United States as blank slates. Prior schooling, media diets, nationalism, family narratives, and perceived PRC performance shape starting beliefs.

C2 · AUTHORITY CUE

The university is a trusted institutional environment

A recurring flag placed by the administration can be read as an official cue of inclusion and normality, especially when displayed beside U.S. and other national symbols.

C3 · CATEGORY SLIPPAGE

Nation, state, government, and regime may collapse into one category

If the distinctions are not taught explicitly, legitimate respect for Chinese people and national identity can be cognitively fused with judgments about PRC political institutions.

C4 · PASSIVE EXPOSURE

Living in a democracy does not guarantee comparative learning

Students may remain within co-national information networks, avoid politics, focus on degrees and careers, or interpret U.S. politics through preexisting frames.

C5 · FOREGONE COMPARISON

No structured reason to ask what the institutional differences produce

Without comparative civic education, everyday freedoms can be experienced as conveniences rather than institutional outcomes generated by law, rights, accountability, and contestation.

The prior regime may remain “normal enough”

The predicted effect is not necessarily active CCP support. It may simply be reduced curiosity about whether alternative institutions are desirable or transferable to China.

10. The anti-backfire constraint: criticize institutions without stigmatizing people

The distinction is not merely ethical; it is empirically important. Fan, Pan, Shao, and Xu’s experiment among Chinese undergraduates in the United States found that anti-Chinese discrimination reduced support for political reform and increased support for authoritarian rule. By contrast, exposure to non-racist criticism of the Chinese government did not produce the same authoritarian backlash. A university that wants genuine critical inquiry therefore has reason to separate Chinese identity from PRC institutions with unusual care.

The educational implication is a dual obligation: full belonging for Chinese students as persons and members of the university community; full intellectual contestability for the PRC political system as an object of scholarship. A flag can serve the first function without being allowed to foreclose the second.

11. Observer attribution, public visibility, and the Lone Mountain photography problem

A separate reported episode concerns motive attribution during a Lone Mountain visit undertaken to check the university’s PRC-flag display. According to the firsthand account, ordinary campus photography was interpreted as possible interest in a distant Bay Area security or military facility. The episode is not independently adjudicated and cannot establish institutional policy, discriminatory motive, retaliation, or coordination. Its analytical value is narrower: it illustrates why a high-specificity security inference requires evidence about the actual photographic target and collection behavior rather than inference from identity, nationality, or the mere presence of a camera.

The episode is not independently adjudicated and cannot establish institutional retaliation, coordination, or discriminatory intent. The appropriate evidentiary standard is symmetrical. Just as PRC flag ≠ CCP agent, the inference Taiwanese visitor + camera ≠ evidence of PRC intelligence activity requires an independent evidentiary bridge.

11.1 The public-information novelty test

The mere visibility of distant Bay Area infrastructure carries little diagnostic value. Lone Mountain is an elevated urban campus, and large portions of San Francisco Bay, bridges, islands, waterfronts, and former military properties are observable from numerous ordinary public locations. Public web maps, aerial imagery, photographs, and other lawful public imagery also make the existence and general exterior geography of such facilities widely accessible. A normal phone photograph whose intended subject is a campus flag therefore adds little or no novel information merely because distant infrastructure happens to fall within the same broad field of view.

Public availability does not make hostile reconnaissance impossible. Intelligence services can aggregate open-source material, use high-resolution optics, study patterns over time, or combine public and non-public information. But those possibilities establish only that public observation can sometimes be dual-use. They do not transform every publicly taken photograph into evidence of espionage. The evidentiary weight depends on what new information was being sought and how specifically the collection behavior targeted it.

\[ \text{Diagnostic value of reconnaissance evidence} \propto \text{Information novelty}\times \text{Target specificity}\times \text{Collection persistence}\times \text{Access circumvention}\times \text{Tasking evidence} \]

When information novelty and target specificity are near zero—for example, an ordinary wide-angle campus photograph taken from a lawful public location—the standalone evidentiary value for an espionage hypothesis should also be low. This is an analytic heuristic, not a rule of criminal law.

11.2 Identify the alleged facility before reasoning from it

The phrase “naval base across the Bay” is itself too imprecise for serious analysis. If the intended reference was former Naval Air Station Alameda, the Navy records that operational activity ended on 30 April 1997. If the reference was former Naval Station Treasure Island, the Navy records closure on 30 September 1997; the site is now a redevelopment and environmental-remediation area, although federal property and Coast Guard functions remain elsewhere in the Bay. If the intended object was an active site such as Coast Guard Island, that is a different factual question, but a distant line of sight still does not establish that it was the photographic target.

The proper sequence is therefore: identify the facility; determine whether it was actually framed or magnified; distinguish ordinary background visibility from deliberate repeated collection; and only then ask whether the behavior is more probable under benign documentation or hostile reconnaissance.

11.3 Recognition–suspicion consistency

A second issue is institutional consistency. USF's display of a PRC national flag does not mean the university believes the PRC government is benign, democratic, or strategically aligned with the United States. Universities can display the flags of states with which the United States has serious security disputes because the display may represent students, nationality, diplomatic recognition, or international inclusion. It would therefore be a false dichotomy to argue that a university cannot both display the PRC flag and take PRC state-security risks seriously.

But the reverse proposition matters equally. If the institution itself uses the PRC flag as an ordinary symbol of national inclusion, then PRC-related identity, language, or interest in that same publicly displayed symbol cannot logically function as sufficient evidence of hostile intent. A security inference must be supplied by conduct-specific evidence. Otherwise the institution risks a recognition–suspicion asymmetry: PRC national identity is normalized when useful for international inclusion, recruitment, and ceremony, but treated as intrinsically suspicious when an individual examines the institution's own PRC symbolism.

\[ \text{Institutional inclusion of PRC nationality} \not\Rightarrow \text{PRC regime endorsement} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \text{PRC-related identity or inquiry} \not\Rightarrow \text{hostile state intent} \]

The proper inferential sequence is Bayesian rather than accusatory. A routine visitor/flag-documentation explanation and a hostile-reconnaissance explanation are competing hypotheses. Evidence should update between them according to how specifically it is predicted by each. A camera aimed at the campus flag is strongly expected under the first hypothesis. The hostile-reconnaissance hypothesis becomes materially stronger only with behavior such as persistent targeting of a sensitive facility, unusual magnification or pattern collection, access circumvention, concealed tasking, or a foreign-principal relationship.

Security personnel are not barred from asking neutral questions when conduct creates a genuine site-specific concern. The methodological and civil-liberties boundary is crossed when a low-specificity fact—appearance, ancestry, language, or ordinary public photography—is converted into a high-specificity accusation without the missing evidentiary bridge. The same evidentiary principle applies throughout: observable symbols and identities are not substitutes for evidence of hidden motive.

Longitudinal student panel

Survey students before arrival, after orientation, after one academic year, and after graduation. Track political knowledge, media use, social networks, classroom exposure, discrimination, and regime evaluations.

Removal of the PRC flag is not implied by the argument. Removal could itself communicate exclusion of Chinese students and collapse Chinese identity into the CCP. Six Causal Mechanisms

The same observable act—a PRC flag, a consular banquet, a lobbying position, a delegation, or a hometown-association membership—can arise from distinct causal mechanisms. Six pathways are analytically separable and may operate alone or in combination.

M1 · MIGRATION SELECTION

Exit for reasons unrelated to politics

Education, family, professional opportunity, and income can generate migration without anti-regime selection. Emigration is therefore weak evidence of homeland-regime preference.

M2 · CIVILIZATIONAL / NATIONAL IDENTITY

Ancestral attachment without communist ideology

Cultural pride, national identity, and support for a homeland’s prosperity may coexist with criticism of authoritarian rule. Host-state citizenship and ancestral-country attachment are not mutually exclusive.

M3 · ECONOMIC / NETWORK PRAGMATISM

Opportunity asymmetry and cross-border utility

For many sectors during 1990–2019, the scale of PRC markets and institutions created larger potential opportunity sets than Taiwan. Alignment can therefore be instrumental rather than ideological.[14, 15, 34, 35]

M4 · INSTITUTIONAL PATH DEPENDENCE

Relationships outlive shifts in mass opinion

Consular contacts, delegations, business introductions, donors, media relations, and leadership interlocks create durable organizational capital that does not reset when public opinion changes.

M5 · FOREIGN-STATE CULTIVATION

Top-down influence and co-option

U.S. government and independent research documents PRC party-state efforts to cultivate selected diaspora leaders and organizations. The existence of this mechanism does not justify treating the diaspora collectively as suspect.[27, 28]

M6 · COERCIVE CONNECTIVITY

Family, asset, business, and professional leverage

Threats against relatives or other interests in China can create self-censorship or compliance in particular cases. FBI and transnational-repression research document the mechanism; population prevalence remains unknown.

\[\text{Diaspora alignment}=\text{bottom-up preference}+\text{institutional incentives}+\text{top-down cultivation}+\text{coercive constraints}\]

China has a large genuine private sector. Official 2025 data reported more than 57 million registered private enterprises, more than 92% of businesses, with official estimates above 60% of GDP and 80% of urban employment. The relevant mechanism is therefore not the absence of private enterprise, but the possibility that party-state regulatory, political, and financial power increases the value of governmental relationships in particular commercial settings.

Cross-System Brokerage Rents

The central economic mechanism is cross-system brokerage: actors positioned between economically connected but institutionally divergent systems can earn rents from information asymmetry, linguistic advantage, network access, regulatory differences, and status portability.

\[\text{Value of intermediary}\;\propto\;\text{institutional difference}\times\text{ability to bridge the systems}\]
Complete separationFew transactions cross the boundary; brokerage opportunities collapse.
Partial integration + divergenceTransactions remain possible while information and institutional differences stay large; brokerage rents can be maximized.
Complete integrationInformation asymmetry and boundary-specific advantages shrink; brokerage rents erode.

Chinese Americans whose income, professional status, social capital, family interests, or business opportunities depend more heavily on access to China should, other things equal, exhibit more favorable attitudes toward China and stronger preferences for continued U.S.–China engagement.

The greater the China-dependent economic or network exposure, the stronger the predicted preference for avoiding policies that threaten cross-border access.

As U.S.–China relations deteriorate after 2018–20 and expected brokerage rents fall, China favorability should decline more among actors whose earlier alignment was primarily opportunity-driven than among actors whose preferences are ideological or nationalist.

Legal Implications: Foreign Direction, Coercion, Transparency & Neutrality

Conduct, not identity
Political agreement ≠ foreign agency. Nationality ≠ foreign agency. A flag ≠ foreign direction. The legally relevant variables are relationship, direction or control, covered conduct, disclosure duties, discriminatory treatment, misuse of protected records, threats, coercion, stalking, and other independently provable acts.[31, 67, 81]

1. Existing U.S. law: what each framework actually reaches

Foreign-influence law does not create a general offense of being sympathetic to a foreign country. FARA is principally a disclosure regime for specified activities performed at the order, request, direction, or control of a foreign principal, subject to exemptions. Section 951 is a distinct criminal notification statute for persons acting in the United States as agents of foreign governments. Neither statute is established by favorable views of China, a PRC flag, Chinese-language activity, consular attendance at a public event, or ordinary academic/business engagement standing alone.[31, 67, 81]

2. Transnational repression: conduct-specific legal analysis

The FBI defines transnational repression as foreign-government conduct reaching into the United States to intimidate, silence, coerce, harass, or harm diaspora and exile communities. Reported tactics include stalking, threats, family pressure abroad, cyber activity, coercive return efforts, assault, and attempted kidnapping. The category identifies a threat pattern; it does not create a presumption that ordinary diaspora organizations, students, or political advocates act for a foreign government.[29, 71]

Where a concrete incident involves repeated following, a credible threat, threatened injury, coerced transfer of property, interference with a legally protected right, or disclosure of protected institutional records, the appropriate legal analysis begins with the elements of the relevant statute. The nationality of the alleged actor is not a substitute for those elements.[74, 75, 76, 77, 78, 73, 69]

3. Institutional and statutory implications

The legal implications follow from the same distinction that structures the empirical analysis: regulation is most defensible when it targets undisclosed foreign-government direction, coercion, discriminatory enforcement, misuse of protected records, and opaque financial relationships rather than nationality, lawful advocacy, cultural affiliation, or political opinion. A rule aimed at suppressing “pro-PRC” speech as such would collapse viewpoint into agency and reproduce the attribution error identified by the evidence.

4. Comparative transparency regimes

Australia’s Foreign Influence Transparency Scheme and the United Kingdom’s Foreign Influence Registration Scheme provide comparative models centered on specified relationships and influence activity rather than ethnic identity. Their details differ from U.S. law, but both reinforce the same structural principle: disclosure duties should attach to defined foreign-principal or foreign-power arrangements, not to a person’s ancestry or political preference.[32, 33]

Legal distinction
The legally durable line is not “China-friendly versus China-critical.” It is independent versus foreign-directed, transparent versus undisclosed, voluntary versus coerced, viewpoint-neutral versus discriminatory, and public information versus protected or unlawfully obtained information.

Empirical Strategy, Identification & Falsification

A. Evidence provenance and source weighting

Primary statutes, official institutional records, audited financial statements, and original survey reports receive the greatest evidentiary weight. Government and advocacy sources are used for attributed claims or event occurrence unless independently corroborated; state-media crowd estimates and causal characterizations are not treated as neutral measurements. Survey findings retain their original question wording, sampling frame, and methodological limits. This weighting rule separates evidence of occurrence from evidence of motive, prevalence, or causation.

B. San Francisco organization-year panel, 1990–2026

The primary unit of observation is the organization-year. A 100–300-organization panel can separate durable institutional alignment from one-off events and from individual public opinion.

D. U.S. public survey experiment: testing the Exit = Opposition intuition

A nationally representative experiment can randomize why a hypothetical China-born adult migrated to the United States: graduate education, economic opportunity, family reunification, political persecution, or an unspecified reason. Respondents then estimate the person’s likely views of the PRC government, CCP, Taiwan, U.S.–China restrictions, and continuing China-linked professional relationships. Party identification, ideology, education, age, military background, and China knowledge enter as moderators.

“A flag is not an opinion poll.”Valid limitationCorrect. Flag display cannot identify private beliefs, CCP ideology, or legal agency. It does not invalidate flags as a measure of public organizational symbolism when the estimand is institutional alignment rather than individual ideology.Keep separate outcomes for public opinion, institution-weighted flags, physical flag density, statements, events, and network ties. Recent political-science research likewise shows that flag display can reflect social norms or mobilization rather than underlying preference.
“Chinatown associations are not representative of Chinese Americans.”CorrectThis defeats any claim that association behavior estimates the national Chinese-American population. It does not defeat the narrower claim that a historically important local institutional ecosystem may diverge from national mass opinion. That divergence is itself the dependent variable.Never pool association observations with national survey respondents. Report national opinion and SF institutional alignment as separate series.

“APEC crowds measured logistics and mobilization, not underlying opinion.”CorrectExactly. Scope of supported claim
The alternative explanations and scope conditions eliminate the overclaims—flags are not ideology, Chinatown is not the national population, 75% was not CCP support, and 2019 is not a measured opinion peak. They do not eliminate the narrower propositions that exit does not identify regime opposition, San Francisco institutions underwent a long PRC-oriented realignment, cross-system incentives are testable causes of instrumental alignment, institutions can remain aligned after mass attitudes change, and university-authorized national symbolism can be tested as a possible legitimacy cue without treating flag display as proof of regime endorsement.

Two recent flag studies illustrate why this discipline matters. Dinas, Martínez, and Valentim (2024) show that longitudinal changes in public flag display can reflect social-norm change and expressive behavior rather than a one-for-one change in private preferences. Jonathan Hassid’s cross-national flag-density work likewise reports null relationships between flag exposure/density and several presumed measures of political power. These findings do not make flags useless; they require the dependent variable to be specified as symbolic display and paired with surveys, events, and organizational data when preference is the object of inference.

Conclusion: What the Evidence Supports

Migration does not identify political opposition

Education and family reunification dominate the stated reasons for migration among Chinese immigrants in Pew’s survey. The proposition “an immigrant left China, therefore the immigrant opposed the PRC/CCP” is logically and empirically unsupported.
[1]

China favorability fell sharply after the high-integration era

The cross-survey indicator moves from roughly 75% favorable in 2005 and 68% in 2016 to 41% in 2022–23. This is a decline in country favorability, not a measured collapse from 75% CCP support.
[2, 3, 4, 5]

San Francisco institutions diverged from national mass opinion

Traditional Chinatown organizations moved from historical Taiwan dominance to parity by 2004 and later to a PRC-majority flag environment. That institutional orientation persisted after national Chinese-American China favorability fell below a majority.

August–October 2019 is a plausible local institutional apex

Dense educational, tourism, commercial, civic, and consular networks overlapped with unusually visible pro-PRC institutional mobilization during the Hong Kong crisis and PRC 70th-anniversary events. The claim concerns organizational mobilization, not private ideology.

Cross-system brokerage can create instrumental alignment

Bilingual, bicultural, or institutionally connected intermediaries can earn rents by bridging partially integrated but structurally different systems. Such rents can generate preferences for bilateral stability and access without requiring ideological CCP support.

University flags can create an institutional-equivalence cue

Several U.S. universities demonstrably display PRC national flags under university authority, but the political effect is unknown. The testable proposition is that equal-footing, administratively salient flag display may reduce the salience of regime differences for some students unless national inclusion is paired with explicit comparative civic education. The model predicts heterogeneous and context-dependent effects, not automatic pro-CCP persuasion.

USF has weaker mission-derived necessity to display a PRC flag than a China-representational organization

Some Chinatown organizations may use PRC or Taiwan flags as part of communal, historical, or cross-Strait identity. USF is an American university; its PRC flag is therefore better analyzed as a discretionary internationalization and inclusion choice. That weakens any claim that the display itself proves pro-PRC ideology, while strengthening the case for examining the university-specific policy and incentives behind the choice.

USF had material financial exposure to China-origin enrollment

USF's president stated in 2016 that roughly 10% of students came from provinces across China. Its FY2027 plan reports 747 Chinese School of Management students in Fall 2015 and only 43 in Fall 2025, alongside major enrollment, program, faculty, and staff contraction. The evidence establishes exposure and institutional adjustment; it does not prove that any flag policy was adopted for revenue reasons.
[53, 54, 55, 56, 57]

Revenue exposure can increase incentives for symbolic and service accommodation

Where a national student market contributes materially to net tuition, administrators have rational incentives to invest in recruitment, retention, culturally specific services, and low-cost symbols of inclusion. Whether those incentives ever suppress contentious comparative education remains an open causal question.

Public visibility and hostile-intent inference

A distant facility that is routinely visible from public viewpoints or public imagery contributes little by itself to a hostile-reconnaissance inference. Diagnostic weight comes from information novelty, target specificity, persistent collection, access circumvention, and evidence of tasking. The same rule applies regardless of ancestry, language, or political interest.
[62, 63, 64, 65]

Current percentage of Chinese Americans who support the CCP

No directly comparable nationally representative CCP-support time series was identified in the principal sources reviewed. Claims assigning the 2005 75% China-favorability figure to CCP support are therefore invalid.

Competing causal explanations

Observable PRC-oriented behavior may reflect sincere ideological support, Chinese nationalism, cultural or ancestral identity, economic pragmatism, cross-border brokerage rents, organizational path dependence, elite status incentives, PRC cultivation, fear of anti-Chinese backlash, coercion through relatives or assets, or combinations of these mechanisms. Motive cannot be inferred from ethnicity or symbolism alone.
