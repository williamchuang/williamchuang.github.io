Reconstruction method

The page follows the Logarchéon pattern: a historically bounded persona, 33 overlapping method cards, a question atlas, ranked prevalence from 300 case rows, a searchable corpus, worked demonstrations, and an explicit source spine. A “case” is not a copied archival file; it is a structured decision unit: situation, why-questions, likely work move, skill family, strategy tags, and public-source anchor.

Core thesis

Friedman’s method joined literary skepticism, cryptanalytic patience, austere pencil-and-paper discipline, courtroom communication, small-team training, interagency translation, and public-safety judgment. Her strength was not only solving messages; it was turning uncertain signal into lawful, usable intelligence.

Reading unit

Each row asks: where does the problem start, what would Friedman need to know, what would she do with the evidence, how would the result be explained, and what record should survive secrecy?

Ethical overlay

The page emphasizes legal authority, court admissibility, source-protection, privacy, proportionality, and credit repair. Secrecy is treated as a constraint to manage, not a reason to erase people from history.

Decision tree: how to read a Friedman case

1. What kind of signal is this?

Literary claim, wartime message, smuggling traffic, courtroom exhibit, Nazi spy circuit, domestic open-code clue, liaison report, or archival memory problem.

2. What evidence exists before interpretation?

Inventory the message, sender context, date, route, external records, and prior traffic before allowing a pattern to become a conclusion.

3. Who can lawfully act?

Distinguish cryptanalytic insight from Coast Guard action, Treasury authority, Navy routing, FBI investigation, or courtroom proof.

4. What confidence and caveat travel with it?

Separate proven solution, probable inference, possible open-code reading, and unsupported suspicion.

5. What action could burn the channel?

Ask whether raids, publicity, or premature arrests would alert the target and destroy continued intelligence value.

6. What record prevents erasure?

Preserve contribution records, sealed credit, worksheets, source caveats, and archives for later correction of public memory.

Question atlas — 12 situation families

These are the reusable front-door questions. The 300 rows below instantiate them across Riverbank, World War I, Prohibition, Unit 387, World War II counterintelligence, courts, and legacy work.

Cipher claim / suspected hidden text

  • What exactly is the claim?
  • What would disprove it?
  • What chance pattern is being mistaken for intent?
  • Who benefits from belief?
  • What record should survive?

Government cryptologic request

  • Who is asking, and under what authority?
  • What operational decision depends on the answer?
  • What can be taught safely?
  • What confidence level is honest?
  • What artifact should be delivered?

Law-enforcement intercept

  • Which traffic matters now?
  • What external evidence corroborates it?
  • What lawful action can follow?
  • What must remain a lead rather than proof?
  • How does the report protect sources?

Maritime smuggling network

  • Which vessel, shore station, or route recurs?
  • What timing changes matter?
  • What customs or Coast Guard record fits?
  • Where could inference overreach?
  • What disruption is proportional?

Courtroom evidence

  • What is technical fact versus inference?
  • How will a non-specialist understand it?
  • What chain of custody is needed?
  • What caveat must not be hidden?
  • What should not be claimed?

Small analytic unit

  • Which work is bottlenecked?
  • What can be trained?
  • How will peer checks work?
  • Who is exhausted or overloaded?
  • What contribution record is kept?

Counterintelligence circuit

  • Which circuit, region, or actor is central?
  • What does traffic prove and not prove?
  • Who can act without exposing the channel?
  • What happens if the target goes silent?
  • Which liaison caveat is required?

Machine-cipher escalation

  • What changed in the system?
  • What resources does the difficulty require?
  • What strategic consequence follows delay?
  • Who needs to approve the escalation?
  • How is uncertainty described?

Interagency routing

  • Who owns intelligence, enforcement, prosecution, and publicity?
  • What should each agency receive?
  • What action could burn the source?
  • Who gets credit in the record?
  • What audit trail remains?

Open-code suspicion

  • What hidden meaning is hypothesized?
  • What innocent reading remains?
  • Which external facts support it?
  • How is the risk of overreading controlled?
  • What legal standard applies?

Secrecy and credit

  • What secrecy is necessary?
  • What erasure is avoidable?
  • What sealed record can preserve attribution?
  • When should declassification be revisited?
  • Who gets written back into history?

Archive and legacy

  • What papers, notes, and artifacts should be preserved?
  • Which myths does the archive correct?
  • Who should have access?
  • What privacy or security limits remain?

Limits and ethics

Non-operational abstraction

The page does not teach how to break modern codes, intercept communications, evade law enforcement, or conduct surveillance. It studies historical reasoning patterns at a high level.

Evidence humility

Many accomplishments were classified, later declassified, or publicly misattributed. The reconstruction therefore treats public-source anchors as evidence families and marks uncertainty through questions.

Legacy caution

Correcting erasure should not create a flawless-hero myth. Friedman’s work is strongest when read as disciplined labor, public-safety intelligence, team-building, and archival justice.
