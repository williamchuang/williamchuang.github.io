Focused Ultrasound Neuromodulation for Substance Use Disorder:
A λ-Stack Mathematical Framework, Safety Architecture, and National Translation Plan

FUS · LIFU Nucleus accumbens λ-Stack CEAS GRAIL Ψ-Operator Adverse-event analysis Optimal control William Chuang · Logarcheon Inc.
BLUF
This work develops a unified mathematical framework for low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU) neuromodulation targeting the nucleus accumbens (NAc) in substance use disorder (SUD), couples that framework to the λ-Stack (CEAS + GRAIL + Ψ-Operator) as a real-time safety and control architecture, and situates the proposal against the current U.S. burden of disease: in 2024, 48.4 million people aged 12 or older had a past-year SUD, 52.6 million were classified as needing substance use treatment, and only 10.2 million received any substance use treatment in the prior year.1

What is being attempted?

Substance use disorder remains a major neurobehavioral and public-health burden in the United States. According to SAMHSA’s 2024 National Survey on Drug Use and Health, 16.8% of Americans aged 12 or older—48.4 million people—had a past-year SUD, while 52.6 million were classified as needing substance use treatment.1 More broadly, NIDA reports that the use and misuse of alcohol, nicotine, illicit drugs, and prescription drugs cost the United States more than $700 billion annually in health care, crime, and lost productivity.2

Mechanistically, a 2024 Science study identified a Rheb-linked nucleus accumbens pathway through which repeated drug exposure augments natural reward-responsive ensembles, sharpening the case for nucleus accumbens-directed intervention in addiction biology.3 That study does not by itself establish a clinical treatment, but it materially strengthens the biological rationale for targeting NAc circuitry.

Focused ultrasound is one candidate modality for that purpose because it can noninvasively reach deep brain structures with high spatial selectivity.4 In the first published clinical study directly relevant here, Rezai et al. reported a prospective, open-label, single-arm trial of 8 participants with severe primary opioid use disorder and co-occurring substance use who received a single 20-minute session of 220-kHz LIFU targeting the bilateral nucleus accumbens.5

The published signal is preliminary but notable. In that study, investigators reported no serious device-related adverse events or imaging abnormalities; mean cue-induced opioid craving fell by 91% over 90 days (p < 0.0001), with median craving scores declining from 6.9 to 0.6 on a 10-point scale.5 Seven of eight participants were abstinent at 30 days, and five of eight remained abstinent through 90 days, with decreases in substance use corroborated by urine toxicology.5 Craving reductions were also reported for methamphetamine (p < 0.002) and cocaine (p < 0.02), a clinically important observation because NIDA states that there are currently no FDA-approved treatments for cocaine, methamphetamine, or marijuana use disorders.6

Those findings must, however, be read with proper discipline. The study was not randomized, not sham-controlled, and enrolled only eight participants.5 The correct inference is therefore not that efficacy has been definitively established, but that a mechanistically coherent and clinically nontrivial signal exists and warrants rigorous controlled confirmation.

The national need for such confirmation remains large. CDC final mortality data show that 79,384 drug overdose deaths occurred in the United States in 2024, including 54,045 opioid-involved deaths.7 In parallel, CDC reported that in 2022, 3.7% of U.S. adults needed opioid use disorder treatment, yet only 25.1% received medications for OUD.8 That treatment-gap reality is a stronger and more defensible backdrop for your argument than generalized rhetoric about “standard-of-care failure.”

The purpose of this work is therefore narrower, cleaner, and more defensible than a sweeping cure claim: it provides a mathematics-first safety, observability, and control architecture for NAc-targeted LIFU; formalizes adverse-event analysis; and argues that any credible path to scale must satisfy transparent parameter reporting, reproducible safety constraints, and prospective controlled clinical validation.

Identity Friction, Diplomatic Nomenclature, and Pastoral Strategy: Taiwan in Holy See–PRC Statecraft, 2026–2106

A decision-analytic memorandum offered with filial reverence toward the Holy See and in humble service to the Church's mission — written for senior ecclesial and policy principals whose remit includes Taiwan, China, the Holy See, or long-horizon institutional strategy.

Working Paper · William Chuang · 14 March 2026 Taiwan · Holy See · PRC Pastoral Strategy Civic Identity 2026–2106 Horizon

✠   Offered Under the Patronage of Our Lady, Blessed Gerard, and Saint John the Baptist

This work is offered under the intercession of the three great patrons of the Order of Saint John — in the spirit in which that Order has served the Church for nearly a thousand years: not with the pride of rank, but with the humility of those who understand themselves to be, in the words of the ancient Rule, serfs and servants of the poor and the sick. May whatever is true in these pages be received in that spirit, and whatever is in error be charitably corrected by those whose wisdom and authority surpass the author's own.
Our Lady, Queen of Heaven The Blessed Virgin Mary — model of faithful discipleship and the Order's highest protector. At Cana she directed the servants: "Do whatever he tells you" (John 2:5). She stands as the first evangelist — the one who brought Christ into the world without condition or exception. The missionary challenge this paper addresses begins with her fiat.
Blessed Gerard (c. 1040–1120) Founder of the Hospital of Saint John in Jerusalem. A humble lay brother who cared for pilgrims and the sick regardless of nationality, language, or civic identity — seeing in every patient the image of Christ. It is in his spirit of radical universal welcome — welcome that does not first demand that the guest identify themselves in the host's preferred civic category — that this paper finds its pastoral warrant.
Saint John the Baptist Patron of the Order and herald of the Incarnation. Sent before the Word to prepare the way and make the paths straight — to attend to the conditions of reception so that when the Lord arrived, the people were ready. His is the logic of the precursor: remove the obstacles, level the ground. That is precisely this paper's analytical task — not to change the Gospel, but to ask honestly whether the Church's public language in Taiwan is preparing the way or inadvertently placing a stumbling block before the sixty-two per cent majority who might otherwise draw near.

What This Paper Is — and Is Not

This paper is an exercise in ecclesial strategic analysis offered in a spirit of service to the Church's mission. It makes no claim about the Holy See's legal positions or sovereign intentions, which remain the Holy See's own to define. Decision-makers with access to the fuller institutional record are best positioned to determine which options apply to present conditions — and the author defers to that judgment with full sincerity.

The governing question is not theological — it does not touch doctrine, the sacraments, or papal authority. It is strategic and conditional:

Does the Holy See's current public diplomatic language about Taiwan create a risk to Catholic mission there — and if so, which course of action best protects Taiwan's Catholic community while preserving the Church's engagement with China?

The paper's analytical fulcrum is the distinction between what the Holy See privately intends by its language — which is legally and canonically significant — and how that language is received on the ground in Taiwan. The two can diverge, and when they do, reception is the pastoral variable that matters.

Why This Paper — and Why Now

First, a naming incident. On 3 July 2025, the Vatican Press Office publicly referred to Taiwan's newly credentialled envoy as "Ambassador of China (Taiwan)" — placing Taiwan inside a Chinese bracket in a formal papal ceremony. Whatever the Holy See privately intends, the signal to Taiwanese audiences is legible: the Church speaks of Taiwan in a Chinese civic register, not a Taiwanese one.

Second, a structural reality about the clergy. Taiwan's Catholic clergy and institutional leadership were historically formed by priests from mainland China, many arriving after 1949. That origin has left a lasting imprint on the Church's language and civic associations. As a result, the less than one per cent of Taiwan's population that is Catholic has high overlap with the approximately two per cent who identify as Chinese only — not Taiwanese at all.

This is the mission ceiling the paper addresses.

傳教 — evangelization, the Church's Great Commission to "go and make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19) — is directed outward, toward those not yet within the community. If the Church's civic register aligns primarily with the two per cent who identify as Chinese only, that two per cent is the hard ceiling on natural mission reach. Even broadening to those who hold both a Taiwanese and a Chinese identity (roughly thirty per cent) still leaves the Church speaking past the sixty-two per cent majority who identify as Taiwanese only — the primary pool from which any serious growth must come.

The naming incident was not an isolated technicality. It was a publicly visible confirmation of a deeper structural misalignment that, left unaddressed, converts a generous and faithful community into one whose growth ceiling is set not by the breadth of the Gospel's invitation but by the Church's own public language. That is an avoidable constraint. This paper shows what it would take to remove it.

✠ The Poland Argument — and Why the 2% Ceiling Must Be Broken First

There is a larger strategic logic behind the urgency of this paper — one that reaches beyond Taiwan itself.

Poland's Catholic Church survived four decades of Soviet communism not by accommodating the regime's civic language, but by being so deeply embedded in Polish national identity that it became institutionally inseparable from the Polish people. When the moment came, it was Poland — free, Catholic, and nationally rooted — that provided the platform from which Saint John Paul II helped bring the entire Soviet bloc toward liberation. The Church did not evangelize the USSR from Moscow. It evangelized it from Warsaw.

Taiwan can be Poland to China — but only if it first escapes the 2% ceiling.

The People's Republic of China today suppresses its Catholic community, controls bishop appointments through a state-registered church, and permits no free evangelization. When that changes — and historically, such seasons do change — the Church will need a free, credible, Chinese-language institutional base from which to re-enter the mainland. Taiwan is the only plausible candidate for that role. But a Taiwan Catholic Church whose civic register aligns primarily with the 2.5% of Taiwanese who identify as Chinese only cannot be Poland. A community that large audiences experience as civically foreign cannot be the platform for a national renewal movement. To be Poland, Taiwan's Church must first become genuinely, visibly, and unreservedly Taiwanese.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Poland worked because Catholicism was the faith of the Polish majority — not a remnant community associated with a foreign civic identity. Taiwan's Church, if it remains bounded by the 2% Chinese-only ceiling, is not Poland. It is, at best, a small chaplaincy to a diaspora community. The path to the Poland scenario runs directly through the identity-friction problem this paper addresses.

A fresh confirmation from January 2026. A survey conducted by Academia Sinica — Taiwan's leading national research institution — in January 2026 and presented jointly with CSIS found that 58.7% of Taiwanese adults said they would resist a Chinese invasion at all costs, even without US military assistance (41.2% "definitely willing"; 17.5% "somewhat willing"). Only 36.2% said they would not resist. This is the Poland spirit in data form: a majority that would defend its identity and freedom without waiting for outside rescue — precisely the civic soil in which a genuinely Taiwanese Catholic Church could one day plant the seeds of a mission to China. The Church's task is not to create that spirit. It is to stop alienating it.

✠ Before 1949, There Was No Ceiling — The Historical Record

The identity-friction problem is not ancient. It was created at a specific historical moment. The pre-1949 record demonstrates that without it, the Catholic mission in Taiwan had no artificial ceiling at all.

The first mission — 1626. Spanish Dominican friars arrived in northern Taiwan in 1626, coming from Manila — not from China. They worked in the languages of the Taiwanese people. Over sixteen years, approximately 4,500 to 7,000 Taiwanese were baptised — aboriginal and Hokkien Taiwanese alike — before the Spanish withdrew in 1642. No Chinese civic identity overlay. No identity ceiling. The mission was simply Catholic and Taiwanese.

The second mission — 1859 to 1948. The Dominicans returned from the Philippines in 1859, again working among Taiwanese people in southern Taiwan. By the eve of the great disruption in 1948, there were approximately 9,000 to 10,000 Catholics and fifteen clergy — a small community, but organically Taiwanese, with no structural ceiling imposed by a Chinese civic association.

Then 1949 changed everything.

With the KMT's retreat from the mainland, thousands of Chinese Catholic clergy, religious orders, and laity arrived in Taiwan — bringing with them a Church whose institutional culture, Mandarin liturgical language, and civic associations were formed on the mainland, not in Taiwan. From approximately 8,000 to 10,000 Catholics in 1948, the Church grew rapidly to 170,000 by the early 1960s — primarily among mainland Chinese refugees, not among native Taiwanese. Growth among Taiwanese themselves tapered sharply thereafter and entered a long stagnation. The diocesan leadership became primarily mainlander in makeup. Many large church buildings in Taiwan became largely empty of Taiwanese.

The 2% ceiling was not a theological datum. It was an institutional accident of 1949 — and it can be undone.

A personal note on this history. The author's family has been in Taiwan for approximately four hundred years — tracing back to the period of the first Spanish Dominican missions of 1626, when Taiwanese families first encountered the Catholic faith through missionaries who came not from China but from Manila and Rome. That original encounter had no Chinese civic register. It was a meeting between the universal Church and the Taiwanese people — exactly the encounter this paper argues must be recovered and renewed.

The strategic implication for the Holy See. The most clear-eyed missionaries who came to Taiwan after 1949 stated their purpose directly: they came to Taiwan explicitly waiting for the political climate in mainland China to change so that the mission could return. Taiwan was always understood, by those who thought most strategically, as a platform for China — not an end in itself. But a platform that is civically alienated from 62% of Taiwan's population — and that the 58.7% willing to resist China without outside help experience as speaking in the wrong national register — is a platform built on sand. The Poland logic and the Taiwan-first logic are not in tension. They are the same logic: become genuinely Taiwanese first, so that Taiwan can one day become the free base from which the Church re-enters China.

✠ The Knights of Columbus Argument — and the Curriculum Generation That Makes It Decisive

Consider a simple institutional question. The Knights of Columbus — the world's largest Catholic fraternal organization, with over two million members — has councils in Ukraine, the Philippines, South Korea, and across Latin America and Africa. It does not have a council in Taiwan.

In every country where the Knights of Columbus operates, the definition of "patriot" follows the host country's own civic identity. An American Knight is an American patriot. A Ukrainian Knight is a Ukrainian patriot — and since February 2022, that definition has been written in blood. A South Korean Knight is a Korean patriot. A Filipino Knight is a Filipino patriot. There is no ambiguity, because the Church in those countries speaks the same civic language as the people it serves.

If a Knights of Columbus council were founded in Taiwan tomorrow, what would its definition of "patriot" be?

The answer depends entirely on which civic register the Church in Taiwan uses. If the Church continues to align primarily with the 2.5% who identify as Chinese only, then a Taiwanese K of C would be, in effect, a Chinese patriot organization — invisible to 62% of the population it is supposed to serve, and structurally incapable of becoming the lay Catholic civic force that the Knights have become everywhere else they operate.

But if the Church repairs its civic register — if it speaks clearly as the Church in Taiwan, for Taiwanese people — then a Taiwanese K of C could be exactly what Poland's Catholic lay organizations were under communism: a formation school for a generation of Taiwanese Catholic patriots, ready to serve their society and, one day, their neighbors across the strait.

The Generation That Changes Everything: Taiwan's 課綱 (Curriculum) Revolution

The 2% ceiling is not permanent. It is a generational artifact — and it is being systematically dismantled by Taiwan's own education system, one graduating class at a time.

Before 1997: China-centric education for all. From the 1950s through the 1980s, every student in Taiwan studied history and geography under a curriculum described by Taiwan's own Ministry of Education as designed to "re-form Taiwanese into Chinese." Chinese history occupied the center; Taiwanese history was a minor footnote. The goal was explicit: prepare students to "retake China," with Taiwan as a temporary base. There was one textbook, written by the government, used by everyone. There was no Taiwanese history as a distinct subject.

1997: The 認識台灣 (Knowing Taiwan) revolution. In September 1997, under President Lee Teng-hui — Taiwan's first native Taiwanese president — the Ministry of Education introduced a new mandatory curriculum for all junior high school students (ages 12–15): three books titled Taiwan History, Taiwan Geography, and Taiwan Society. For the first time, Taiwanese history was taught as distinct from Chinese history. The Japanese colonial period was treated objectively. The February 28 Incident and White Terror — previously forbidden topics — were included. The curriculum explicitly embraced Taiwanese identity and nationality. The first cohort to study these books: students born in September 1984, entering junior high in 1997. In 2026, they are 41 years old.

2000s onward: deepening Taiwanization at all levels. The Chen Shui-bian administration (2000–2008) extended the curriculum reform to high schools using a "concentric circles" framework — Taiwan at the core, China as one layer among others, the world beyond. Chinese history was compressed and repositioned as part of world history, not the other way around. The Tsai Ing-wen administration's 108課綱 (2019 curriculum) continued this trajectory, reducing classical Chinese texts and deepening Taiwan-centred content. Since 2014, Taiwan has had twelve years of compulsory education, meaning every student from age 6 to 18 now passes through a curriculum that treats Taiwan's history and geography as central, not peripheral.

The causal evidence is now peer-reviewed.

A 2023 study published in the Journal of Development Economics (Wei-Lin Chen, Ming-Jen Lin, and Tzu-Ting Yang, National Taiwan University) used a regression discontinuity design to identify the causal effect of the 1997 curriculum reform. Students born just after the September 1984 cutoff — who studied the new Taiwanese-identity textbooks — were statistically significantly more likely to hold exclusive Taiwanese identity rather than dual identity. The effect was larger for academic-track students (the students most likely to attend university and become civic leaders) and larger for students in neighborhoods where Taiwanese identity had previously been lower — meaning the curriculum actively converted populations that the old system had held in a Chinese-identity register.
70% of Taiwan's 18–22 age cohort attends a higher education institution — the second highest rate in the world after South Korea. University acceptance rate has been above 90% since 2006. (Brookings Institution)
50.6% of Taiwan's total population holds a technical college or university degree — and every one of them was taught Taiwan history and geography as a mandatory subject on which university entrance examinations are tested. (Taiwan Ministry of Education)
42 and under Every Taiwanese person aged 42 or under in 2026 received their junior high education under the new Taiwanese-identity curriculum. This cohort is now the majority of Taiwan's working-age adult population.
The generational arithmetic for the 62% figure — and why it will only grow. Those who identify as "both Taiwanese and Chinese" — currently 31.7% — are disproportionately the products of the old, pre-1997 China-centric curriculum. They are getting older. As Taiwan's demography turns over, the old-curriculum cohorts naturally recede and the new-curriculum cohorts — for whom Taiwanese identity is simply the water they swim in, confirmed by twelve years of mandatory history and geography — become the dominant demographic. The NCCU trend line has been monotonically rising for thirty years for exactly this reason. The 2% Chinese-only ceiling is not a stable equilibrium. It is a residual of a political project that Taiwan's own education system has been systematically unwinding since 1997.
The strategic implication for the Church and for K of C.

A Knights of Columbus council founded in Taiwan in 2026 would be recruiting primarily from the new-curriculum generation — people for whom being Taiwanese is as natural and uncontroversial as being American is for a Knight in Chicago or being Filipino is for a Knight in Manila. The Church's current civic register speaks to the 2.5% Chinese-only cohort and partially to the 31.7% dual-identity cohort — a pool that is aging and diminishing. A Church that repairs its civic register now will be positioned to serve — and to form — the majority that is already here, and that will only grow larger. That majority is the civic soil in which a Taiwanese K of C, a Taiwanese Poland, and one day a free Taiwanese Catholic platform for the China mission can take root.

The window is open. The generation is already formed. The Church's task is simply to speak their language.

Three Converging Pressures

The paper's identity-friction model — where "identity friction" means the reduction in trust and receptivity that occurs when a community perceives the Church's public language as misaligned with their civic self-understanding — rests on three documented facts that compound one another:

62% identify as Taiwanese only — a share rising steadily for thirty years (National Chengchi University, Dec. 2025)
−57% projected decline in Taiwan's working-age population by 2070 — shrinking the pool for catechumens (those preparing for baptism), clergy, and volunteers (Taiwan government projection)
~220,000 baptised Catholics in Taiwan — small in number but diplomatically singular: the Holy See is Taiwan's only formal diplomatic partner in Europe
Why model to 2106? A seminary built today trains priests who serve into the 2060s. Demographic trends established today shape the parish network of 2080. The long horizon is the normal planning window for an institution of the Church's age and permanence — not speculation.

What the Paper Contributes

  • A three-way conceptual distinction between the Holy See's legal position on Taiwan's sovereignty, its diplomatic terminology (e.g., "China (Taiwan)"), and how that terminology is actually heard by Taiwanese Catholics — three things that must be kept separate for sound institutional reasoning.
  • Explicit crisis criteria — three measurable tests (mission reach, public trust, and institutional planning stability) that replace vague alarm with specific thresholds.
  • A quantitative scenario model projecting baptisms and total Catholic population under five strategic paths, calibrated to Taiwan's real demographic trajectory across four time horizons.
  • A complete, non-overlapping solution architecture covering what the Church should say locally, build institutionally, adjust diplomatically, plan for contingently, and measure over time.
  • A threshold instrument answering the decisive question: how large would gains among Catholics in mainland China need to be, and how durably, to justify the quantified costs to Taiwan? The paper gives that number. Whether to act on it requires non-public information only the Holy See possesses — and the author defers entirely to that judgment.

The Paper's Conclusion

Protect Taiwan now, while keeping the China channel open.

Repair local language. Reinforce Taiwan's Catholic institutions. Clarify that diplomatic labels do not define civic identity. Preserve pastoral engagement with China. Measure whether it is working.

The Church cannot resolve the military and political pressures shaping cross-strait relations. It can resolve its own communicative misalignment with the sixty-two per cent of Taiwanese who identify as Taiwanese — because that misalignment is avoidable in a way that geopolitical coercion is not.

A note on this paper's spirit.

The motto of the Order of Saint John — Tuitio fidei et obsequium pauperum, meaning "Defence of the faith and service to the poor and the sick" — has guided the Order's discernment since Blessed Gerard first opened his hospital in Jerusalem. In that tradition, service to the poor begins with knowing who they are, where they stand, and what language they speak. Asking honestly whether the Church's public language is preparing the way or placing an obstacle before those who might draw near is, at its root, a work of that same discernment.

May Our Lady, who said yes to God on behalf of all humanity, intercede for the Church's mission in Taiwan. May Saint John the Baptist, who spent his life making straight the paths, guide all who read these pages toward whatever action best serves that end. And may Blessed Gerard remind us that the first act of hospitality is to welcome the guest as they are — not as we would prefer them to be.

Logarchéon AI Manifesto

Logarchéon treats advanced models as an outer cortex for human beings, not a forbidden ghostwriter. The core stance is ethical, not cosmetic: replace hazing with stewardship and make outer cortex access a matter of justice, not privilege.

Hazing says: “I did it, so you must.”
Logarchéon chooses stewardship: “I did it, so you don’t have to.”

If tools can lift truck drivers, migrants, the poor and the sick to think and speak on equal footing with elites, then withholding those tools is not virtue; it is gatekeeping. The guiding line is tuitio fidei et obsequium pauperum — defence of the faith and service to the poor and the sick — now extended into the domain of AI and outer cortex.

The doctrine of Imago Dei teaches that every person is made in the image of God and therefore possesses inherent dignity. Tools that help someone exercise their mind and voice honor that dignity. Think of Pentecost: at the first Pentecost, the Holy Spirit enabled the apostles to speak so that “we all hear these people speaking in our own languages about the wonderful things God has done.” The message is that God’s truth transcends barriers of speech and culture; it is for everyone, not only for the educated elite. In the same spirit, if a talented person cannot easily express themselves in polished English, why should they be treated as less intelligent, suspected as a fraud, or quietly excluded from serious conversation and public respect? Providing such a person with an outer cortex that lets them think and speak clearly is not cheating; it is an act of justice toward the image of God in them.

Furthermore, the real “joy of writing” often comes from having something worth saying, not from the rote mechanics of typing every character by hand. Using AI to overcome blank-page anxiety or language barriers does not deprive someone of joy; it can unlock that joy for people who would otherwise be shut out. In modern life most professionals already rely on tools: architects use CAD, scientists use software, writers use editors. We do not say they learned less because they did not draw blueprints or typeset pages with a pen. In the same way, an underdog using AI to check his English or clarify an argument is still engaging intellectually; he is simply using a new kind of “ink” in service of his own judgment and conscience.

Moreover, the output of a large language model can be guided through retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). In a RAG setup, the model is first given a set of documents retrieved from a library and only then asked to answer; its responses are grounded in that curated corpus rather than in random internet scraps. In practice this reduces hallucinations by constantly supplying the model with up-to-date or domain-specific sources. For a small parish, lodge, council, assembly, clinic, school, or underfunded organization, this means you can index digitized minutes, approved historical texts, and leaders’ writings inside a local LLM; any answer the system gives can then be traced back to those authoritative documents, honoring both truth and confidentiality while letting “underdogs” work with the same depth of reference that once belonged only to well-resourced elites.

What this means in practice

  • Outer cortex as a right, not a scandal. Using AI to write, reason, and translate is normal wherever there is no explicit “no-tools” contract (exams, sworn statements, sacraments). Tools are not cheating; dishonesty is.
  • Local, sovereign stacks. Whenever possible, models run locally or in λ-secure deployments under the user’s keys, with logs and authorship metadata so human judgment remains visible and accountable.
  • Bias toward the bottom of the pyramid. Design choices are evaluated against a simple test: “Does this materially empower workers, migrants, the poor and the sick, or only those already on top?”

Supplement: First-Level Symmetry and Exact Hausdorff Dimension

This supplementary note highlights a key insight: if the initial generators of a Schottky group exhibit complete first-level symmetry—that is, the magnitudes of their derivatives at a common base point \( z_0 \) satisfy \( |T_i'(z_0)| = \text{const} \)—then the entire Hausdorff dimension of the limit set can be determined using only this first-level data.

Specifically, under these conditions, the zero-pressure equation \[ \sum_{|T_i| = 1} |T_i'(z_0)|^{-\delta} = 1 \] yields an exact solution for the Hausdorff dimension \(\dim_H(\Lambda_\Gamma) = \delta\), without requiring data from deeper iterates.

Even when perfect symmetry breaks at higher levels, as long as bounded distortion holds, the contribution of higher iterates remains controlled. The result is robust: full symmetry at the first level ensures the validity of the explicit formula throughout the group’s dynamical hierarchy.

This observation strengthens the theoretical justification for using well-distributed Schottky generators to derive explicit, closed-form dimension formulas.

This work provides a novel and explicit closed‐form formula for computing the Hausdorff dimension of limit sets associated with Schottky groups that are well‐distributed—that is, those with uniformly arranged generators. In this framework, the Hausdorff dimension is given by $$\dim \mathcal{H}(\Lambda_\Gamma) = \frac{\ln(2m - 1)}{r_{\mathrm{eff}}},$$ where m is the number of free generators and reff is the effective translation length determined via a rigorous two‐step displacement method.

Always-Required General Conditions (For Every Method Below)

To gain a plenary indulgence in any of the cases listed, you must:

  • be a baptized Catholic, not excommunicated, and in the state of grace at completion;
  • have at least a general intention to gain the indulgence;
  • go to sacramental confession within about 20 days (before or after);
  • receive Holy Communion (preferably on the same day as the work);
  • pray at least an Our Father and a Hail Mary for the intentions of the Pope;
  • be completely detached from all sin, even venial, and count at most one plenary indulgence per day.

A. Any-Day Works (Available All Year)

  1. 30 Minutes of Eucharistic Adoration — Spend at least 30 continuous minutes in true adoration before the Blessed Sacrament (exposed or in the tabernacle), then fulfil the general conditions.
  2. 30 Minutes of Scripture Reading — Read Sacred Scripture prayerfully for at least 30 continuous minutes, asking the Holy Spirit for light, then fulfil the general conditions.
  3. Way of the Cross — Devoutly make all 14 Stations of the Cross at legitimately erected stations (or the form allowed if impeded), then fulfil the general conditions.
  4. Group Five-Decade Rosary — Pray one uninterrupted five-decade Rosary in a church, family, or group setting with meditation on the mysteries, then fulfil the general conditions.

B. Fixed Calendar Days Each Year

  1. Divine Mercy Sunday — On the Second Sunday of Easter, devoutly take part in Divine Mercy devotions or pray before the Blessed Sacrament with trust in Jesus, receive Communion, and fulfil the general conditions.
  2. Portiuncula (Pardon of Assisi) — Between noon on August 1 and midnight on August 2, visit any Catholic church, pray the Creed and an Our Father there for this indulgence, and fulfil the general conditions.
  3. All Souls' Day Church Visit (2 November) — On November 2, visit a church, pray for the dead (e.g. Our Father, Creed, and a prayer such as “Eternal rest...” ), and fulfil the general conditions, applying the indulgence only to the faithful departed.
  4. November 1–8 Cemetery Visits — On each day from November 1 to 8, visit a cemetery or columbarium, pray devoutly for the faithful departed, and fulfil the general conditions, applying each day's indulgence only to the dead.

  1. Jubilee Pilgrimage to a Designated Church or Shrine — During the Jubilee period (to 6 January 2026), go as a pilgrim to a designated Jubilee church or shrine, participate there in Mass, the Liturgy of the Hours, Way of the Cross, Rosary, or at least recite the Creed and an Our Father with a Marian/patronal prayer, then fulfil the general conditions (for yourself or a deceased person).

Algorithm: How to Aim at One Plenary Indulgence Each Day

Basic Idea

You want a simple rule:

Every day: do one plenary-indulgenced work (A or B or Jubilee),
while keeping the general conditions in place (confession window, Communion, prayer for the Pope, detachment).

This algorithm does not guarantee the plenary effect (God alone judges, especially detachment), but it shows a logical plan that is fully in harmony with the Church's norms.

Step 0: Standing Assumptions

At all times, you intend to:

  • remain a practicing Catholic (baptized, not excommunicated);
  • avoid mortal sin, and if you fall, go to confession as soon as possible;
  • accept all indulgences the Church wishes to give you.

You can renew a general intention from time to time, e.g.:

“Lord, I accept all the indulgences you wish to give me through your Church, for myself or for the faithful departed.”

Step 1: Confession Rhythm (Every ≈ 2–3 Weeks)

  1. Choose a stable rhythm, e.g. confession every 2 weeks (or at least every 3 weeks), and put it on your calendar.
  2. When you confess, intend that this confession will support any plenary indulgences you seek to gain in the days around it (about 20 days before/after each indulgenced work).
  3. If you fall into mortal sin, reset the plan: go back to confession before counting on plenary indulgences.

Step 2: Daily Morning Check

Each morning:

  1. Check state of grace (internally):
    • If you are aware of mortal sin, plan to go to confession as soon as possible; until then, you cannot gain a plenary indulgence.
  2. Renew your intention:
    • “Lord, if it pleases you, may I gain a plenary indulgence today (for myself / for N. deceased).”
  3. Plan to receive Holy Communion:
    • normally at daily Mass;
    • if you cannot attend Mass that day, you cannot gain a plenary indulgence that day.

Step 3: Choose Today’s Indulgenced Work

For each calendar day, follow this logic:

  1. If today is a special calendar day (Block B):
    • Divine Mercy Sunday,
    • Portiuncula (Aug 1 noon to Aug 2 midnight),
    • All Souls' Day (Nov 2) church visit for the dead,
    • November 1–8 cemetery visits,

    then:

    • perform the appropriate special work (B.1–B.4) once;
    • intend to gain today's plenary indulgence through that special work.
  2. Else, if you plan a Jubilee pilgrimage that day (Block C):
    • go as a pilgrim to a designated Jubilee church or shrine;
    • there, take part in Mass, Liturgy of the Hours, Way of the Cross, Rosary, or at least the Creed + Our Father + Marian/patronal prayer;
    • intend to gain today's plenary indulgence through this Jubilee work.
  3. Else (ordinary day): choose one All-Year work (Block A):
    • Option A.1 — 30 minutes of Eucharistic adoration; or
    • Option A.2 — 30 minutes of Scripture reading; or
    • Option A.3 — the Way of the Cross; or
    • Option A.4 — group five-decade Rosary.

    Perform one of these devoutly, intending to gain today's plenary indulgence through it.

Step 4: Complete the General Conditions That Day

On the same day as the chosen work (as far as possible):

  1. Holy Communion: receive Communion in the state of grace, offering it in union with the indulgenced work.
  2. Prayer for the Pope: pray at least one Our Father and one Hail Mary for the intentions of the Holy Father.
  3. Detachment from sin: make a sincere act of renunciation:
    “Lord, I reject every sin, even venial, and I do not want to cling to any attachment that displeases you.”

Note: You may do several indulgenced works in one day, but you can only gain one plenary indulgence per day; the others are partial.

Step 5: End-of-Day Trust

At the end of the day, you can simply say:

“Lord, if I have fulfilled the conditions as your Church requires,
please grant the plenary indulgence I have sought (for myself / for N. deceased);
if not, I accept whatever partial indulgence and graces you wish to give.”

You thus:

  • live in daily trust rather than anxiety;
  • aim reasonably at one plenary indulgence per day;
  • leave the exact measure of the grace to God.

This rule of life outlines a daily and weekly rhythm of prayer for a lay person inspired by Ignatian spirituality (especially the Examen and Suscipe) and the monastic balance of the Cistercian tradition. Times are recommendations to aim for, not rigid laws: adapt them to your real responsibilities and state of life.

Times are recommendations; adjust to your real obligations while keeping the basic structure.

  • Morning (2–5 min): Sign of the Cross; Prayer for Generosity; Daily Prayer of the Order of Malta.

  • Evening (10 min):
    • Short Examen: “Thank You for …”, “I am sorry for …”, “Help me tomorrow with …”
    • Suscipe once, slowly and attentively.
  • One Floating Devotion (choose one): Rosary or 30 minutes of Bible reading or one Hour of the Office (Lauds or Vespers).

  • Day of Confession:
    • Before entering: short prayer for humility and trust in God's mercy.
    • After Confession: complete the assigned penance promptly and spend a few minutes in thanksgiving (psalm or spontaneous prayer).
    • If combined with Mass, follow with the Anima Christi after receiving Communion.
