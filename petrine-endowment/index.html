The Petrine Endowment: How the Vatican Library and Archive Can Pay the Holy See's Deficit in Perpetuity — a Euclidean Plan for Salaries, Pensions, and the 25-Year Bridge

A demonstration, in the order of definitions, postulates, and propositions, of why the Holy See's deficit is structural; an evidence-graded valuation of what the publishable layer of the Vatican Library and the 85 kilometres of the Apostolic Archive could earn without alienating a single object; a proof that this income, saved rather than spent for about 25 years, pays the structural deficit every year in perpetuity; and a three-vault endowment design, with a bridge for the first 25 years, meant to keep paying employees and pensioners through inflation, depression, war, sanctions, market collapse, and a second pandemic.

Abstract

The Holy See reported a consolidated operating deficit of €44.4 million for 2024, down from €83.5 million in 2023, and reached a small surplus only through investment returns, asset sales, and a rise in donations. Its pension fund carries an unfunded liability that has never been published and is reported between €0.35 and €1.5 billion. This page argues four things. First, the deficit follows from the form of the Holy See's revenue and cost functions, so no cost discipline closes it. Second, the Library and Archive hold a large informational layer — on this page's estimate, roughly 125–590 billion tokens of publishable text, much of it never transcribed — that can be licensed without alienation, while every individual scholar keeps free reading access. Third, the net income from licensing that layer has a present value of about €0.05–2.9 billion, most likely about €0.6 billion — a price on a new service, not on the collections, which are inalienable and lie beyond market valuation. That income is necessary, but not sufficient if it is spent as it arrives. The earlier version of this plan, which rested the endowment on corpus licensing, is corrected here. Saved instead of spent, the same income becomes perpetual: about €40 million a year placed in a permanent fund for 25 years, earning 4 percent a year after inflation, grows to about €1.67 billion; spending 3.5 percent of it pays about €58 million a year for ever — roughly the likely structural deficit of €60 million — whether subscriptions rise or fall, and through the next pandemic (Proposition 6). The price is a 25-year wait, bridged by operating gains, donations given as permanent capital, and a spent share of the archive income; the pension gap still needs other money. Fourth, what survives every economic regime is an order of vaults — a liquidity vault of 24–36 months of payroll, an unlevered pension-matching vault, and a perpetual vault spent at 3.5 percent real — capitalized from six uncorrelated sources over 25 years. The base case requires about €3.1 billion and raises about €3.35 billion. Every figure carries a grade; every model input is exposed so that it can be replaced. Every step is also warranted from Scripture, Tradition, the Catechism, the Code of Canon Law, and papal acts (§01A); the warrants support principles, never numbers, and where the tradition cuts against the plan, §18A says so.

Core Finding: What the Archive Can Do, and What It Cannot

In brief

The Holy See spends more each year than its recurring income, for reasons built into how it earns and spends. Its libraries and archives can earn real money from their digital copies without selling anything — but not enough to fix the problem alone. The durable answer is a permanent endowment in three separate compartments, filled from several sources that do not all fail at the same time.

F1 · THE GAP

Structural, not administrative

Operating deficit €44.4M in 2024 (€83.5M in 2023); the year closed €1.6M positive only after €46M of investment returns, one-off asset sales, and higher donations.[1][4]

Grade A
F2 · THE HIDDEN LIABILITY

An unpublished pension gap

No official figure exists. Reported estimates run from about €0.35B to at least €1.5B, for roughly 5,000 employees and retirees.[5][6][8]

Grade C
F3 · THE OPEN LAYER

A service, not a sale

About 125–590B tokens of publishable text across Library manuscripts, the open Archive, and unique printed matter. Present value of the licensing income about €0.05–2.9B, most likely €0.6B. The collections themselves are not priced.

Grade D (model)
F4 · THE STRUCTURE

Three vaults, six sources

Liquidity (24–36 months of payroll), pension-matching (unlevered), perpetual (3.5% real draw). Need €1.7–4.4B, base €3.1B; base-case sources about €3.35B over 25 years.

Grade D (design)
The defensible model
The informational layer of the patrimony is a real and growing asset, and it should be licensed — non-exclusively, term-limited, and never at the cost of free reading. But survival through every economic regime comes from structure, not from any single revenue stream: a consolidated treasury, a measured pension liability, and an order of vaults that absorbs shocks in sequence.
The archive route to perpetuity · Proposition 6
About €40 million a year saved in a permanent fund for 25 years, at 4 percent after inflation, grows to about €1.67 billion. Spending 3.5 percent of it pays about €58 million a year for ever — roughly the likely €60 million structural deficit — whatever subscriptions do. Two costs: a 25-year wait, during which the deficit is still carried as it is today while the bridge of §13A takes over; and the pension gap, which the archive does not close.
A correction to the earlier draft. The first statement of this plan made licensing of the corpus the principal source of the endowment. The valuation in §09 does not support that. The corpus is one of six sources, and the plan is built so that it still closes if AI-data prices collapse (§14, §18).

Sources, Evidence Grades, and Scope

In brief

Some numbers here are official, some are journalism, and many are this page's own estimates. Each carries a grade so the reader can see which is which, and every estimate shows its inputs so a better number can be substituted.

Grade A
Official publication of the Holy See or of the institution itself (Secretariat for the Economy, Press Office, the Library, the Archive, the Church Commissioners).
Grade B
Reputable press or scholarly reporting of official figures; peer-reviewed or institutional technical reports.
Grade C
Single-source reporting, leaked internal estimates, advocacy outlets, or figures not yet matched to an official document.
Grade D
This page's model assumption or estimate: stated, bounded, and replaceable.

Scope. The subject is the Holy See (D1), not Vatican City State, dioceses, religious orders, or Catholic universities, whose assets the Holy See does not own. The Vatican Museums belong to the Governorate of Vatican City State and pass their profits upward; they appear here only as a revenue line and a shock channel.

What is excluded. Nothing on this page values or proposes to release material that is closed: documents after the present opening boundary (the end of the pontificate of Pius XII, October 1958),[19] anything under the pontifical secret or the sacramental seal, diplomatic material still sensitive, and personal data of living persons. That material is not estimated at all; it is left out of this plan entirely by postulate (P3).

Standing. This is an outside analytic proposal, written from public sources. It is not advice to any Vatican body, and it makes no claim about what any official intends.

Proposition 1: The Deficit Is Structural

The identityDeficit = (Mission cost + Pension accrual) − (Donations + Investment yield + Commercial receipts + Diocesan contributions)
Proposition 1

Every term of the identity moves against solvency for reasons internal to the institution's form; therefore the deficit is structural (D8).

Demonstration

Mission cost does not fall to fit income, by P1: a universal Church cannot keep a partial presence. It rises with inflation and with the maintenance of historic property, which the 2024 statement names as a cause of higher costs.[4]

Donations are the largest discretionary term and the least reliable. Giving capacity sits disproportionately in the North, where affiliation and institutional trust have declined; membership growth sits in the South, where capacity is lower. In 2024 the Peter's Pence rise came largely from private donors and from one country's jump, while diocesan giving was flat.[10] A term that moves on trust and geography cannot be steered by administration.

Investment yield is bounded by a small corpus and by P4. It was decisive in 2024 precisely because the operating result was negative (CN4: part of it was one-off).

Commercial receipts are bounded by physical throughput, and that throughput wears the asset: footfall, light, and humidity degrade what is shown. In 2020 museum visits fell by about 82 percent in one year.[12]

Diocesan contributions under canon 1271 are requests, not assessments; the Holy See has no power of levy.

Pension accrual rises with longevity while the contributing base does not grow (§06).

Each term is determined by the institution's form, not by the quality of its administration; so the sum is too. ∎

Corollary 1.1. No cost discipline, however strict, closes a gap whose cause is the shape of the revenue function. This is why a decade of reform reduced waste and improved transparency without reaching solvency.

Corollary 1.2. There are only two exits: make assessment real, or endow the function permanently. The first pushes cost onto dioceses that are themselves strained and would contradict canon 1271's voluntary character. The second is the subject of Part III.

The three gates

  1. Temporal gate. Only material within the opened pontificates — currently through October 1958 — passes. When a new pontificate opens by papal decision, its material becomes eligible; the plan never anticipates that decision.
  2. Categorical gate. Even within opened periods, anything under the seal, the pontifical secret, or still-sensitive diplomatic confidence is excluded, as are any categories the Archive itself withholds.
  3. Personal gate. Personal data of people who may still be living is excluded or redacted.

Each gate has a machine pre-screen and a mandatory human archivist decision; a document passes only when all three are passed and signed (§15). A single leak of closed material would defeat the plan on principle, whatever its economics (§18).

Market Comparables: What Text Is Worth

In brief

Prices for text used to train AI range over roughly four orders of magnitude. Copyrighted trade books sit at the top; old printed public-domain books command almost no price, because a leading university released a quarter-trillion tokens of them openly. The Vatican's value lies in the gap: text that exists nowhere else in machine-readable form, with an unbroken record of where it came from.

What follows. The Vatican cannot sell copyright in a twelfth-century register; there is none to sell. What it can offer is access and labour: high-resolution images not otherwise available, transcriptions that did not exist before, catalogue structure, and certified provenance. The Harvard team itself argues that the scarcity of well-documented data with clear provenance chains is the pressing need in AI training.[25] This page therefore prices the Vatican corpus at €10–75 per million tokens per licence term: above the bulk floor because it is unique and verified, far below the copyright ceiling because it is not copyright. Proposition 3: The Open Layer Is Necessary but Not Sufficient

Proposition 3

The informational layer yields permanent income without alienation, and that income is uncorrelated with donations; but the present value of its licensing income, about €0.05–2.9B with a base of about €0.6B, is less than the capital the Holy See requires, roughly €1.7–4.4B with a base of about €3.1B (§11). It is therefore one necessary source among several, not the sufficient one.

Demonstration

Without alienation: the value of a codex lies in its content, provenance, and authenticated custody. Separating the physical object from its informational layer (D5) lets the second be licensed (D7) while the first moves nowhere and is handled less. P2 holds.

Uncorrelated: licence and subscription revenue depends on research budgets and technology markets, not on the geography of Catholic giving or on trust in Church administration. It therefore satisfies the requirement, stated in the earlier draft as N5, for a revenue source independent of donation trends.

Not sufficient: by §09 the base-case value is €0.58B; by §11 the base-case requirement is €3.1B. The ratio is under one-fifth. Even the high case (€2.9B) falls short of the high requirement (€4.4B). ∎

Proposition 6

If the likely net archive income of about €40 million a year is placed in the perpetual vault for 25 years and earns 4 percent a year after inflation, the vault then pays about €58 million a year in perpetuity under the 3.5 percent rule, which covers the likely structural deficit independently of subscriptions and donations. Demonstration

The future value of a level annual saving A at real rate r over n years is A[(1 + r)n − 1]/r; with A = €40M, r = 0.04, n = 25 this is ≈ €1.67B. Under the spending rule of §12, the draw is 3.5 percent of the vault, ≈ €58M, which lies within 4 percent of D = €60M. Since the draw is set below the expected real return, the principal is preserved in real terms (CN3), so the payment recurs every year without limit. The draw depends on the vault's value, not on the year's sales, so a fall in subscriptions or a closure like 2020 does not stop it; vault I absorbs the fall in market value during a crash (Proposition 4). The pension gap, P ≥ €0.35B, is a separate liability that this vault is not drawn to meet. ∎

The two costs

  1. The 25-year wait. The same €40M cannot be both spent and saved, so until the vault is large enough the deficit is carried as it is today — by donations and one-off sales — and, increasingly, by the bridge below. Because the platform ramps up over years 3 to 7 (§13), 25 years of mature income ends near year 30 from launch.
  2. The pension gap still needs other money: the founding campaign, bequests, the Vatican Bank dividend, and property (§13).

A cathedral, not a purchase

The Church has built this way before. The foundation stone of the new St Peter's was laid in 1506, and the basilica was consecrated in 1626; Cologne Cathedral, begun in 1248, was completed in 1880. Most of those who laid the stones never saw the roof. Yet Cologne's choir was consecrated in 1322 and served for worship for more than five centuries while the nave stood unfinished — as the bridge of this section pays from the first year while the perpetual vault is still rising. The endowment is built for those who come after: "one sows and another reaps" (Jn 4:37–38); "these all died in faith, not having received what was promised, but having seen it and greeted it from afar" (Heb 11:13).

The record also carries a warning. St Peter's was financed in part by the preaching of indulgences, and the St Peter's indulgence preached in 1517 became an occasion of the Reformation. How the money is raised matters as much as what it builds. Hence this plan's rules: gifts are free (2 Cor 9:7), no spiritual good is sold (CCC 2121),[33] and every account is published (can. 1287 §2).[32]

Proposition 5

A unitized Consolidated Investment Pool satisfies N2 while each entity keeps the administration of its own units; it therefore agrees both with Il diritto nativo, under which ownership already rests with the Holy See, and with Coniuncta cura, under which responsibility is shared under one committee.

Demonstration

By Il diritto nativo, ownership already rests with the Holy See: "no institution or entity can claim private and exclusive ownership or title to the goods of the Holy See."[34] Let each entity — a dicastery, the Peter's Pence reserve, APSA, a pontifical fund — hold units in a single pool, as participants hold units in a common investment fund, and keep the administration of its units, its own accounts, and the right to redeem them for its institutional purposes. Nothing is centralized that is not already the Holy See's. Yet the pool has one mandate, one custodian panel, one risk budget, and one audited net asset value, which is what N2 requires, because the failures N2 addresses came from invisible parallel portfolios, not from dispersed administration as such. Coniuncta cura's rule that investment follow the Investment Committee and the Investment Policy — through the IOR, or through intermediaries abroad where the competent bodies judge that more efficient — is already the pool's mandate in law.[35] ∎

Governance of the vaults

  • An investment committee with a majority of independent professional members, fixed terms, published conflict-of-interest declarations, and no single person holding discretion over a transaction above a set threshold.
  • A licensing board, separate from the investment committee, that approves every licence against P2–P5; licences are non-exclusive (CN5), time-limited, output-capped, and revocable for breach.
  • A statute of perpetuity: vault III principal may be spent only by a formal act of the Pope after a published opinion of the committee — the endowment equivalent of a constitutional amendment.

Pension reform consistent with P6

  1. Commission and publish the actuarial valuation (N3).
  2. Guarantee every accrued benefit through vault II.
  3. For future service, move to a hybrid: a modest defined-benefit floor plus a defined-contribution account. The Church of England made a comparable division in 1998, leaving the Church Commissioners responsible for pensions accrued before that date.[30]
  4. Index wages and pensions by a published rule, not by annual discretion, so that the burden of balancing is not carried silently by employees.

Objections from Scripture and Tradition

In brief

The strongest arguments against the plan come from inside the tradition, not outside it. They are set out here in the form of the Summa: objection, the contrary authority where there is one, and reply. One of them (Ambrose) is conceded in part.

Objection 1

"Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth"

ObjectionChrist forbids storing treasure (Mt 6:19) and condemns the rich man who built bigger barns (Lk 12:16–21). An endowment is a barn.
On the contraryJoseph stored a fifth of the harvest as "a reserve for the land against the seven years of famine" (Gen 41:34–36), and Egypt lived.
ReplyThe fool is condemned as one "who lays up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God" (Lk 12:21). The fault is the owner's purpose, not the barn. The endowment is held for workers, retirees, and the poor (CCC 2404)[33], is spent by rule, and returns any surplus to charity.
Objection 2

St Ambrose: the Church's gold is for spending

Objection"The Church has gold, not to store up, but to lay out, and to spend on those who need." Ambrose broke up sacred vessels to ransom captives, and St Lawrence showed the poor as "the treasures of the Church" (De officiis II.28).[58] P2 contradicts him.
ReplyAgreed, and canon law agrees: alienation is lawful for "urgent necessity … charity" (can. 1293 §1).[32] P2 is prudential, not doctrinal. It holds only while licensing the informational layer meets the need without destroying what cannot be replaced. If captives must be ransomed, Ambrose governs, and P2 yields.
Objection 3

"Give without pay"

Objection"You received without pay, give without pay" (Mt 10:8). Licensing the Church's learning sells what was freely received.
On the contrary"The laborer deserves his wages" (1 Tim 5:18; Lk 10:7).
ReplyReading stays free (P5). What is charged for is new labour — imaging, transcription, search, service — and commercial use. Simony concerns spiritual goods (CCC 2121)[33]; no sacrament and no spiritual good is licensed.
Objection 4

Interest and usury

ObjectionScripture forbids interest (Ex 22:25; Lev 25:36–37), and Benedict XIV's Vix pervenit (1745) condemned gain from a loan as such.[57] An endowment lives on returns.
ReplyAquinas holds that one who entrusts money to a merchant in a kind of partnership "does not transfer the ownership" and may lawfully take a share of the profit (ST II-II q.78 a.2 ad 5).[61] Vix pervenit itself says it does "not deny that … certain other titles … may run parallel" with a loan, giving "entirely just and legitimate reasons" for return (§III).[57] Present law requires administrators to invest (can. 1284 §2 6°; can. 1305)[32], and P4's ethical screen applies.
Objection 5

"Do not be anxious about tomorrow"

ObjectionChrist says, "Do not be anxious about tomorrow" (Mt 6:34). Planning for a pandemic shows a lack of trust in Providence.
On the contraryThe same Lord tells the builder to "sit down and count the cost" before he begins (Lk 14:28).
ReplyAquinas teaches that solicitude for the future is wrong only when it is excessive, untimely, or put before God (ST II-II q.55 a.7).[61] A reserve fixed by rule removes anxiety rather than feeding it.
Objection 6

Canon 1271 names the dioceses as the lawful source

ObjectionThe Code provides that bishops assist the Apostolic See (can. 1271). An endowment bypasses that bond of communion.
ReplyCan. 1271 asks "according to the resources of their dioceses," in "the bond of unity and charity."[32] The endowment keeps the bond and lightens the load on poor dioceses: "not that others should be eased and you burdened, but … as a matter of equality" (2 Cor 8:13).
Objection 7

Licensing feeds concentrated technological power

ObjectionAntiqua et nova warns against technocratic control by large companies.[41] Licensing the corpus to them cooperates with that power.
ReplyLicences are non-exclusive (CN5), term-limited, output-capped, revocable, and reviewed against P4 by a board separate from the investment committee. Any cooperation with a licensee's misuse is material and remote, and the board can end it; exclusivity, which would build a single firm's moat, is forbidden.

Conclusion

The Holy See was impoverished in 1870, recapitalized in 1929, and has been in structural deficit for decades. Its 2024 accounts show discipline and transparency that earlier decades lacked, but they also show that the operating gap remains and that the year was balanced by donations and one-off items. The pension liability, the largest single number, is still unmeasured in public.

The Library and the Archive can help. For the first time, machines can read a millennium of handwriting at scale, and the resulting corpus — unique, multilingual, and backed by an unbroken record of custody — can be licensed without moving or selling anything, while every person keeps the right to read it free. On this page's model, the income from licensing it has a present value of about €0.05 billion to €2.9 billion, most likely around €0.6 billion — a measure of one new service, not of the collections, which no price describes.

That is not enough to carry the Church's central government alone, and a plan that pretended otherwise would fail in the first decade of falling AI-data prices. What survives every regime is structure: one set of books, one investment mandate owned in units, a measured pension, and three vaults — liquidity, pension, perpetual — filled from six sources that do not fail together. In the base case the need is about €3.1 billion and the sources reach about €3.35 billion in 25 years. And the archive, saved rather than spent for those 25 years, pays the structural deficit on its own thereafter, for ever (Proposition 6). Like the cathedrals, it is built by one generation for the next. The originality claimed here is in the combination: machine-read corpus valuation under canonical gates, informational licensing kept apart from alienation and from free reading, and capitalization into a regime-tested order of vaults — not in any single element.

Summary
The archive pays a stream; the structure pays the salaries. Measure the pension, consolidate the mandate, keep reading free, license without exclusivity, and let three vaults absorb what no single source can.
